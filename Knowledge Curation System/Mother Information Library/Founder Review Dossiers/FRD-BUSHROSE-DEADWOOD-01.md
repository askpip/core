### 3. Research Plan

Research questions used to structure the investigation:

1. What visual, external indicators (bark colour, texture) distinguish dead from living wood on a dormant bush rose?
2. What internal indicator (pith colour/condition when the stem is cut) distinguishes dead from living wood, and how reliable is it treated as being across sources?
3. What role do dormant buds/eyes play as an indicator?
4. What role, if any, does stem flexibility or brittleness play?
5. What ambiguous or lookalike conditions could cause a beginner to misjudge dead vs. living wood?
6. What safety-relevant cautions apply to physically checking this (tool use, thorny-stem handling)?
7. What can a single photograph establish about this distinction, and what does it require physically checking the plant to establish?

Anticipated source types: university/government cooperative extension horticulture services; a national gardening authority (RHS); a national rose-growing authority (American Rose Society); public-health sources for the safety question. Anticipated challenge, confirmed during research: extension sources are practitioner guidance, not peer-reviewed primary literature, and vary in how much of the commission's specific sub-questions (especially stem flexibility) each one addresses.

Each of the seven research questions above maps to one or more of the Assessed Findings in §7–§8: Questions 1–4 map to AF-1 through AF-4 respectively; Question 5 maps to AF-5; Question 6 maps to AF-6; Question 7 maps to the interpretation discussed at §7.5 and §8.7.

### 5. Evidence Evaluation

Evaluated against the Evidence Assessment Factors (EAS Chapter 4: Relevance, Source Reliability, Consistency, Independence, Limitations, Uncertainty), per Assessed Finding.

**AF-1 — Pith colour.** *Relevance:* direct — every source addresses the exact diagnostic question. *Source reliability:* very high — seven distinct US land-grant/UK national horticultural authorities, several with named professional authors (Russ & Polomski at Clemson; Watson at UGA; McMullen at OSU; Steil at Iowa State). *Consistency:* strong — all seven converge on white/pale-green pith = living, brown/gray/black = dead, each in independent wording ("white and plump," "creamy white," "white or pale green," "white pith core"). *Independence:* genuine — different institutions, states, authors and site infrastructures; distinct phrasing indicates shared professional consensus rather than one source copied repeatedly (EAS §4.5). *Limitations/uncertainty:* minimal; one unresolved internal RHS discrepancy noted at Source 1 does not bear on the pith-colour claim itself.

**AF-2 — External bark/stem colour.** *Relevance:* direct, but explicitly secondary to the pith test in several sources. *Source reliability:* high (Illinois Extension, Iowa State, American Rose Society, RHS). *Consistency:* reasonable agreement that green = living, brown/gray/black/shriveled = dead, but PlantTalk Colorado (Source 3) documents a specific, sourced exception: live wood on older canes or bronze-stemmed cultivars can appear brown. *Independence:* the 4–5 corroborating/caveat sources are independent of one another. *Limitations:* the documented false-negative risk is the material limitation driving this below AF-1.

**AF-3 — Dormant bud presence.** *Relevance:* direct (Clemson, Iowa State, American Rose Society). *Source reliability:* high. *Consistency:* the three sources agree without contradiction. *Independence:* genuine, three separate institutions. *Limitations:* fewer independent sources than AF-1 or AF-2 (three), and no source addresses this indicator's reliability where a bud has been damaged by causes other than cane death (e.g. frost, insects) — an unresolved gap, not previously flagged.

**AF-4 — Stem flexibility/brittleness.** *Relevance:* direct to the question asked, but the only source addressing it is not rose-specific. *Source reliability:* the single source (LSU AgCenter) is an institutionally credible cooperative extension service, but its guidance is generic to woody plants. *Consistency/Independence:* cannot be assessed — single source, no corroboration found despite the search effort. *Limitations:* this is the commission's weakest-evidenced indicator by source count and specificity.

**AF-5 — Ambiguous/lookalike conditions.** *Relevance:* direct to the "what could a beginner misjudge" sub-question. *Source reliability:* high (University of Maryland Extension) and a directly on-point real-world case (Ask Extension / Colorado Master Gardener Program). *Consistency:* the two sources do not conflict; the second illustrates the first's practical difficulty. *Independence:* genuine, but only two sources, and only one lookalike condition (canker) was surfaced by this search — other possible lookalikes were not investigated. *Uncertainty:* the Ask Extension case is direct evidence that even an experienced Master Gardener could not resolve a real instance of this ambiguity from a photograph and description.

**AF-6 — Sporotrichosis/thorn-puncture risk.** *Relevance:* direct and specific — the source names rose gardeners as a recognised risk group. *Source reliability:* very high — a US state government public-health authority, a source type suited to establishing a medical/safety fact even where independent corroboration is thin. *Consistency/Independence:* only one source was found; no contradicting or corroborating source was located despite the search effort. *Limitations:* single-sourced, and the source does not quantify how common this risk is in practice — only that rose gardeners are a recognised risk group.

### 6. Managing Conflicting Evidence and Uncertainty

No source directly contradicts another on the AF-1 (pith) or AF-3 (bud) indicators. The one apparent tension — external stem colour as an indicator (AF-2, several sources) versus the explicit caution that live wood "may look brown instead of green" on some canes/cultivars (PlantTalk Colorado) — is not a disagreement between sources so much as a documented limitation on one indicator's reliability, and is recorded as such within AF-2 rather than resolved by preferring one source over another.

Genuine, source-documented uncertainty exists around cane discolouration that could indicate either normal seasonal/cultivar pigmentation or fungal canker (AF-5, Sources 12–13): the Ask Extension case (Source 13) shows a trained Master Gardener unable to reach a confident diagnosis from a photograph and description alone. This uncertainty is preserved here rather than resolved, consistent with ROC Operations Manual §7.5.

One minor, unresolved internal inconsistency exists within Source 1 (RHS): a second fetch of what presents as the same general-tips page did not reproduce a "grey, flaky bark" detail returned by the first fetch. This detail is not relied upon in any Assessed Finding above and is recorded here only for traceability.

### 7. Principal Findings

Findings are presented as Assessed Findings per EAS §3.2, each a single, discrete claim capable of being right or wrong independently of the others. None is merged with another for convenience, per EAS §3.2's explicit prohibition — note in particular that AF-2 and AF-3 were treated as one combined observation in the original (pre-FRDS) draft and are now separated, since their evidential support (source count and documented limitations) genuinely differs.

**7.1 AF-1 — Pith colour (established finding).** Cutting into a stem: white or pale-green pith indicates living wood; brown, gray, or black pith indicates dead wood. This is the most strongly evidenced single indicator in this commission. Several sources also describe a related working procedure: because a cane can be dead at the tip while still alive further down, cut from the tip downward in small increments, checking the pith at each cut, until living pith is found (PlantTalk Colorado; UGA CAES; consistent with cutting depth guidance in Texas A&M AgriLife, Iowa State, and Missouri Extension, which recommend cutting roughly 1/2 inch to 1 inch past any visible browning). Tool condition affects this check: dull tools crush the cane and can obscure the pith reading (Iowa State); sharp, clean tools are recommended generally (Illinois Extension; RHS). This procedural detail is drawn from the same and overlapping sources as AF-1 and is not separately confidence-rated.

**7.2 AF-2 — External bark/stem colour (established finding, with a documented limitation).** Green stem colour generally indicates living wood; brown, gray, black, or shriveled bark generally indicates dead wood (Illinois Extension; Iowa State; American Rose Society; RHS). This indicator carries a specific, sourced false-negative risk: live wood on older canes or bronze-stemmed cultivars can appear brown rather than green (PlantTalk Colorado). Several sources treat the pith cut (AF-1) as the check that follows an uncertain external read.

**7.3 AF-3 — Dormant buds/eyes (established finding).** Plump, healthy dormant buds indicate living tissue; a cane with no live buds anywhere along its length is a candidate for full removal (Clemson; Iowa State; American Rose Society).

**7.4 AF-4 — Stem flexibility/brittleness (weakly-evidenced finding).** Living wood bends; dead wood snaps cleanly and feels brittle. Found in only one source (LSU AgCenter), addressing woody plants generally rather than roses specifically. No rose-specific source in this search corroborated it.

**7.5 Photograph vs. physical check (ROC interpretation, not a sourced finding).** A photograph can reasonably show what the cited sources describe as externally visible: overall stem colour (AF-2), obvious shriveling or cracking, visible canker lesions (AF-5), and the presence or visible absence of buds and foliage (AF-3). A photograph cannot perform, or substitute for, the pith-colour check (AF-1) or the bend/flex test (AF-4), nor can a single static image replicate the "cut from the tip downward, checking as you go" procedure (§7.1). The clearest direct evidence for this distinction is the Ask Extension case (§7.6): an experienced Master Gardener working from a submitted photo and description could not resolve a genuinely ambiguous case, and recommended continued physical observation instead. **This synthesis is ROC's own reasoned interpretation connecting the cited sources' physical/procedural methods to the commission's photograph question. No single source in this evidence base states the photo-versus-physical-check distinction directly; it is inferred from what each indicator structurally requires, and is presented here as interpretation, not verified fact**, consistent with PIP AI Constitution §5 and §12. It is not assigned an Evidence Confidence Level (see §8.7).

**7.6 AF-5 — Ambiguous/lookalike conditions (established finding, narrow evidence base).** Red/purple/bronze cane colouring can be entirely normal (cultivar characteristic, seasonal pigmentation, cold exposure) or can indicate cane canker, a fungal disease (University of Maryland Extension). The evidence includes a directly on-point real case (Ask Extension) in which an experienced Extension Master Gardener, working from a submitted photo and description, could not confidently distinguish the two and recommended continued physical observation rather than a diagnosis. Cane canker lesions are described as sunken, discoloured, sometimes target-ringed areas that progressively kill tissue beyond the lesion — visually resembling simple dead wood at the point of removal, but reflecting a spreading disease process rather than static dormant-season death. The connection between canker management and tool-sanitising advice (Clemson, §4 Source 7) is ROC's own reasoned connection, not a statement made by either source alone.

**7.7 AF-6 — Sporotrichosis/thorn-puncture risk (established finding, single-sourced).** The New York State Department of Health documents rose gardeners as a recognised risk group for this fungal skin infection, entering through thorn punctures, and recommends gloves and long sleeves when handling rose bushes. This is the only public-health-sourced caution found and is treated as a genuine, if not quantified for frequency, safety-relevant fact. Multiple extension sources separately recommend sharp, clean tools (Illinois Extension; Iowa State), and Clemson HGIC specifically recommends disinfecting shears (e.g., a 70% alcohol dip) between cuts to avoid spreading disease — relevant both to making a clean diagnostic cut (AF-1) and to not spreading canker (AF-5) while checking or removing suspect wood. This tool-hygiene point supports both AF-1 and AF-5/AF-6 practically but is not itself set up as a separate Assessed Finding, since no source treats it as a point of genuine uncertainty or disagreement.

**8.1 AF-1 — Pith colour.** **Evidence Confidence Level: High.** Rationale: seven independent, institutionally reliable, geographically and editorially independent sources corroborate this finding in consistent (if not identically worded) terms, satisfying EAS §4.4–§4.5. Minor limitations: none material to this finding specifically. Remaining uncertainty: none material.

**8.2 AF-2 — External bark/stem colour.** **Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.** Rationale: reasonably well corroborated (4–5 independent sources) but carries a documented, sourced false-negative risk on older or bronze-cultivar canes that the evidence itself flags rather than resolves, per EAS §5.4's definition of Moderate ("notable limitations... remain; findings should be applied with appropriate caution"). Limitations: the false-negative risk noted above. Uncertainty: how frequently this false-negative occurs in practice is not established by the available evidence.

**8.3 AF-3 — Dormant buds/eyes.** **Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.** Rationale: three independent, reliable sources agree without contradiction, but this is fewer independent sources than AF-1 achieved, and no source addresses bud damage from causes other than cane death (e.g. frost, insects) as a potential confound. This finding is assessed independently of AF-2; the two happen to share a level, but for different reasons (AF-2 is limited by a documented caveat; AF-3 is limited by narrower source count and an unaddressed confound), consistent with EAS §4.8's requirement that no single factor be decisive alone. Limitations: narrower source base than AF-1; possible confounds not addressed by any source. Uncertainty: reliability of this indicator where bud damage has a cause other than cane death is not established.

**8.4 AF-4 — Stem flexibility/brittleness.** **Evidence Confidence Level: Low.** Rationale: this indicator — one of the specific sub-questions the research commission asked to be covered — rests on a single source that is not rose-specific, per EAS §5.4's definition of Low ("limited or subject to significant limitations... applied with considerable caution"). It should be treated in any downstream drafting as plausible and physiologically consistent with the other findings, but not as independently confirmed for roses by this research. Limitations: single-sourced; source not rose-specific. Uncertainty: whether the bend/snap test performs as reliably on rose canes specifically as the general woody-plant source suggests is not established.

**8.5 AF-5 — Ambiguous/lookalike conditions.** **Evidence Confidence Level: Low.** Rationale: only two independent sources address this finding, below the three-source threshold that supported a Moderate assessment for AF-3. One of the two is a single real-world case rather than general guidance; while directly on point and qualitatively strong (it demonstrates, rather than merely asserts, the difficulty), it is one instance and other lookalike conditions beyond canker were not investigated within this commission's scope. Limitations: narrow source base; single lookalike condition investigated. Uncertainty: whether other lookalike conditions exist that a beginner might confuse with dead wood is not established.

**8.6 AF-6 — Sporotrichosis/thorn-puncture risk.** **Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.** Rationale: this finding is single-sourced, which on source count alone would parallel AF-4's Low assessment. It is assessed higher because the single source is a government public-health authority speaking with direct, specific relevance to the exact claim (rose gardeners named as a recognised risk group), and because Source Reliability and Relevance are separate Evidence Assessment Factors from Independence (EAS Chapter 4) — no single factor is decisive alone (EAS §4.8). This is a reasoned judgement call within the range EAS §2.7 anticipates ("minor differences in reasoned judgement may occur"), and is recorded transparently as such rather than presented as a mechanical source-count outcome. Limitations: single-sourced; no independent corroboration found despite search effort. Uncertainty: the source does not quantify how common this risk is in practice.

**8.7 Photograph vs. physical check (§7.5).** Not assigned an Evidence Confidence Level. This is ROC's own reasoned interpretation connecting sourced material (AF-1 through AF-5) to the commission's photograph question, not itself a claim drawn from and directly supported by cited evidence. Per EAS §5.2, "Not Assigned" is used where a claim has not been sufficiently evaluated against sourced evidence to support a confidence level in its own right; that is the position here, and the Founders should weight §7.5 accordingly — as ROC's synthesis, not as an independently confirmed finding.
