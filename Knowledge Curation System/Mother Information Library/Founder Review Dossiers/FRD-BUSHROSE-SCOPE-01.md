## 1. Executive recommendation

"Bush rose" should not be treated by PIP as a formal botanical taxon or a universally standardised rose class.

The evidence indicates that it is primarily a **horticultural grouping based on plant form and garden use**. In British and Australasian usage, it most commonly refers to modern, free-standing roses grown as ordinary branched bushes—especially Hybrid Tea and Floribunda roses—as distinct from climbing, rambling, groundcover and standard-trained forms.

The Royal Horticultural Society gives the clearest narrow institutional definition: its "modern bush roses" category includes **Hybrid Tea and Floribunda roses**. The RHS treats shrub roses, climbers, ramblers, groundcover roses and standards as separate groups. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/modern-bush?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Australian rose-society material supports the idea that "bush" can describe the plant's **grown form**, rather than its formal cultivar classification. The Rose Society of New South Wales states that Hybrid Tea roses are available in both bush and standard form, while its broader classification pages separately recognise Hybrid Tea, Floribunda, shrub, miniature, standard, weeping standard and groundcover roses. ([The Rose Society of NSW](https://nsw.rose.org.au/roses/hybrid-tea?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### Recommended PIP position

For the first PIP profile, define a Bush Rose as:

**A free-standing, basal-branching modern garden rose grown in its natural bush form, belonging primarily to the Hybrid Tea, Floribunda or Grandiflora horticultural classes, and not trained or grafted into a climbing, rambling, groundcover, miniature, patio-standard or tree-like standard form.**

This definition should be accompanied by an explicit scope note:

**Within PIP, "Bush Rose" is a practical profile category, not a botanical classification. Individual cultivars retain their recognised horticultural class.**

I recommend including:

  - Hybrid Tea roses grown in bush form;
  - Floribunda roses grown in bush form;
  - Grandiflora roses grown in bush form, provisionally and with class-specific qualifications.

I recommend excluding:

  - shrub roses, including most English or Austin-type shrub roses;
  - Old Garden Roses;
  - species roses;
  - climbing and rambling roses;
  - groundcover roses;
  - miniature and miniflora roses;
  - patio roses where their classification or management differs materially;
  - standard, half-standard and weeping-standard roses;
  - any climbing sport of an otherwise included cultivar.

## 2. The central terminology problem

The expression "Bush Rose" is potentially misleading because three different classification ideas can become conflated:

1.  **Botanical identity** — membership of the genus *Rosa*;
2.  **Horticultural class** — Hybrid Tea, Floribunda, Grandiflora, Shrub, Miniature and others;
3.  **Growth or presentation form** — bush, climbing, groundcover or standard.

Authoritative sources do not consistently use "Bush Rose" at the same classificatory level.

The RHS uses "modern bush roses" as a practical group containing Hybrid Tea and Floribunda roses. It then treats shrub roses, standards, climbers, ramblers and groundcovers separately. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/modern-bush?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The American Rose Society instead presents Hybrid Tea, Floribunda, Grandiflora, Shrub and other categories as rose classifications. Its classification material does not establish "Bush Rose" as a parallel formal class. ([American Rose Society](https://rose.org/rose-classifications/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The Rose Society of New South Wales illustrates a third use: a Hybrid Tea cultivar can be sold or grown in "bush" form or in "standard" form. In this usage, "bush" describes how the rose is presented or propagated rather than replacing the Hybrid Tea classification. ([The Rose Society of NSW](https://nsw.rose.org.au/roses/hybrid-tea?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### Finding

PIP should therefore avoid describing Bush Rose as though it were a universally recognised formal class. It is better understood as an **operational grouping constructed from both horticultural class and growth form**.

## 3. How authoritative sources define or use the term

### 3.1 Royal Horticultural Society

The RHS provides the strongest direct definition found.

Its "Modern bush roses" guide states that the group includes:

  - Hybrid Tea roses;
  - Floribunda roses.

It distinguishes them principally by flowering presentation: Hybrid Teas tend to bear shapely flowers singly or in small clusters, while Floribundas generally bear flowers in larger clusters. Both are described as flowering over a long period, with modern cultivars often repeat-flowering. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/modern-bush?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The significance of the RHS framework is not only what it includes, but what it separates:

  - Hybrid Tea roses have their own pruning group;
  - Floribunda roses have their own pruning group;
  - climbing roses are separate;
  - ramblers are separate;
  - groundcover roses are separate;
  - shrub roses are separate. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/pruning/rhs-pruning-groups?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The RHS describes shrub roses as a group containing old-fashioned and modern shrub roses, including English shrub roses. It groups them together because of similar appearance and care, rather than placing them within the modern-bush category. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/shrub?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The RHS also defines standards structurally: a rose cultivar, often a modern or miniature rose, is grafted at the top of a tall bare stem. A climbing or rambling cultivar grafted in this manner produces a weeping standard. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/standard?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

#### Interpretation

Under the RHS framework, "modern bush rose" is a relatively narrow maintenance grouping, centred on Hybrid Tea and Floribunda roses and distinguished from shrub roses despite the fact that shrub roses are also physically bush-like.

This distinction is highly relevant to PIP because it indicates that **ordinary language habit alone is insufficient**. A rose can be a shrub in physical form without belonging to the horticultural grouping called modern bush roses.

### 3.2 American Rose Society

The American Rose Society's classification framework describes Hybrid Tea, Floribunda, Grandiflora, Shrub, Polyantha, Miniature and other recognised horticultural classes. Its Floribunda description emphasises clustered flowering and distinguishes this class from Hybrid Tea and Grandiflora. ([American Rose Society](https://rose.org/rose-classifications/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The ARS approach is important because it shows that the three modern classes most likely to be encountered as upright garden bushes—Hybrid Tea, Floribunda and Grandiflora—remain formally distinct.

ARS judging guidance likewise names Hybrid Tea, Grandiflora, Floribunda and Shrub roses separately rather than placing them within an official "Bush Rose" class. ([American Rose Society](https://rose.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/2024-Horticulture-Judging-Guidelines.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

#### Interpretation

The American system supports two conclusions:

  - "Bush Rose" should not displace recognised horticultural classes.
  - Grandiflora requires an explicit PIP decision because it occupies a recognised class between, or combining characteristics of, Hybrid Tea and Floribunda traditions but is omitted from the RHS's brief modern-bush definition.

### 3.3 Australian rose-society usage

The Rose Society of New South Wales describes a Hybrid Tea as being available in both "bush and standard form." This is particularly useful evidence because it demonstrates that a cultivar's horticultural class and its supplied physical form are separate descriptors. ([The Rose Society of NSW](https://nsw.rose.org.au/roses/hybrid-tea?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The same society describes Floribundas as generally upright plants bearing flowers in clusters or trusses, while Miniature and Miniflora roses are treated separately and generally remain much smaller. ([The Rose Society of NSW](https://nsw.rose.org.au/roses/floribunda?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Its rose-type overview separately lists:

  - standard roses;
  - weeping-standard roses;
  - groundcover roses;
  - Hybrid Tea roses;
  - Floribundas;
  - shrubs;
  - miniatures.

It explains that standards may be produced from Hybrid Tea, Floribunda, shrub or miniature cultivars grafted onto a single stem. ([The Rose Society of NSW](https://nsw.rose.org.au/roses?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Australian national exhibition rules also maintain separate registrations for Hybrid Tea, Floribunda, Grandiflora, Shrub, Ground Cover, Miniature and Miniflora classes. They place English, Hybrid Musk, Hybrid Rugosa and related types within the shrub grouping. ([Rose Society of Australia](https://rose.org.au/downloads/2026-02-28_041037_schedule-2026-australian-rose-championships.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

#### Interpretation

Australasian usage strongly supports treating "bush" as a **form qualifier**. A Hybrid Tea may be a bush rose when grown from the base as a conventional branched plant, but not when the same cultivar is grafted as a standard or represented by a climbing sport.

## 4. Where the sources agree

Despite variations in terminology, the sources converge on several important points.

### 4.1 Hybrid Tea roses are central

The RHS explicitly includes Hybrid Teas in modern bush roses, and Australian rose-society material describes them as available in bush form. The ARS recognises Hybrid Tea as a principal modern class. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/modern-bush?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

There is strong support for including conventional bush-form Hybrid Teas.

### 4.2 Floribunda roses are central

The RHS explicitly includes Floribundas in modern bush roses. American and Australian rose societies recognise them as a principal modern class characterised by clustered flowering. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/modern-bush?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

There is strong support for including conventional bush-form Floribundas.

### 4.3 Growth form matters independently of cultivar class

A cultivar may be grown as a conventional bush, grafted onto a tall stem as a standard, or occur in a climbing form. The NSW Rose Society and RHS standard-rose guidance both demonstrate that the same underlying class can appear in a different plant architecture. ([The Rose Society of NSW](https://nsw.rose.org.au/roses/hybrid-tea?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

PIP must therefore define the profile by both **class and form**.

### 4.4 Climbers, ramblers and standards require separate treatment

The RHS maintains separate groups for climbing roses, ramblers and standards, reflecting major differences in architecture and management. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/pruning/rhs-pruning-groups?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

These forms should not be absorbed into the Bush Rose profile merely because their cultivar ancestry may include a Hybrid Tea or Floribunda.

### 4.5 Shrub rose and bush rose are not dependable synonyms

The RHS places shrub roses outside its modern-bush category. Australian exhibition rules also recognise Shrub as a separate class that includes English, Hybrid Musk, Hybrid Rugosa and related roses. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/shrub?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

PIP should not treat every rose with a shrubby habit as a Bush Rose.

## 5. Where the sources differ

### 5.1 Whether Grandiflora belongs within the group

The clearest RHS modern-bush description mentions Hybrid Tea and Floribunda, but not Grandiflora. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/modern-bush?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The American Rose Society and Australian rose organisations recognise Grandiflora as a distinct class. Australian judging rules sometimes group Grandifloras operationally with Hybrid Tea or Floribunda roses depending on the purpose of the schedule, which indicates affinities with both but not identity with either. ([American Rose Society](https://rose.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/2024-Horticulture-Judging-Guidelines.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

#### Assessment

This appears more likely to reflect **regional classification practice** than a biological reason to exclude Grandifloras from an upright modern-bush profile.

Grandifloras are ordinarily free-standing modern garden roses and are likely to share much of the structural and maintenance framework relevant to Hybrid Teas and Floribundas. However, that similarity should be verified during later biological and pruning research rather than assumed at the definition stage.

**Recommendation:** Include Grandiflora provisionally, but preserve it as a distinct subclass and require later evidence to identify any class-specific exceptions.

### 5.2 Whether "bush" means a class or a physical form

The RHS uses "modern bush roses" as a named practical grouping. Australian usage can describe a Hybrid Tea as being supplied in either bush or standard form. American classification material generally relies on formal class names without creating a formal Bush Rose class. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/modern-bush?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

#### Assessment

These uses are compatible only when "Bush Rose" is understood as a **practical umbrella term**, not a formal registration category.

### 5.3 Whether shrub roses belong

In ordinary language, a shrub rose is plainly a rose that grows as a bush. Nevertheless, the RHS separates modern bush roses from shrub roses because the groups differ in appearance and care. Australian classification also preserves Shrub as a separate registered grouping. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/shrub?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

#### Assessment

Including shrub roses would make the profile superficially intuitive but horticulturally unstable. It would combine plants that may have different framework development, flowering patterns and pruning requirements.

For a trustworthy Plant Intelligence Model, the narrower functional boundary is preferable.

### 5.4 Whether miniature and patio roses belong

Miniature and Miniflora roses may grow as compact bushes, but recognised rose-society systems treat them as separate classes. The NSW Rose Society describes them as generally no more than about 50 centimetres high and suitable for edging or containers. ([The Rose Society of NSW](https://nsw.rose.org.au/roses/miniflora?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Standards may also be created using miniature cultivars, confirming again that class and physical presentation are separate. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/standard?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

#### Assessment

A definition based only on "small free-standing bush" would unintentionally include miniatures. Their scale and potentially different management justify a separate profile.

"Patio rose" is especially problematic because it may be used commercially for compact roses drawn from more than one formal class. PIP should not include patio roses automatically until the term receives its own classification review.

## 6. Proposed inclusion decisions

### 6.1 Hybrid Tea roses — include

**Evidence strength:** Strong

Reasons:

  - explicitly included by the RHS;
  - recognised internationally as a major modern class;
  - commonly offered in bush form;
  - structurally distinct from standards and climbing forms. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/modern-bush?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Boundary:** Include only the ordinary free-standing bush form. Exclude climbing Hybrid Teas and standard-grafted presentations.

### 6.2 Floribunda roses — include

**Evidence strength:** Strong

Reasons:

  - explicitly included by the RHS;
  - recognised internationally;
  - conventionally grown as free-standing, cluster-flowered garden bushes. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/modern-bush?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Boundary:** Include only the conventional bush form. Exclude climbing Floribundas and standards.

### 6.3 Grandiflora roses — include provisionally

**Evidence strength:** Moderate

Reasons:

  - recognised as a major modern rose class in American and Australian systems;
  - associated operationally with Hybrid Tea and Floribunda classes;
  - commonly occupies a similar upright garden role. ([American Rose Society](https://rose.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/2024-Horticulture-Judging-Guidelines.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Reason for qualification:

  - not named in the RHS's concise definition of modern bush roses;
  - possible differences in mature stature, flowering structure or pruning response must be preserved for later investigation.

**Boundary:** Include bush-form Grandifloras as a distinct internal subtype, not as interchangeable with Hybrid Tea or Floribunda.

## 7. Proposed exclusions

### 7.1 Shrub roses — exclude

This includes, unless later research justifies subdivisions:

  - modern shrub roses;
  - English or Austin-style shrub roses;
  - Hybrid Musk roses;
  - Hybrid Rugosa roses;
  - Hybrid Moyesii;
  - Kordesii shrub types;
  - comparable landscape shrub classes.

The RHS places old and modern shrub roses together in a separate care grouping. Australian classification similarly treats English and several hybrid groups as shrub roses. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/shrub?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Reason:** Similar physical appearance does not establish sufficiently similar biology or maintenance.

### 7.2 Old Garden Roses — exclude

Old Garden Roses encompass multiple historical classes with diverse flowering and growth behaviours. They should not be silently incorporated into a modern-bush profile merely because individual plants are bush-shaped.

**Reason:** Their inclusion would undermine the profile's biological and procedural coherence.

### 7.3 Species roses — exclude

Species roses represent botanical taxa rather than a uniform modern garden group. Their growth forms, flowering cycles and ecological behaviours may differ substantially.

**Reason:** They require a botanical or species-rose framework rather than a modern Bush Rose profile.

### 7.4 Climbers and ramblers — exclude

The RHS identifies climbers and ramblers as separate pruning groups. Their long canes, support requirements and framework management distinguish them from self-supporting bush roses. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/pruning/rhs-pruning-groups?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This exclusion applies even when the climbing cultivar is a sport or derivative of an included Hybrid Tea, Floribunda or Grandiflora.

### 7.5 Groundcover roses — exclude

Groundcover roses are treated as a separate group by the RHS and NSW Rose Society. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/pruning/rhs-pruning-groups?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Reason:** Their low, spreading architecture creates different structural and management questions.

### 7.6 Miniature and Miniflora roses — exclude

These are internationally recognised as separate classes and are substantially smaller in plant and flower scale. ([The Rose Society of NSW](https://nsw.rose.org.au/roses/miniflora?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Reason:** They deserve a dedicated profile rather than being treated as scaled-down Bush Roses.

### 7.7 Patio roses — exclude pending definition

"Patio" may indicate size, intended use or commercial presentation rather than a stable horticultural class.

**Reason:** Inclusion cannot be determined reliably from the label alone. PIP should first investigate whether a cultivar marketed as a patio rose is formally a compact Floribunda, Miniature, Miniflora or another type.

### 7.8 Standard and weeping-standard roses — exclude

Standards consist of a rose grafted onto the top of a tall, bare stem; weeping standards use climbing or rambling material to create a cascading crown. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/standard?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Reason:** Their architecture is deliberately constructed and changes structural assessment, support and pruning decisions.

## 8. Recommended working PIP definition

### Full internal definition

**For the purposes of the PIP Knowledge Library, a Bush Rose is a modern garden rose of the Hybrid Tea, Floribunda or Grandiflora horticultural class, grown as a free-standing, self-supporting plant whose principal branches arise from or near the crown or graft union. The term describes a practical profile grouping and growth form; it does not replace the plant's formal horticultural class.**

### Explicit exclusions attached to the definition

**The Bush Rose profile does not include shrub roses, Old Garden Roses, species roses, climbers, ramblers, groundcover roses, miniature or miniflora roses, undefined patio roses, or roses presented as standards, half-standards or weeping standards. Climbing forms of otherwise included classes are also excluded.**

### Plain working formulation

**A PIP Bush Rose is a conventional free-standing Hybrid Tea, Floribunda or Grandiflora rose grown from the base as a bush rather than as a climber, groundcover, miniature, shrub-class rose or standard.**

The full internal definition should govern research. The shorter formulation may later assist editors, but it should not replace the formal scope record.

## 9. Why the proposed definition is recommended

### 9.1 It follows the clearest authoritative use

The definition retains the RHS core of Hybrid Tea and Floribunda roses. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/modern-bush?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 9.2 It accommodates Australasian terminology

Describing the rose as being "grown in bush form" reflects Australian usage in which a Hybrid Tea may be supplied as either a bush or a standard. ([The Rose Society of NSW](https://nsw.rose.org.au/roses/hybrid-tea?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This is particularly relevant to PIP's likely New Zealand context.

### 9.3 It preserves recognised classification

A Floribunda does not cease to be a Floribunda when included in the PIP Bush Rose profile. The definition avoids creating a false competing taxonomy.

### 9.4 It creates a coherent future research subject

The included classes are sufficiently close in garden form to justify investigating shared structure, establishment and care, while still permitting subtype-specific findings.

Conversely, broad inclusion of all physically bushy roses would combine:

  - repeat-flowering modern roses;
  - once-flowering historical shrubs;
  - rugged landscape shrubs;
  - compact miniatures;
  - low groundcovers;
  - and potentially species roses.

That would make later recommendations difficult to qualify and potentially unsafe to generalise.

### 9.5 It prevents pruning advice from outrunning classification

The RHS already separates Hybrid Tea, Floribunda, shrub, climbing, rambling and groundcover roses in its pruning framework. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/pruning/rhs-pruning-groups?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

A narrow scope therefore reduces the risk that PIP will later issue a single pruning model to plants with materially different frameworks or flowering patterns.

## 10. Confidence and unresolved questions

### High-confidence conclusions

  - "Bush Rose" is not a botanical taxon.
  - Hybrid Tea and Floribunda roses belong within the core profile.
  - Standard, climbing, rambling and groundcover forms should be excluded.
  - Shrub rose should not be treated automatically as a synonym.
  - The profile must preserve each cultivar's formal class.

### Moderate-confidence conclusion

  - Bush-form Grandifloras should be included.

This is a reasoned scope recommendation rather than a universally stated institutional definition. The class is recognised authoritatively, but its inclusion extends beyond the RHS's explicit two-class wording. ([RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/roses/modern-bush?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### Questions reserved for later commissions

The following should not be decided within this definition commission:

  - whether Grandiflora pruning response is sufficiently similar for shared advice;
  - how compact Floribundas marketed as patio roses should be assigned;
  - whether any modern shrub subclasses eventually justify inclusion in a broader profile;
  - how grafted and own-root bush roses should be separated internally;
  - whether regional rose registration systems classify individual cultivars differently.

These questions concern later classification detail or plant biology and should not expand the present commission.

## 11. Founder decision requested

The founders are asked to decide whether PIP should adopt the following scope:

### Recommended scope

**Include**

  - Hybrid Tea — bush form;
  - Floribunda — bush form;
  - Grandiflora — bush form, provisionally.

**Exclude**

  - shrub classes;
  - Old Garden Roses;
  - species roses;
  - climbers and ramblers;
  - groundcovers;
  - miniatures and minifloras;
  - patio roses unless subsequently assigned to an included class;
  - standards, half-standards and weeping standards;
  - climbing forms of included classes.

### Recommended classification rule

**"Bush Rose" is a PIP operational profile category defined by horticultural class plus growth form. It is not presented as a formal botanical or universal rose classification.**

## 12. Curator's recommendation

I recommend approval of the proposed definition.

It is narrow enough to support coherent future research, broad enough to reflect common British and Australasian garden usage, and transparent about its status as a PIP working category.

Most importantly, it avoids the misleading conclusion that every rose growing as a shrub is horticulturally the same kind of rose.

The PIP Charter requires the Research Curator to preserve context, distinguish evidence from uncertainty and present findings for human review rather than determining final truth independently. The proposed definition follows that mandate by retaining recognised classifications, exposing the Grandiflora uncertainty and placing the final scope decision with the founders.
