## 1. Purpose and Scope

This ARC is the authoritative, structured representation of the Founder-approved information produced by the research commission on damaged growth on an established, dormant bush rose: definitions, causes and the boundary with dead wood (Q1); visible indicators by cause (Q2); what a photograph can and cannot establish (Q3); lookalikes and confounders (Q4); pruning choices and how far back to cut (Q5); deferral and borderline cases (Q6); and task-specific safety (Q7).

It supports the **damaged growth** supported observation in the Ask Pip MVP – Bush Rose V1 Architecture, one of the six observations the pruning journey addresses once a rose has passed the recently-planted and dormancy gates (`ARC-BUSHROSE-RECENTPLANT-01`, `ARC-BUSHROSE-DORMANCY-01`) and dead wood has been addressed (`ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01`). Weak or congested growth is researched and approved separately (`ARC-BUSHROSE-WEAKCONGESTED-01`). General cut mechanics remain governed by `ARC-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01`.

This ARC is the source from which KIT will build the damaged-growth Observation PKR.

## 4. Approved Findings — Full Content

Each Assessed Finding is reproduced here faithfully from the Founder-approved Evidence Assessment in `FRD-BUSHROSE-DAMAGEDGROWTH-02` §8, without new interpretation, per ROC Operations Manual §12.4.

### AF-1 — "Damaged" Is Conceptually Distinct From "Dead," Though Some Sources Blur the Two Procedurally

**Finding:** Several sources name damaged growth as its own category, distinct from dead wood, with its own causes and indicators. Other sources combine "dead, damaged or weak" into a single opening removal instruction, relying on a separately-stated pith test to distinguish them in practice.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Multiple independent institutional sources across publisher types converge on treating damage as a real, distinct category — the disagreement is about how concisely instructions are written, not about substance.

**Sources:** Kansas City Rose Society; Dunedin Botanic Garden ("Remove the 3Ds — Dead, Diseased and Damaged"); RHS; Clemson; Iowa State (the latter two combine dead/damaged in an opening instruction).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Several frequently-cited sources rely on a separately-given pith test rather than spelling out how to tell "damaged" from "dead" in the same sentence. See C-1.

### AF-2 — Multiple Distinct Causes of Damage Are Named Across Sources

**Finding:** Sources collectively name several distinct causes of damaged growth: frost/winter injury, mechanical breakage (including wind, rodent activity, and crossing/rubbing-cane contact), splitting/cracking, fungal canker, borer or other pest damage, and sunscald.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Each cause is independently named by at least one credible rose-specific source, and several (canker, frost injury) are corroborated by multiple independent sources.

**Sources:** RHS; Kansas City Rose Society; University of Missouri IPM; Kansas State MF799; Clemson; University of Maryland; University of Illinois (RPD 626); Marin Rose Society; PlantTalk Colorado; Oregon State; Rose Society of Victoria (sunscald).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Not every cause is evidenced equally — sunscald rests on a single source; rodent and snow-load damage are each named by only one source and not corroborated elsewhere.

### AF-3 — Weak/Thin/Unproductive Growth Is Its Own Category, Separate From Damage

**Finding:** Where sources address stem vigour or thickness as a pruning criterion, they treat it as a distinct category from damage — usually a size/vigour threshold (e.g., "thinner than a pencil") rather than a description of physical injury.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Three independent institutional sources apply the same separation, supporting this project's decision to research damaged growth and weak/congested growth as two distinct observations.

**Sources:** RHS ("spindly and crossing stems" listed separately from "dead and damaged wood"); Clemson; Kansas State MF799.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No source discusses whether a stem can be both weak and damaged at once, or how that overlap should be handled — left for `ARC-BUSHROSE-WEAKCONGESTED-01`.

### AF-4 — Canker Gives the Best-Corroborated, Most Consistent Visible-Indicator Picture of Any Damage Cause

**Finding:** Multiple independent sources converge on a consistent set of visible canker symptoms: discoloration ranging from tan to dark purplish-brown, a sunken texture, cracked margins where tissue falls away, girdling of the cane leading to dieback above the lesion, visible fungal fruiting bodies or spore masses, and sometimes a target-shaped ring pattern on larger stems.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Five independent institutional sources, across UK, US university-extension and specialist-society publisher types, converge on the same symptom picture with no contradiction.

**Sources:** Clemson; University of Maryland; Kansas State MF799; University of Illinois (RPD 626); RHS (fruiting-body detail).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No source describes oozing for rose cane canker specifically. None of substance otherwise — one of the two best-attested findings in this dossier.

### AF-5 — Frost/Winter Damage Shows Visually, but Definitive Confirmation Requires a Destructive Pith Test

**Finding:** Frost/winter-damaged growth is described as showing browning or blackening of shoot tips and general cane discoloration, but the boundary between damaged and dead tissue is confirmed only by cutting into the cane and inspecting the pith colour beneath — not by a non-destructive visual or flex check.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Multiple independent sources agree that surface discoloration is the visible cue, and separately agree on the pith test as the confirmation method.

**Sources:** RHS; Kansas City Rose Society; Texas A&M/El Paso handout.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** The pith test requires cutting into the cane, which cannot be done from a photograph. Whether surface discoloration alone is ever reliable enough to skip the pith test was not addressed.

### AF-6 — Mechanical/Crossing-Contact Damage and Broken Canes Are Visually Identifiable Where Visible; No Non-Destructive Test for a Hidden Split

**Finding:** Where a cane shows visible contact-wear from crossing/rubbing, or is visibly broken, sources treat this as sufficient visual confirmation. No source, rose-specific or general, describes a non-destructive test for detecting a split or crack that is not yet visible from outside.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low.

**Rationale:** Two independent sources confirm visible contact-wear and visible breakage as recognised indicators; a dedicated search for a flex/bend test found none.

**Sources:** RHS; Kansas City Rose Society.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Only visible damage is covered; a genuinely hidden split has no described detection method short of a destructive pith cut (U-1).

### AF-7 — Borer/Pest Damage Shows as Wilting Tips, an Entry Hole and Swelling, With a Notable Caveat

**Finding:** Where sources describe borer or stem-girdler damage, the indicators given are a suddenly wilting new cane tip, a visible entry hole, and sometimes a slight swelling near the affected area. One commonly-cited "cane borer" source, however, actually describes a beneficial hunting wasp that nests inside an already-existing pruning cut rather than a pest causing damage to live tissue.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low.

**Rationale:** Two to three independent sources on the indicators themselves; the wasp-versus-borer distinction is a genuinely useful, source-documented nuance that prevents over-flagging a harmless sign as damage.

**Sources:** Marin Rose Society; PlantTalk Colorado (#1463, the wasp caveat); Oregon State (rose stem girdler).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Sawdust/frass, often associated with borer damage in other plants, is not confirmed by any rose-specific source (U-2). Evidence is thinner than for canker or frost damage.

### AF-8 — A Photograph Can Plausibly Capture External Signs but Not Internal Tissue Condition; Physical Examination Is the Accepted Fallback

**Finding:** No rose-specific source directly addresses photo-versus-physical diagnosis of damaged growth. A photograph can plausibly capture external, gross-morphological signs (colour, shape, obvious splits, visible fruiting bodies, swelling location) but cannot establish internal tissue condition. Where a photo is ambiguous, the accepted professional fallback is a physical sample or an in-person visit, not a better photograph. Real diagnostic outcomes are inconsistent even among trained experts (C-3), so confidence should always be stated rather than presented as certain.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low (a ROC synthesis of general, non-rose-specific evidence, extrapolated per EAS §2.10).

**Rationale:** Convergent conclusion across peer-reviewed academic literature, multiple independent extension diagnostic-clinic sources, and rose-specific practice, even though no single source addresses the question in full.

**Sources:** Berland et al. (2019), *Forests* 10(4):349; Kennard et al. (1996), *Journal of Arboriculture* 22(6):249-254; Mississippi State Extension; Colorado State Extension (Douglas County); Alabama Extension; Marin Rose Society (pith-cut for borer extent).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No source frames this exactly as a "photo-based cane-damage diagnosis" question; all evidence is assembled from adjacent contexts. How much this general limitation matters for a garden rose specifically (much smaller than an urban tree) was not addressed.

### AF-9 — Harmless Insect Galls Are a Well-Documented Lookalike for Damage

**Finding:** Certain insect galls on rose canes (the rose bedeguar gall/"Robin's pin-cushion," and mossy rose gall) look alarming but are generally harmless to the plant, and could plausibly be mistaken for damage by a beginner. Heavy or prolonged gall infestation can, however, occasionally weaken or kill tissue above the gall.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Three independent institutional sources agree on the general harmlessness of galls, valuable as a lookalike category to prevent unnecessary cutting.

**Sources:** RHS; Washington State University (Hortsense); Iowa State University Extension (the heavy-infestation caveat).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** The heavy-infestation caveat comes from only one of the three sources, without a clear severity threshold.

### AF-10 — Canker's Early Colouring Genuinely Overlaps With Normal New-Cane Pigmentation

**Finding:** Early canker symptoms are described as small yellow, dark red or purple spots — colours that also occur naturally in new rose-cane growth in many cultivars. This is a real, source-documented confounder, serious enough that a trained Extension diagnostician declined to resolve one real case from photographs alone.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low (a single documented case plus one descriptive source; genuine but thin).

**Rationale:** A concrete, real-world documented instance of exactly this confusion, from a credible extension source.

**Sources:** University of Illinois (RPD 626); an Ask Extension case response.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Rests on one descriptive source and one case, not a systematic study of how often this confusion occurs.

### AF-11 — Once Damage Is Confirmed, Sources Near-Unanimously Instruct Cutting to Healthy Tissue, Verified by Pith Colour

**Finding:** With near-unanimous, broad support across the source base, once damaged growth is confirmed, it is cut back until healthy tissue is reached — verified by cutting into the cane and inspecting the exposed pith colour (white or pale green indicates living tissue; brown, black or grey indicates dead tissue).

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Broad, independent corroboration across nine or more institutional sources of every publisher type used in this project, unanimous in direction and method.

**Sources:** American Rose Society; RHS; Kansas City Rose Society; Clemson; Oregon State; Iowa State; Texas A&M/El Paso; PlantTalk Colorado; University of Arkansas.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** None of substance. Alongside AF-4, the best-attested finding in this dossier, and consistent with, and building directly on, the live/dead pith test already approved in `ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01`.

### AF-12 — How Far Back to Cut Depends Mainly on Severity, Not Cause — a Materially Different Rule From Prior Dossiers

**Finding:** Sources give an explicit severity-based branch: minor or slight damage is shortened only as far as needed to reach healthy tissue (a partial cut); severe damage, or the absence of any living pith/buds below the damaged area, results in full removal of the cane to its base. This differs materially from the "always remove fully to point of origin" pattern already recorded for crossing/rubbing and inward-growing stems, where the removal decision does not depend on degree.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Six independent sources agree on the same severity-dependent branching logic.

**Sources:** Purdue Extension (clearest statement); Clemson; Texas A&M/El Paso; Iowa State; University of Georgia; an Ask Extension case (Knock Out roses).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** "Slight" versus "severe" is not given a precise, universal threshold by any source. The operational answer regardless is to keep cutting progressively further down the cane until healthy pith is found (AF-11).

### AF-13 — Cause Adds Specific Extra Steps Beyond the General Healthy-Tissue Rule

**Finding:** Beyond the general "cut to healthy tissue" rule (AF-11, AF-12), canker/disease-suspect damage gets two extra steps not given for plain mechanical or frost damage: a larger safety margin (commonly 2–3 inches below the visibly infected area, or "well below" it) and tool disinfection between cuts. Borer/pest damage gets a different extra step: sealing the fresh cut to deter re-infestation.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Two independent sources for the canker margin/disinfection step; two independent sources for the borer/sealing step; a clear contrast within a single source between how it treats plain damage versus disease.

**Sources:** University of Illinois (RPD 626); an Ask Extension response (UF/IFAS Orange County); Marin Rose Society; PlantTalk Colorado; Kansas City Rose Society (contrast).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** The exact margin for canker (2–3 inches, or "well below") is not perfectly consistent in wording between sources, though the direction is.

### AF-14 — Frost/Winter Damage's Full Extent Is Often Not Visible Immediately

**Finding:** Several sources advise waiting until at or near spring bud break to assess and prune frost/winter-damaged canes, since the full extent of damage may not be apparent right away, and note that a second, later pruning pass can be needed as further dieback becomes visible.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Four independent sources agree on the general "wait, don't prune immediately after cold damage" principle.

**Sources:** Portland Rose Society; UF/IFAS (Okaloosa); NDSU; N.C. Cooperative Extension.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Sources differ subtly on exactly what "waiting" means (see C-2). Whether a specific still-attached, partially damaged cane might recover if left unpruned was not addressed by any tier-1 source (U-3).

### AF-15 — For Canker or Disease-Suspect Damage, Disinfect Pruning Tools Between Cuts

**Finding:** Beyond generic "keep tools clean and sharp" advice already approved for general pruning, multiple independent sources recommend disinfecting the cutting blade specifically when working on canker- or disease-suspect wood, using 70% rubbing alcohol or a diluted bleach solution (commonly around 1:9 to 1:10), at a frequency ranging from "before each cut" to "at minimum between plants."

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Five independent institutional sources, across plant-pathology extension, general extension and RHS, converge on the same additional step.

**Sources:** University of Illinois (RPD 626); Oklahoma State; Clemson; Iowa State; RHS.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Exact frequency and dilution vary somewhat between sources (a range, not a single universal number).

### AF-16 — Disease- or Canker-Affected Prunings Should Be Burned or Trashed, Not Composted

**Finding:** Sources consistently advise disposing of canker- or disease-affected prunings by burning them or discarding them with household trash, rather than adding them to an ordinary compost pile, because home composting rarely reaches temperatures sufficient to kill plant pathogens in slow-to-break-down woody material.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Four independent institutional sources agree on the same disposal guidance, with RHS explicitly explaining the underlying reason.

**Sources:** RHS; University of Illinois (RPD 626); Oklahoma State; Clemson.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Guidance is specific to disease/canker-affected material; sources do not address disposal of plain mechanically-damaged prunings, presumably needing no special treatment.

## 5. Preserved, Unreconciled Points of Variation

**C-1 — Whether "damaged" is cleanly distinguished from "dead" wood in source language.** Kansas City Rose Society, RHS and Dunedin Botanic Garden name damaged growth as its own distinct category; other sources combine "dead, damaged or weak" into a single opening instruction, relying on a separately-stated pith test to sort them in practice. Not a substantive disagreement about whether the categories are real — likely a difference in how concisely each source writes. Preserved as unresolved terminological variation. Practical default: §6.

**C-2 — What "wait for spring" means for assessing frost/winter damage.** Two subtly different timing philosophies appear under the same general "wait for spring" advice: waiting to see live growth actually emerge (UF/IFAS Okaloosa; Myers, non-tier-1), versus assessing just before bud break by cane colour (NDSU; N.C. Cooperative Extension). Both share the same underlying concern but differ on how much certainty to wait for. Preserved as unresolved. Practical default: §6 favours the more conservative timing.

**C-3 — Real-world inconsistency in whether a photo alone is enough to diagnose canker.** Two Ask Extension cases with very similar red/purple cane colouring reached different outcomes — one expert declined to diagnose from photos, another did diagnose "stem canker" from photographs alone. Not treated as evidence that photo-based diagnosis is reliable or unreliable in general; treated instead as direct support for stating uncertainty honestly rather than presenting any single photo-based observation as certain. No default is needed beyond that existing Architecture principle.

## 6. Approved Practical Defaults Carried Forward

The following ROC-recommended defaults were adopted for questions the evidence left open, and are approved for operational use alongside the Assessed Findings above. Each may be overridden by a Founder at any time.

- **No non-destructive test for a hidden split (U-1):** treat any visible sign (a bulge, a visible crack line, discoloration, or wilting beyond a point on the cane) as sufficient trigger for closer physical examination of that spot; absent any visible sign, rely on the existing requirement to physically examine the rose.
- **Sawdust/frass as a borer indicator (U-2):** not confirmed for roses; do not include it as a stated indicator until sourced. Rely on the confirmed indicators (wilting tip, entry hole, swelling near the site).
- **Whether a compromised but still-attached cane might recover unpruned (U-3):** once damage is confirmed on a specific cane, guide toward the severity-based cutting rule (AF-12) rather than an indefinite "leave it and see" for that cane; "decide later" remains available at the whole-plant/whole-decision level (consistent with AF-14/C-2), not as a claim that a specific damaged cane will heal itself.
- **Whether professional help is ever specifically warranted because of damage severity (U-4):** governed by the app's general uncertainty/confidence gates; the evidence if anything argues against a "give up" outcome, since sources frame roses as resilient even after severe cutback.
- **Safety for handling broken/splintered canes or canes under tension (U-5):** cross-refer to `ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01`'s approved generic safety content as primary guidance; additionally, by analogy from general (non-rose) arboriculture sources: handle a broken or hanging cane carefully, anticipate that a bent or trapped cane may move unexpectedly when cut, and cut a little at a time where a cane appears to be under tension.
- **Further beginner lookalikes — thorns, leaf/bud scars, graft-union swelling (U-6):** no source names any of these as damage lookalikes; no specific confounder list is offered for them. The existing requirement to confirm any observation on the physical rose before acting remains the primary safeguard.
- **Recognising sunscald with enough detail to distinguish it from disease (U-7):** thin, single-source evidence with no visual descriptor; treat sunscald as a low-confidence possibility only, not a confidently recognisable category from a photograph, and rely on physical confirmation.
