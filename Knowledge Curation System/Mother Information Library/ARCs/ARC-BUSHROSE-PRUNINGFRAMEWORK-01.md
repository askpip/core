## 1. Purpose and Scope

This ARC is the authoritative, structured representation of the Founder-approved information produced by the research commission on how an established, dormant bush rose (Hybrid Tea, Floribunda, or Grandiflora) is whole-bush pruned: the purposes of pruning (Q1), categories of growth removed (Q2), order of work (Q3), how growth categories interact (Q4), structure and framework (Q5), limits and restraint (Q6), deferral and when to seek help (Q7), what can be judged from a photograph (Q8), and cut mechanics (Q9).

It supports the pruning stage of the Ask Pip journey once a rose has passed the recently-planted and dormancy gates (governed by `ARC-BUSHROSE-RECENTPLANT-01` and `ARC-BUSHROSE-DORMANCY-01`) and once dead wood has been addressed (`ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01`, not re-assessed here). This ARC is the source from which KIT will build the pruning-framework PKR content covering the six supported observations: damaged growth, crossing or rubbing stems, inward-growing stems, weak or congested growth, old/unproductive wood, and the main framework to retain.

## 4. Approved Findings — Full Content

Each Assessed Finding is reproduced here faithfully from the Founder-approved Evidence Assessment in `FRD-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-04` §8, without new interpretation, per ROC Operations Manual §12.4. Source codes not spelled out below are as defined in that dossier's §4 Evidence Collected — Source Log.

### AF-1 — Purposes of Pruning

**Finding:** Recognised sources describe the purposes of pruning a dormant bush rose as removing unhealthy wood (dead, damaged or diseased), opening the bush to light and air, and encouraging vigorous flowering growth while controlling size and shape.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Sixteen sources across UK, NZ, society/garden and US extension groups state closely similar purposes with no contradiction; none ranks the purposes against each other, so the finding cannot say which purpose prevails when they pull apart (see §7, U-1).

**Sources:** RHS, DUN, RBG, OSU, ILL, CLE, ISU, UCG, UMD, PUR, KCRS, CCER, TAMN, YAT, SPR, ARS.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Minor emphasis differences only (bloom quality, appearance, disease reduction).

### AF-2 — Damaged or Diseased Wood Is Removed

**Finding:** Damaged wood (broken, cold-injured, or wind- or insect-damaged) and diseased wood is removed when pruning a dormant bush rose.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Nineteen sources across all four source groups agree; none advises leaving damaged or diseased wood. Dead wood itself is governed by ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01 and is not re-assessed here.

**Sources:** RHS, DUN, YAT, SPR, ARS, KCRS, RBG, OSU, ILL, CLE, ISU, CSU, UCG, UCF, UGA, UMD, PUR, TAMK, CCER.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Sources rarely describe how to recognise damage as distinct from death on a dormant stem, or from a photograph.

### AF-3 — Crossing or Rubbing Stems Are Removed

**Finding:** Crossing or rubbing stems are a category of growth that sources advise removing when pruning a dormant bush rose.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Eleven sources across all four groups name crossing or rubbing growth as growth to remove; none advises retaining it, and several treat it as an early step.

**Sources:** RHS, YAT, SPR, KCRS, RBG, ARS, OSU, CLE, UCG, UCF, UGA.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No source says how to choose which of two crossing stems to remove (partially addressed at AF-33), or how to tell rubbing from touching.

### AF-4 — Inward-Growing or Centre-Crowding Stems Are Removed

**Finding:** Stems that grow towards, or crowd, the centre of the bush are a category of growth that sources advise removing.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Eight sources across three groups point the same way and none advises retaining such growth, but the terms used are not uniform and no source defines inward growth or distinguishes it from crossing or general crowding.

**Sources:** DUN, UGA, KCRS, RBG, UCG, ISU, OSU, CLE.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Whether inward-growing and centre-crowding stems are one category or two is not settled by the sources; no UK source addresses it.

### AF-5 — Weak, Thin or Spindly Growth Is Removed

**Finding:** Weak, thin, spindly or twiggy growth is removed when pruning a dormant bush rose.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Thirteen sources across three groups agree using consistent language; a pencil-thickness measure (AF-6) is commonly used to define "weak."

**Sources:** DUN, YAT, SPR, RBG, KCRS, ARS, ILL, CLE, UCG, UCF, TAMK, UAEX.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** RHS does not list weak growth as a removal category; it says less vigorous shoots are pruned more severely instead. Whether a weak stem is removed outright or cut short varies by source.

### AF-6 — A Pencil-Thickness Threshold Separates Weak from Sound Canes

**Finding:** Sources use roughly the thickness of a pencil as the threshold below which a cane is regarded as weak, and above which a cane is worth retaining.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Nine sources use the same measure with consistent meaning, though all are from the United States and independence between them cannot be established (EAS §4.5); at least one source treats it as less important for floribundas.

**Sources:** ILL, CLE, UCG, UCF, TAMK, RBG, ARS, KCRS.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Whether the threshold holds for floribundas and grandifloras is unclear.

### AF-7 — Old, Woody or Unproductive Canes Are Removed

**Finding:** Old, woody or unproductive canes are removed in favour of younger canes.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Thirteen sources across all four groups agree on the direction of the advice, but do not agree on what makes wood "old" (an age of three years, a grey and craggy appearance, or simply the oldest or thickest canes) or on how many to remove.

**Sources:** RHS, DUN, YAT, ARS, KCRS, RBG, UCG, UCF, UMD, UAEX, CCER.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** How a gardener, or a photograph, would tell old wood from young wood is addressed separately at AF-36; the age-in-years question remains unresolved (§5, C6).

### AF-8 — Suckers Below the Bud Union Are Removed

**Finding:** On grafted roses, growth arising from below the bud union (rootstock suckers) is removed, and two sources say to tear it away at its origin.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Seven sources across three groups agree on removal; two specify tearing rather than cutting, the rest give no method.

**Sources:** ILL, ISU, UCG, UCF, PUR, ARS, YAT.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Applies to grafted roses only; how a gardener recognises a sucker from a basal shoot of the rose itself is not established.

### AF-9 — Strong, Vigorous and Generally Younger Canes Are Retained

**Finding:** The canes retained are the strong, healthy and vigorous ones, and in several sources the younger or previous year's canes.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Ten sources across three groups agree that retained canes are strong and healthy; seven of the ten specify younger or newer wood, the rest say only healthy or stout.

**Sources:** RHS, OSU, CLE, UGA, UMD, UCG, UCF, ILL, KCRS, UAEX.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Definitions of "younger" differ; no NZ source supports the point.

### AF-10 — New Basal Shoots Are Signs of Health and Replacement Canes

**Finding:** Sources treat new shoots arising from the base of the bush or the bud union as a sign of good health and as a route to renewing the framework.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low.

**Rationale:** Two reputable, consistent sources; one concerns health, the other encouraging shoots. No source describes how to recognise basal shoots on a dormant bush.

**Sources:** ARS, UCG.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Not tested against other sources in this commission.

### AF-11 — Stated Reasons for Removing Crossing Stems

**Finding:** Sources give two reasons for removing crossing stems: that rubbing damages the canes and encourages disease, and that crossing stems obstruct air circulation.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low.

**Rationale:** Two university extension sources give these reasons; the other nine sources that name crossing stems give no reason at all.

**Sources:** OSU, UCG.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No source explains how much rubbing is harmful, over how long, or whether contact without rubbing is harmful.

### AF-12 — Dead, Damaged and Diseased Wood Is Dealt with First

**Finding:** Where sources give an order of work, dead, damaged and diseased wood is removed first.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Twelve sources across all four groups give the same first step, with no contrary order found; several remove crossing or rubbing stems in the same opening phase.

**Sources:** RHS, YAT, SPR, OSU, CLE, ISU, CSU, UMD, PUR, RBG, KCRS, ILL.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Sources rarely give a reason for the order beyond removing obvious problems before shaping.

### AF-13 — Removal Is Completed Before the Remaining Canes Are Shortened

**Finding:** Sources that give a sequence complete the removal of unwanted growth (thinning) before shortening the canes that remain (heading).

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Eight sources across all four groups give this sequence and none reverses it; the stated reason is usually only that shaping follows clearing.

**Sources:** RHS, YAT, SPR, OSU, ILL, UCG, RBG, KCRS.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** The remaining sources are silent on sequence rather than disagreeing.

### AF-14 — The Aim Is an Open-Centred Form with Canes Spread Around the Base

**Finding:** Sources describe the aim as an open-centred bush (vase, cup or spoked shape) with canes spread evenly around the base, so that light and air reach the interior.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Fifteen sources across all four groups agree, using differing terms (vase, cup, V-shape, "spokes of a wheel," "open centre") for the same aim.

**Sources:** OSU, RHS, DUN, UCG, YAT, PUR, ARS, UGA, ILL, CLE, RBG, KCRS, CSU, SPR.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No source says how to judge from an overview photograph whether a bush is close to this form (partially addressed at AF-41); how strictly vase shapes apply to floribundas is not stated.

### AF-15 — A Small Number of Strong Canes Is Retained; Published Counts Vary

**Finding:** Sources advise retaining a small number of strong canes, and the published counts fall between three and eight.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Ten sources agree the number is small and the canes are spaced around the base; specific counts differ (three to eight, depending on source) and none ties the count to bush size or class.

**Sources:** OSU, ILL, CLE, UGA, UCG, DUN, KCRS, PUR, CCER.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Nine of ten sources are US-based; how the count depends on size, age or class is not addressed.

### AF-16 — Several Sources Frame the Task as Choosing Which Canes to Keep

**Finding:** Several sources frame the task as choosing the canes to keep and removing the rest, alongside or instead of removing categories of growth.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Seven sources describe this approach; none conflicts with the category-by-category approach. This is a finding about how guidance is organised, not a horticultural outcome in itself.

**Sources:** OSU, ILL, CLE, UCG, DUN, PUR, CCER.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Whether the two framings give the same result on the same bush is not addressed by any source directly, though §6 records the ROC's own reasoning that they are sequential, not competing, stages.

### AF-17 — Height Is Commonly Reduced by About One-Third to One-Half

**Finding:** Sources commonly express the reduction of the retained canes as a fraction of their length or height, most often about one-third to one-half.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Ten sources across three groups agree on the order of magnitude, though the range runs from one-third to two-thirds and absolute final heights differ widely between sources (§5, C1).

**Sources:** UCG, UCF, UGA, DUN, RBG, YAT, UAEX, SPR, ILL, KCRS.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Which fraction is appropriate for a given rose or climate is not established.

### AF-18 — Hybrid Teas Are Pruned Lower Than Floribundas

**Finding:** Hybrid teas (and grandifloras) are pruned lower, or to fewer canes, than floribundas.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Three reputable UK and US sources agree in direction and, where quantified, in magnitude; other sources give one figure for all classes together or none.

**Sources:** RHS, OSU, CCER.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Whether grandifloras should be grouped with hybrid teas or treated separately is addressed further at AF-37.

### AF-19 — Once-Flowering, Old Garden and Rugosa Roses Are Pruned Differently

**Finding:** Once-flowering roses (old garden roses, ramblers and rugosas) are pruned lightly and, where they flower on old wood, after flowering rather than in dormancy.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Nine sources across three groups agree these roses are not pruned as modern repeat-flowering bush roses are; this marks a boundary of supported scope rather than a pruning rule for hybrid teas or floribundas.

**Sources:** ILL, CLE, UGA, RBG, YAT, SPR, CCER, KCRS, UCG.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** None material to the claim itself.

### AF-20 — Multi-Year Renewal Removes About One-Third of the Old Canes Each Year

**Finding:** Where sources describe renewing a bush over more than one year, they remove about one-third of the old canes each year.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low.

**Rationale:** Four sources use consistent figures, though they concern shrub, landscape and climbing roses as well as general bushes, none specifically an overgrown hybrid tea or floribunda. Reinforced, not extended, by the general-arboriculture figure at AF-40.

**Sources:** ILL, UCF, UAEX, CCER.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Whether the one-third figure applies specifically to hybrid teas and floribundas is not established; no source gives a condition that triggers staged renovation instead of a single session (partially addressed at AF-39, AF-40).

### AF-21 — The Cut Is Made Above an Outward-Facing Bud

**Finding:** For most cuts back to a bud, sources advise choosing a bud that faces outward, away from the centre of the bush.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Eighteen sources across three groups agree, some with stated reasons (a more open centre, air circulation); a second RHS source corroborates this independently (AF-38).

**Sources:** DUN, YAT, SPR, ARS, KCRS, RBG, OSU, ILL, CLE, ISU, ISUF, CSU, UCG, UCF, UGA, UMD, PUR, TAMK.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** RHS's primary source gives no bud-direction advice; two sources allow exceptions for a horizontally spreading cane.

### AF-22 — The Cut Is Made About a Quarter-Inch Above the Bud

**Finding:** Sources that give a distance most often say to cut about one-quarter inch (about 6 mm) above the bud. Texas A&M's Earth-Kind Landscaping page gives the reasoning behind the minority half-inch figure directly: too close to the bud risks the bud dying; too far risks the wood above it dying back. The quarter-inch and half-inch figures are different, defensible choices along the same tradeoff, not an unexplained disagreement.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Eleven sources give the same quarter-inch figure, repeated across institutions; three US sources give a different distance (§5, C2), now understood as a different resolution of the same tradeoff rather than an arbitrary difference.

**Sources:** OSU, ILL, CLE, ISU, ISUF, CSU, UCG, UCF, UGA, RBG, ARS.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** All eleven primary sources are from the United States; whether a quarter or half inch changes outcomes in practice is not addressed. The majority figure is adopted as the practical default (§6).

### AF-23 — The Cut Is Slanted at About 45 Degrees

**Finding:** Sources that give an angle describe a slanting cut of roughly 45 degrees, not a flat cut.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Seven sources give an angle in the same range, from NZ and US sources only, with no UK or society source; two Southern Hemisphere sources dispute whether angle matters at all (carried as conflict C11, AF-38).

**Sources:** OSU, ILL, UCG, DUN, YAT, TAMK, CSU.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** See §5, C11.

### AF-24 — Which Way the Slant Falls in Relation to the Bud

**Finding:** Sources that specify the direction of the slant most often say it slants away from the bud.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low.

**Rationale:** Five sources use consistent wording and one gives a reason (sap flow); three other sources describe the slant as "in the same direction as the bud" or "at the same angle as the bud," which cannot be shown to be the same geometry even after re-reading the original pages directly for a diagram or fuller context — none was found.

**Sources:** OSU, ILL, UCG, DUN, ARS.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Whether the wordings describe one geometry or two remains genuinely unresolved. The majority "away from the bud" wording is adopted as the practical default (§6).

### AF-25 — The Tool Is Chosen by the Thickness of the Stem

**Finding:** Sources advise choosing the tool by stem thickness: bypass pruners for thin canes, loppers for thicker canes and a pruning saw for the thickest or oldest canes.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Nine sources across three groups agree that tool choice follows stem thickness and most name the same tiers; several separately state a preference for bypass over anvil pruners.

**Sources:** OSU, CLE, ISU, UCG, UMD, TAMK, RBG, ARS, YAT.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** The thresholds at which one tool gives way to another differ (AF-26); RHS gives no tool advice.

### AF-26 — Thickness at Which Hand Pruners Give Way to Loppers

**Finding:** Sources that give a figure say hand pruners are suitable up to about three-quarters of an inch, with a general woody-plant text giving one-half inch.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low.

**Rationale:** Two sources agree on three-quarters of an inch; a general (non-rose) text gives a different, smaller figure and also depends on the tool.

**Sources:** OSU, ISU, TAMK.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Only three sources give any figure at all.

### AF-27 — Tools Are Sharp and Clean

**Finding:** Sources advise using sharp, clean tools so that the cut is clean and does not crush the cane.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Twelve sources across three groups agree, with reasons given (dull tools crush canes; a clean cut speeds healing and reduces disease potential).

**Sources:** OSU, ISU, UCG, UCF, RBG, DUN, YAT, SPR, UMD, TAMK, CLE, ARS.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No source reports a direct comparison of sharp versus blunt tools; the advice rests on practitioner consensus.

### AF-28 — Tools Are Disinfected Between Plants

**Finding:** Several sources advise disinfecting tools between plants (or after cutting diseased wood) to avoid spreading disease.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Seven sources agree on the practice; the method and frequency differ, and no source cites evidence of effectiveness.

**Sources:** OSU, CLE, UCG, UCF, YAT, ISU, TAMK.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Frequency and method are not settled; product-specific detail is recorded only as what sources say, not as a recommendation. A simple default is adopted at §6.

### AF-29 — Removed Stems Are Cut at Their Origin and Stubs Are Not Left

**Finding:** A stem chosen for removal is cut off at its point of origin (the base, the bud union or its parent stem), and stubs are not left.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Nine sources across three groups are consistent; five of the nine concern dead canes specifically (also governed by ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01), leaving only four addressing other categories.

**Sources:** ILL, UCG, PUR, DUN, KCRS, YAT, ARS, TAMK, ISU.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Whether the rule applies equally to all categories is not directly addressed.

### AF-30 — Sealing Cut Ends Is Advised by Some Sources and Is Contested

**Finding:** Some sources advise sealing the cut ends of larger canes with glue, nail polish or pruning sealer to deter cane borers, while a general woody-plant text advises against wound dressings.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low.

**Rationale:** Five sources advising sealing agree on the stated purpose (cane borers); seventeen sources, including every UK and NZ source, are silent; the sole dissent is a general, non-rose-specific text. AF-35 explains why the two positions are not actually contradictory.

**Sources:** CLE, ILL, UMD, KCRS, ARS, UGA.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** See AF-35 and §5, C4. A practical default is adopted at §6.

### AF-31 — Thinning Cuts and Heading Cuts Are Functionally Different

**Finding:** A cut that removes a stem entirely, back to its point of origin (thinning), and a cut that shortens a stem partway along its length, back to a bud (heading), are treated in the pruning literature as different in purpose and in the plant's response, not as one kind of cut applied in two places.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** A peer-reviewed general-arboriculture source (Harris, *Journal of Arboriculture*, 1994) defines the distinction; UC Marin Master Gardeners independently corroborates it and adds that heading cuts seal more poorly; UCG applies the same distinction directly to rose pruning, matching the general definition exactly.

**Sources:** Harris, *Journal of Arboriculture* (1994); UC Marin Master Gardeners, "Pruning Cuts"; UCG.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Only one of the three sources is rose-specific; whether the two cut types need different tools was not addressed.

### AF-32 — On a Congested or Overgrown Bush, Keeper Canes Are Identified in a Set Sequence

**Finding:** For a mature or congested bush where the target open-centred structure is hard to see, one institutional source describes identifying keeper canes in sequence: dead/damaged/diseased wood first; then old, thick, unproductive canes; then canes crossing through the centre; only then assess the remaining shape and shorten the survivors.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low.

**Rationale:** One directly verified institutional source (Chicago Botanic Garden, with a named senior horticulturist) gives a specific, reasoned sequence consistent with the general order of work already established (AF-12, AF-13). A secondary, unverified citation of RHS material describes a different, vigour-based selection principle for old/shrub roses specifically, recorded as a caveat rather than a second supporting source.

**Sources:** Chicago Botanic Garden, "Pruning Mature Roses?" (Karen Zaworski, with Tom Soulsby).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Single directly-verified source; whether this sequence differs meaningfully from ordinary annual pruning's order of work was not addressed.

### AF-33 — Where Two Canes Cross, One Source Gives an Explicit Rule

**Finding:** When two canes cross or crowd each other, one source states an explicit tie-breaking rule: remove the smaller of the pair, keeping the larger cane.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low.

**Rationale:** One direct source (Portland Rose Society) states the size-based rule. A second source is loosely consistent in spirit but uses a different (age/colour) criterion, so is not treated as independent confirmation; two further sources explicitly confirm the absence of guidance on this point elsewhere in the literature.

**Sources:** Portland Rose Society, "Guide to Pruning Roses" (Rich Baer).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Single direct source for the specific rule as stated; whether size and age/colour would generally agree on which cane to remove was not addressed.

### AF-34 — General Plant-Diagnostic Guidance Favours Physical Examination Over Photographs Alone

**Finding:** General plant-diagnostics literature (not specific to roses) consistently holds that a photograph alone is usually insufficient for a reliable diagnosis, and that physical examination of the plant, or a physical sample, is preferred where available.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low.

**Rationale:** Four independent institutional extension sources agree on the general principle, with no source found taking the opposite position; the finding is capped at Low because none of the four concerns roses or pruning judgements specifically — it is an extrapolation from general plant-diagnostic practice, per EAS §4.

**Sources:** Mississippi State University Extension; Iowa State University Extension and Outreach; Cornell Cooperative Extension; Colorado State University Extension.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No source breaks the principle down into AskPIP's specific categories; that breakdown is the ROC's own reasoning at AF-41, not sourced here (EAS §2.10).

### AF-35 — Sealing Larger Cuts Targets Cane-Boring Insects, Not Wound Healing

**Finding:** Where rose-specific sources recommend sealing larger cut ends, the reason given is protection against cane-boring insects entering the cane's exposed, soft pith — a different claim from, and not contradicted by, general arboriculture and plant-pathology research finding that wound dressings do not measurably improve wound closure or reduce decay in woody plants generally.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Four independent institutional/society sources converge on the same specific mechanism (soft pith attracts boring insects); the "sealing doesn't help healing" position is separately supported by a peer-reviewed literature review and answers a genuinely different question (healing/decay resistance, not insect entry), resolving the apparent contradiction at C4.

**Sources:** University of Arizona Cooperative Extension (Yavapai County); PlantTalk Colorado; ARS; Texas AgriLife El Paso.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No controlled trial testing sealed versus unsealed canes for borer incidence was found. Whether sealing is a good idea on balance (AF-30) remains contested; this finding explains the reasoning behind one side, not the overall answer.

### AF-36 — Old Wood Is Recognised Visually by Bark Colour and Texture

**Finding:** Where sources give a visual marker for "old" wood due for removal, they converge on gray colouring combined with a rough, scaly, crusty, or streaked bark texture; one source adds deteriorating or missing prickles as a further marker. No source ties "old" to a specific number of years with any stated reasoning or evidence.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate for the visual-marker description; no finding is made on an age-in-years threshold.

**Rationale:** Three independent sources (two institutional extension, one named rosarian via a rose-society newsletter) converge on the same visual description without contradiction.

**Sources:** Texas A&M University Extension (Douglas Welsh, via San Antonio Express-News); University of Arizona Cooperative Extension (Yavapai County); Houston Rose Society (Baxter Williams).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** All qualitative descriptions, not a test that can be applied with certainty; the age-in-years disagreement remains open (§5, C6).

### AF-37 — Grandifloras Are Generally Pruned Like Hybrid Teas, Adjusted for Height

**Finding:** Where sources address grandiflora roses as their own case, they treat grandiflora pruning as closer to hybrid tea practice than to floribunda practice — similar or slightly fewer canes than a hybrid tea, and in some sources a gentler cutback to preserve the class's characteristic height.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** The American Rose Society's own Consulting Rosarian Manual groups hybrid teas and grandifloras together explicitly; an independent university extension source and a specialist nursery corroborate, one giving distinct concrete figures.

**Sources:** American Rose Society Consulting Rosarian Manual; LSU AgCenter; University of Arizona Cooperative Extension (Yavapai County); Heirloom Roses.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Most general sources fold grandiflora in with hybrid tea silently rather than addressing it explicitly; the "gentler cutback" nuance rests on a single commercial nursery source.

### AF-38 — A Second RHS Source Corroborates Existing Figures; Southern Hemisphere Sources Dispute the Convention

**Finding, part 1 (corroboration).** A second, previously unused RHS page independently states the same quarter-inch distance, slant-away-from-the-bud direction, and outward-facing-bud guidance already recorded at AF-21 through AF-24. **Finding, part 2 (a genuine new conflict).** Two Southern Hemisphere sources explicitly disagree with the slant/bud-direction convention: the Rose Society of South Australia says the angle doesn't matter because a healthy cane seals over the cut regardless; Ludwig's Roses (South Africa) recommends an unsloped, 90-degree cut and says the top eye's direction is of minor importance.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Not assigned to a competing claim — a documented, unresolved conflict, carried at §5, C11.

**Rationale:** The dissenting view comes from two independent, credible sources from a different hemisphere and climate than the sources AF-23/AF-24 rest on, strengthening it as a genuine regional difference rather than a single outlier opinion; neither dissenting source gives a reason for its position.

**Sources:** RHS ("Rose Pruning: General Tips"); Rose Society of South Australia (Doug Gregory); Ludwig's Roses (South Africa).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** See §5, C11.

### AF-39 — A Badly Overgrown, Weak or Sickly Bush Is Sometimes Better Replaced Than Renovated

**Finding:** At least one source states that renovation is not always the right choice for a badly overgrown or declining shrub — a weak or sickly plant may be better replaced outright than pruned back hard.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low.

**Rationale:** One direct source (RHS, general shrub guidance, not rose-specific) states this plainly; a second general-shrub source makes a consistent point for a different plant type without independently confirming it for roses.

**Sources:** RHS, "Renovating Overgrown Shrubs"; Iowa State University Extension and Outreach (consistent, different plant type).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No source ties "weak or sickly" to anything a gardener, or a photo-based assessment, could reliably judge, or gives a concrete threshold for the decision.

### AF-40 — No More Than About a Third of a Bush's Growth Should Be Removed in One Session

**Finding:** General arboriculture and shrub-pruning guidance converges on a widely used rule of thumb: no more than about a third of a woody plant's growth should be removed in a single pruning session, for stated reasons of plant stress, depleted energy reserves, and uncontrolled regrowth at cut sites. Where staged, multi-year renovation is described, the same roughly-a-third-per-year figure recurs. This is not rose-specific, but directly answers the total-removal-limit and staging-threshold questions for which no rose-specific source gives any figure at all.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low (a well-attested general principle, not tested for roses specifically).

**Rationale:** Five independent institutional general-arboriculture sources converge on the same figure and reasoning, from a different professional literature than the rest of this dossier — itself a form of corroboration. One rose-specific source found earlier explicitly recommends hard, one-time renovation instead, suggesting the general rule is a conservative default rather than a universal rose-specific requirement.

**Sources:** NC State Extension, "General Pruning Techniques"; Colorado State University Extension (Adams County); City of Seattle, Department of Trees; UC Marin Master Gardeners; Homes & Gardens.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No source of either kind gives a threshold tied to a specific bush's own condition for choosing between a single hard cutback and a staged approach (see §8).

### AF-41 — Overall Shape and Crossing Canes Are Visually Assessable; Vigour and Internal Health Require Handling

**Finding:** Piecing together several partial, mostly rose-specific sources gives a workable, if not exhaustive, breakdown of what a whole-plant judgement can rely on a photograph for, versus what requires physically handling the bush. Visually assessable: overall outline and shape; crossing canes and canes running through the centre; relative cane vigour by colour, to a degree. Requiring physical handling: a bend/flex test for cane vigour; pith colour, visible only once a cut is made; and the base/bud-union area, best judged close up. A cane can look sound from the outside and still be compromised inside, so appearance is not a reliable substitute for a trial cut where health is in doubt.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low (a ROC-assembled synthesis of several partial, independently sourced observations, not one source stating the full breakdown).

**Rationale:** Every individual claim is independently sourced and, where rose-specific, directly on point; a general-woody-plant source independently supports the same close-versus-distant split without being asked to.

**Sources:** David Austin Roses (shrub-rose pruning guide); Orange County Rose Society; American Rose Society ("Basic Pruning Principles"); NC State Extension (general pruning-technique guide); a rose-grower forum discussion (Houzz, informal, cited only for a directly-on-point illustrative account).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No single source states this breakdown as a whole; it is assembled by the ROC from several partial statements (EAS §2.10 governs the synthesis, not the individually-cited claims). Not tested against Pip's actual photo-assessment capability. Whether the breakdown is exhaustive is not claimed.

## 5. Preserved, Unreconciled Conflicts

Eleven conflicts are recorded. Each is preserved as unresolved; where sources give a reason for a difference it is recorded, and where they do not, none is supplied.

**C1 — Final height and how hard to prune.** Sources give final heights from about 4 to about 42 inches, and fractions of one-third to two-thirds (AF-17). No figure for final height is adopted as a finding; AF-17/AF-18 record only the points of agreement in direction.

**C2 — Distance of the cut above the bud.** A quarter inch (eleven sources; AF-22) against one-quarter-to-one-half or one-half inch (a few sources). Now understood as two defensible resolutions of the same bud-death/dead-snag tradeoff, not an unexplained split (§4, AF-22).

**C3 — Direction of the slant relative to the bud.** Most sources say the cut slants away from the bud; a few say "in the same direction as" or "at the same angle as" the bud. Re-reading the original pages directly found no diagram or further explanation; whether this is one geometry described two ways, or two genuinely different techniques, remains unresolved (§4, AF-24).

**C4 — Sealing cut ends.** Several rose-specific sources advise sealing larger cuts against cane borers; a general woody-plant text advises against wound dressings generally. Resolved as answering different questions, not as a contradiction — see AF-35.

**C5 — How boldly to prune.** Sources range from "start conservatively" to "when in doubt, cut it out" to "underpruning is the most common cause of an unproductive rose." No source gives a reason for its stance or states its intended audience; this bears on the practical default for stems that are both weak and structurally useful (§6).

**C6 — What makes wood old enough to remove.** Figures range from "all three-year-old wood" (RHS) to "after about 5 or 6 years" (Virginia Cooperative Extension Master Gardener Training Handbook, found in Revision 3, which widens rather than narrows the existing disagreement). No source derives its figure from evidence; AF-36's visual markers are a separate, better-attested finding that does not resolve the age-in-years question.

**C7 — How many canes to retain.** Counts from three to eight, depending on source and sometimes on rose class or region (AF-15). No source gives an evidential basis for its count.

**C8 — Thickness at which hand pruners give way to loppers.** Three-quarters of an inch (two sources) against one-half inch (a general, non-rose text) (AF-26).

**C9 — Bypass or anvil pruners.** Several sources prefer bypass pruners; a couple name bypass without comparing types; one recommends anvil specifically for dead wood. No source states a reason (AF-25).

**C10 — Disinfecting tools.** Products and frequency both vary (70 percent alcohol, alcohol wipes, Lysol, methylated spirits, a bleach solution, or simply "clean, sterile tools"; before each plant, periodically, or only on diseased plants). No source states a reason for its particular choice (AF-28).

**C11 — Whether the pruning cut's angle and bud-facing direction matter at all.** The great majority of sources (RHS, most US extension sources, New Zealand sources) say to slant the cut and mind the bud direction (AF-23, AF-24). Two Southern Hemisphere sources found in Revision 3 explicitly disagree: the Rose Society of South Australia says the angle doesn't matter; Ludwig's Roses (South Africa) recommends an unsloped cut and says bud direction is of minor importance. Preserved as unresolved, per this project's practice of recording disagreement rather than picking a side; AF-23/AF-24 remain as assessed on the strength of the sources that support them (AF-38).

**Approved findings check.** The ROC did not re-research approved findings. A systematic comparison against the full, approved text of `ARC-BUSHROSE-DORMANCY-01` and `ARC-BUSHROSE-RECENTPLANT-01` was carried out in Revision 2; no conflict was found.

## 6. Approved Practical Defaults for Downstream Use

Where sources genuinely and permanently disagree — ordinary variation among practitioners, not a research gap further search would close — the following working defaults are approved for operational use, with the underlying variation preserved at §5 rather than hidden:

- **Final height (C1):** no single figure is right; use the class-based mid-range already on record (AF-17, AF-18) — roughly one-third to one-half of the previous season's growth, adjusted lower for hybrid teas and higher for floribundas — rather than a fixed number.
- **Cut distance above the bud (C2):** about a quarter of an inch (AF-22), the majority convention; up to half an inch is an acceptable alternative for a gardener who wants to minimise the risk of a dead snag rather than bud death.
- **Slant direction (C3):** slant away from the bud, roughly 45 degrees (AF-23, AF-24), the majority wording; the minority "same direction as the bud" wording is treated as the same technique described differently, not a different instruction, since no source shows otherwise.
- **Sealing cut ends (C4):** seal larger cuts (roughly pencil-thickness or more) with glue, nail polish or pruning sealer as a precaution against cane-boring insects (AF-35); thinner cuts need not be sealed.
- **Age of wood (C6):** use the visual markers (AF-36) as the primary test; where a number is wanted, treat canes older than about three years as candidates for removal (RHS's figure), recognising this is a convention, not a measured fact.
- **Number of canes retained (C7):** four to six strong canes is a reasonable working default within the three-to-eight range the sources give (AF-15).
- **Tool-thickness threshold (C8):** hand pruners up to about three-quarters of an inch; loppers or a saw above that (AF-26).
- **Pruner type (C9):** bypass (scissor-type) pruners are the default; anvil pruners are acceptable for clearing dead wood only (AF-25).
- **Disinfecting tools (C10):** wipe blades with 70 percent alcohol between plants as a simple, well-supported default (AF-28); this is a precaution, not a strict requirement given the mixed evidence on how often it matters.
- **Cut angle and bud direction (C11):** follow the majority convention (45-degree slant, outward-facing bud) as the default; the Southern Hemisphere sources that treat this as unimportant are noted for completeness but do not change the default guidance.
- **Category priority when a stem is both weak and structurally useful:** no source resolves this (§7, U-1). The approved working default is to lean conservative and retain the stem for reassessment at the next pruning, consistent with the "start conservatively" convention (C5) and with AF-9's preference for strong, framework-carrying canes. This is the ROC's own recommendation, not a sourced finding (EAS §2.10), and remains open to a different Founder direction at any time.
- **Order of removal versus framework selection:** remove the unwanted categories first (AF-12, AF-13), then assess what remains for the framework (AF-16). The two framings sources use — "remove these categories" and "choose the canes to keep" — are two stages of one process applied in sequence, not competing methods (ROC synthesis, EAS §2.10).
- **Total removal limit and staging (U-5, U-7):** no more than about a third of a bush's growth in one session; stage heavier renovation over roughly two to three years at the same rate (AF-40, reinforcing AF-20).
