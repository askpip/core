## 1. Purpose and Scope

This ARC is the authoritative record of the Founder-approved information produced by the research commission `BUSHROSE-TIMING-UK`:

- **Q1** In which months is the main pruning of an established Hybrid Tea or Floribunda done in each climate band, and what local signal is it tied to?
- **Q2** When does spring feeding start, and when is the last feed, in each band?
- **Q3** When do deadheading and summer pruning stop before winter, in each band?
- **Q4** In which months are bare-root roses planted, potted roses planted, and established roses moved, in each band?
- **Q5** When are fallen leaves and prunings cleared; where a winter spray is described, when; where winter protection is described, when it goes on and comes off?
- **Q6** Do UK sources give one national window per task, or different windows for colder and milder areas, and how are those areas described?
- **Q7** Could a beginner be placed in the right band from the region or GPS location already recorded, or would they need to be asked?
- **Q8** How do sources handle a late or early season, or a garden near the edge of a band?

**Scope.** Established Hybrid Tea, Floribunda and (provisionally) Grandiflora bush roses, plus planting new roses and moving established ones for Q4. United Kingdom only. Months from one band are not applied to another. Product names, brands and rates are not collected. Every online source is recorded with its web address and access date (ROC Operations Manual §5.5).

It is the source from which KIT wrote what Pip says about which months to do each rose job in the United Kingdom.

#### Main pruning (Q1)

**AF-1 (established; national). As approved:** The main pruning of roses is done in late winter or early spring; the months named are February and March. Late winter, February or March (the RHS); February to March (one garden company); a frost-free period from mid-February to mid-March, or around March (one rose grower); February and March (a second rose grower, on a 2017 page); a March task (the Met Office). **High** (as submitted: National: UK sources place the main pruning of established bush roses in late winter to early spring, most commonly February to March; High).

*Evidence as recorded:* UK sources place the main pruning of established bush roses in late winter to early spring, most commonly February to March. RHS-GT: "Late winter (February or March) is often a good time for pruning roses". PB-WF: "The best time to prune your roses is during February and March." LTG-PR: "stick to February to March, the most common pruning time for roses." HARK-PT: "a frost-free period from mid-February to mid-March". HARK-EG: "Prune fully around March". MO-MAR: "Pruning roses is a classic March task". DA-BASICS: "In late winter or early spring remove anything that is obviously dead, diseased, or rubbing."

*Build Check:* 'Most commonly February to March' is removed: it describes the sources, and only LTG-PR says it ('the most common pruning time for roses'). February and March: RHS-GT ('often a good time', of roses in general), RHS-MB (modern bush roses: late winter, mid-February in the south and March in northern and colder areas), LTG-PR, HARK-PT (established bush roses) and PB-WF. PB-WF dates from 2017; the same grower's 2025 and 2026 pages say January and February (AF-4, C-1), so Peter Beales is on both sides. March only: HARK-EG ('around March') and MO-MAR, which tells readers to seek the latest RHS advice and so is not an independent voice. Season only: HARK-EG ('late winter or early spring', of a bush rose) and DA-BASICS, whose sentence is about removing dead, diseased or rubbing wood. High is kept: the RHS, one garden company and one rose grower independently name February and March. Other windows are in AF-4, AF-5 and AF-6 (C-1).

**AF-2 (supported interpretation; south or milder band). As approved:** In the south, the main pruning is done in mid-February: 'usually in mid-February in the south' (the RHS); 'safest' in mid-February, before new growth begins (one garden company). **Moderate** (as submitted: South or milder band: main pruning is usually around mid-February; Moderate).

*Evidence as recorded:* In the south, main pruning is usually around mid-February. RHS-MB: "usually in mid-February in the south". LTG-PR: "In the south you are safest to prune roses in mid-February before new growth begins on rose plants."

*Build Check:* Neither page says where the south ends, and neither sentence says 'milder'. A location cannot tell whether a garden is in 'the south', so the condition is said in words to everyone in the UK. One rose nursery gives November and December for 'Midlands & South counties' (AF-5, C-9).

**AF-3 (supported interpretation; north or colder band). As approved:** In northern and colder areas, the main pruning waits until March (the RHS); one garden company says mid to late March for 'the north and other colder areas of the UK'. **Moderate** (as submitted: North or colder band: main pruning waits until March, or mid to late March; Moderate).

*Evidence as recorded:* In the north and colder areas, main pruning waits until March, or mid to late March. RHS-MB: "but in northern and colder areas wait until March." LTG-PR: "in the north and other colder areas of the UK we would recommend waiting until mid to late March".

*Build Check:* 'Mid to late March' is LTG-PR alone; RHS-MB says March. Neither page says where the north begins or what counts as colder, so the condition is said in words to everyone in the UK. One rose nursery gives January to late February for the north of England and Scotland (AF-5).

**AF-4 (supported interpretation; national). As approved:** Two rose growers put the main pruning in January and February: 'for most gardens that means January or February' (one); 'January and February are the best months' for shrubs, hybrid teas and other roses, with February 'the ideal month' for hybrid teas (the other). **Moderate** (as submitted: National: several commercial UK growers put the main pruning window earlier, in January and February; Moderate).

*Evidence as recorded:* Several commercial UK growers put the main window earlier, in January and February. DA-PR: "Winter pruning is best completed while roses are dormant, and for most gardens that means January or February." PB-FEB: "January and February are the best months of the year to prune your shrubs, hybrid teas, and any other roses". PB-HT: "February is the ideal month to prune hybrid tea roses" and "January – February: Main pruning period for hybrid tea and climbing roses before new growth begins." (C-1.)

*Build Check:* 'Several' is corrected to two: David Austin (DA-PR) and Peter Beales (PB-FEB, PB-HT, PB-JAN). DA-PR covers shrub, climbing, standard and potted roses and does not name Hybrid Teas or Floribundas. Peter Beales's 2017 page says February and March (PB-WF, AF-1), so its pages differ by year. PB-HT ties the window to 'before new growth begins'. Both growers are commercial. Moderate is kept: two independent growers.

**AF-5 (dissenting opinion, single source; two bands). As approved:** One rose nursery says the main pruning should be completed in November or December in the Midlands and south counties, and in January to late February in the north of England and Scotland. It says that delaying the main pruning to March and beyond delays flowering by up to six weeks, and gives no evidence for that. **Very Low** (as submitted: Two bands: one commercial nursery prunes in November and December in the Midlands and south, and January to late February in the north of England and Scotland; Very Low).

*Evidence as recorded:* STYLE: pruning "should be completed every year during November/December for Midlands & South counties, January/Late February for North of England and Scotland", and says delaying to March "will simply delay your roses from flowering in the following summer by up to 6 weeks!" It gives no evidence for the delay (C-1, C-9).

*Build Check:* One commercial nursery (STYLE), on one page. It contradicts the RHS (AF-2, AF-3) and `ARC-BUSHROSE-DORMANCY-01` AF-2 (C-1, C-9). The page names its places: 'Midlands & South counties' and 'North of England and Scotland'. It does not say where the south counties end, and does not mention Wales or Northern Ireland. Used only as one side of a disagreement (rule 2), in the places the page names, because the RHS and a second source already speak of the south and of northern areas, so the nursery is a dissent there and not the only local source (wording check): shown for the Midlands and the south of England (the East Midlands, West Midlands, East of England, London, South East and South West, reading 'South counties' as the English counties south of the Midlands) and for the north of England and Scotland (North East, North West, Yorkshire and the Humber, and Scotland). It is not joined to the statement for the whole country, so that one nursery's months are shown only where it names. The flowering-delay claim is not used in Pip (R-2); one rose grower says the opposite of March pruning (AF-8, C-12). That 'South counties' takes in the East of England, and that 'January/Late February' is a span, are readings of the page. On record only, at a Founder's direction (8 October 2026): information from one rose nursery that disagrees with the Royal Horticultural Society is not shown to gardeners.

**AF-6 (expert opinion, single source; national). As approved:** One garden company says the best time to prune is a frost-free day between November and March. **Low** (as submitted: National: one commercial source gives a wider window, any frost-free day from November to March; Low).

*Evidence as recorded:* TM: "the best time to prune is a frost-free day between November and March."

*Build Check:* TM alone. The sentence begins 'Apart from ramblers and some shrub roses', which the page prunes after flowering in summer. It names no area. Used only as one side of C-1.

**AF-7 (supported interpretation; national). As approved:** Sources tie the main pruning to a signal or condition as well as to months: a frost-free day or period (three companies); before new growth begins, before leaf buds start to swell, or before fresh buds start to open (three companies); while the rose is dormant (one rose grower); when growth is just resuming (the RHS). **Moderate** (as submitted: National: sources tie pruning timing to local signals (growth resuming, buds, dormancy, a frost-free day) as well as to months; Moderate).

*Evidence as recorded:* Sources tie pruning timing to local signals as well as months. RHS-MB: roses "pruned during late winter when growth is just resuming". LTG-PR: pruned "before leaf buds are starting to swell, but this normally depends on your climate and where you are." HARK-EG: "prune a bush rose before fresh buds start to open". DA-PR: "while roses are dormant". HARK-PT, TM and STYLE: a frost-free period or day. The signals named differ (C-2).

*Build Check:* Frost-free: HARK-PT ('a frost-free period'), TM and STYLE ('a frost-free day'). Before growth or buds: LTG-PR ('before leaf buds are starting to swell'; 'before new growth begins', said of the south), PB-HT ('before new growth begins') and HARK-EG ('before fresh buds start to open', of a bush rose). Dormant: DA-PR. Growth just resuming: RHS-MB alone. Only LTG-PR gives more than one signal, and whether 'growth just resuming' and 'before buds swell' are the same moment is not stated (C-2, R-5d). The approved Suitability Gate uses bud swell (`ARC-BUSHROSE-DORMANCY-01` AF-1). LTG-PR adds that the timing 'normally depends on your climate and where you are'.

**AF-8 (expert opinion, limited; national). As approved:** One rose grower says pruning that slips into March, or after signs of new growth, is still worth doing. **Low** (as submitted: National: one commercial grower says pruning that slips into March, or after new growth shows, is still worth doing; Low).

*Evidence as recorded:* DA-PR says late pruning is still worthwhile: "If you notice signs of new growth and you still haven't pruned, don't worry." and "if March arrives and you still haven't done it, you haven't missed your chance."

*Build Check:* DA-PR alone, a page covering shrub, climbing, standard and potted roses. One rose nursery says the opposite: that pruning in March or later delays flowering by up to six weeks (AF-5, C-12).

**AF-9 (expert opinion, limited; national). As approved:** Two rose growers describe trimming roses in autumn against winter wind. One gives a light prune of established roses in September on one page, and on others a trim of tall hybrid teas or taller stems in October or in October to November; it says October is too early for a hard prune. The other says roses can be trimmed back after they finish flowering in November, to stop wind-rock. **Low** (as submitted: National: two commercial growers describe a light autumn height reduction of tall bush roses against winter wind, September to November, not a hard prune; Low).

*Evidence as recorded:* Two growers describe a light autumn height reduction of tall bush roses against winter wind. PB-HT: "Autumn (October – November): Light prune tall hybrid tea roses to prevent wind damage." PB-CAL (September): "removing approximately 30cm (12 inches) from the top of established plants". ## 5. Preserved, Unreconciled Points of Variation

These are recorded as they stand. They are not resolved. C-1 to C-9 are reproduced from `FRD-BUSHROSE-TIMING-UK-01` §6 as first written; where a finding they cite was corrected, §4 governs. C-10 to C-15 were found at the Build Check.

#### 5.1 Conflicts between sources

**C-1 — Which months for the main pruning (AF-1, AF-4, AF-5, AF-6).** February to March: RHS-GT "Late winter (February or March)"; PB-WF "during February and March"; LTG-PR "stick to February to March"; HARK-PT "mid-February to mid-March"; HARK-EG "around March"; MO-MAR "a classic March task". January to February: DA-PR "for most gardens that means January or February"; PB-FEB "January and February are the best months"; PB-HT "February is the ideal month" and "January – February: Main pruning period". November to March: TM. November and December in the south: STYLE. Peter Beales gives both February–March (2017) and January–February (2025, 2026). All windows overlap in February. Unresolved.

**C-2 — The local signal for pruning (AF-7; `ARC-BUSHROSE-DORMANCY-01` AF-1).** RHS-MB: "when growth is just resuming". LTG-PR: "before leaf buds are starting to swell". HARK-EG: "before fresh buds start to open". DA-PR: "while roses are dormant". HARK-PT, TM and STYLE: a frost-free day or period. The approved Suitability Gate uses bud swell; LTG-PR places pruning before buds swell. Whether these describe the same moment is not stated. Unresolved; R-5d.

**C-3 — The last feed (AF-11, AF-12).** RHS-GG: established roses fed "in March/April" and again "in mid-summer after the first flush"; containers "from mid-spring to late summer". HARK-EG and LTG-GG: twice, the second in mid-summer. PB-FEED and PB-CAL: liquid feeds continue, last "before the end of August". Unresolved.

**C-4 — Bare-root planting in mid-winter (AF-17, AF-18).** RHS-PL: "Avoid planting in the middle of winter when the ground is frozen." PB-DEC: "December is one of the best months of the year to plant your bare root roses"; PB-JAN plants in January and stores roses if the ground is frozen. Both make frozen ground the limit; they differ on whether mid-winter is to be avoided. Unresolved.

**C-5 — Moving window (AF-21).** RHS-MOVE (general shrubs): "late October to mid-March". DA-MOVE: "between November and February". PB-CAL: November. PB-FEB: "February is the time to do it." All are within dormancy. Unresolved.

**C-6 — Stopping deadheading (AF-14; `ARC-BUSHROSE-BASICCARE-01` F5; `ARC-BUSHROSE-POSTBUDBREAK-01` AF-15).** UK sources give no stop month for repeat-flowering bush roses; RHS-DH says "whatever the time of year"; PB-OCT is "still deadheading" in October. The approved F5 records a common stop in early-to-mid autumn and AF-15 discourages late-season deadheading, both from US sources. Unresolved; Founder decision point 8.

**C-7 — Timing of autumn height reduction (AF-9).** PB-CAL: September. PB-HT: "Autumn (October – November)". HARK-PT: after flowering "in November". Unresolved.

**C-8 — Winter protection by mounding (AF-25, AF-26; `ARC-BUSHROSE-BASICCARE-01` F9).** F9 (US sources) mounds soil 10–12 inches over the base after hard frosts. No UK source located describes this; PB-NOV mulches only where roots are exposed. Different climates. Unresolved.

**C-9 — Southern pruning in early winter (AF-5; `ARC-BUSHROSE-DORMANCY-01` AF-2).** STYLE prunes in November and December in the Midlands and south. The approved AF-2 places pruning after the coldest part of winter, and RHS-MB and LTG-PR give mid-February for the south. STYLE is a single commercial source. Preserved as unresolved.

**C-10 — How much to take off in the autumn trim (AF-9).** Peter Beales's own pages differ. PB-CAL: "removing approximately 30cm (12 inches) from the top of established plants". PB-HT: a light prune in autumn, reducing height by about 50%. PB-OCT: "not to do anything too drastic". HARK-PT gives no amount. Unresolved; Pip gives no amount.

**C-11 — The month of the first feed (AF-10).** RHS-GG: "in March/April before flowering". DA-FEED: "late March or early April, just before new growth begins". PB-FEED: "ideally in March". PB-FEB, the same grower: "Towards the end of February or in early March". LTG-GG: "in early spring, or after pruning", no month. Unresolved; the range is late February to April.

**C-12 — Whether pruning in March is too late (AF-5, AF-8, AF-1).** STYLE: delaying the main pruning "to March and beyond" will "delay your roses from flowering in the following summer by up to 6 weeks". DA-PR: "if March arrives and you still haven't done it, you haven't missed your chance." RHS-GT, LTG-PR, HARK-PT and PB-WF name March as a month for pruning. Unresolved; no trial was located (R-2), and neither single-source claim is used.

**C-13 — When to stop deadheading a rose grown for hips (AF-15).** RHS-GG: stop "in mid-summer to give the hips time to form". DA-DH: "Do not deadhead roses renowned for their autumn fruit after the second flush." Unresolved; Pip gives both.

**C-14 — Hard pruning in autumn (AF-16, AF-9, AF-5, AF-6).** One rose grower says not to prune hard in autumn and that October is too early for a hard prune (PB-CAL, PB-WF, PB-OCT; AF-16, AF-9). One nursery puts the main pruning in November and December for the Midlands and south (STYLE; AF-5), and one garden company's window for pruning starts in November (TM; AF-6). Unresolved. Pip leads with February or March and says who differs.

**C-15 — When the north of England and Scotland prune (AF-3, AF-5).** RHS-MB: "in northern and colder areas wait until March". LTG-PR: mid to late March for "the north and other colder areas of the UK". STYLE: "January/Late February for North of England and Scotland". C-9 as first written covers only the south. Unresolved. Pip leads with March and says who differs.

### 8.1 Source characteristics that limit independence or weight

- **Nine organisations** stand behind the 38 pages: the RHS (10 pages), the Met Office (4), David Austin Roses (8), Peter Beales Roses (9), Harkness Roses (2), Love the Garden (2), Style Roses, Thompson & Morgan and Eutrema. Pages from one organisation count as one voice.
- **Seven of the nine organisations are commercial.** Only the RHS and the Met Office are non-commercial. No rose society, botanic garden or broadcaster page with UK months could be opened (§8.2).
- **RHS-FROST, RHS-MICRO, MO-FROST, MO-SPRING, MO-MAR and MO-FEB** concern frost and climate generally, not roses; they bear on how areas are described (Q6 to Q8), not on rose task months. **RHS-MOVE** is general shrub guidance that names *Rosa*. **RHS-DH** is general deadheading guidance that names roses.
- **DA-PR** covers shrub, climbing, standard and potted roses rather than Hybrid Teas and Floribundas by name. David Austin pages are written for English roses.
- **Peter Beales** pages span 2017 to 2026 and do not all give the same pruning months (C-1).
- **STYLE** is a single nursery whose advice departs from every other source located, and its claim that March pruning delays flowering by up to six weeks is stated without evidence.
- **EUTREMA** sells garden products, including the product it describes.
