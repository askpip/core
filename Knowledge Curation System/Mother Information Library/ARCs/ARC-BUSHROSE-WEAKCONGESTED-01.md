## 1. Purpose and Scope

This ARC is the approved, governed record of what AskPIP knows about recognising and pruning weak growth and congested growth on an established, dormant bush rose. It is compiled from `FRD-BUSHROSE-WEAKCONGESTED-02` per Mother Information Library Standard v0.05 and ROC Operations Manual §12.4, reproducing each approved Assessed Finding faithfully, without new interpretation.

**Scope carried over from the FRD:** the established bush rose defined in Architecture §5.1 and `FRD-BUSHROSE-SCOPE-01`; dormant-season pruning; assisted photo interpretation with physical confirmation; recognition and pruning choices for weak growth and congested growth. Excluded: damaged growth, crossing/rubbing stems and inward-growing stems (governed by their own ARCs); general cut mechanics (governed by `ARC-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01`).

Weak-growth findings and congestion findings are kept distinguishable throughout this ARC, even though Architecture §5.2 defines them as one supported observation. With this ARC, all six MVP pruning observations (Architecture §5.2) have an approved ARC.

## 4. Approved Findings — Full Content

Reproduced faithfully from `FRD-BUSHROSE-WEAKCONGESTED-02` §8, without new interpretation, per ROC Operations Manual §12.4.

### AF-1 — Weak growth is an individual-cane issue; congestion is a whole-bush issue

**Question:** Q1
**Assessed Finding:** Sources consistently frame "weak" growth as a property of a single cane (its thickness/vigour) and "congestion" as a property of the whole bush (overall cane density), even when both are mentioned in the same source or the same pruning sequence.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** RHS: sequences "cut out dead, diseased, rubbing and crossing stems" first, then, conditionally, "if the bush is crowded cut out some old shoots completely to keep the centre open" — treating crowding as a separate, whole-plant-level judgment. Clemson/UF/IFAS/Texas A&M (shared text family): "any cane thinner than a pencil should be removed" is a per-cane test, stated separately from centre-opening instructions on the same pages.

**Strengths.** The distinction holds consistently across every source that addresses both conditions, even those that use shared or near-identical phrasing for the weak-cane rule specifically.

**Limitations.** No source states the distinction as an explicit definitional sentence; it is observed through how sources structure their instructions, not asserted directly.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None of substance for the distinction itself.

---

### AF-2 — Causes are thinly evidenced, but congestion plausibly causes weak growth

**Question:** Q1
**Assessed Finding:** Few sources state an explicit cause for either condition. Where causal language exists, congestion is described as plausibly producing weak growth — a crowded base blocking light leads to weak, unproductive tip growth — rather than the two merely happening to co-occur.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low**

**Evidence.** Chicago Botanic Garden: "An overgrown rose is thick and congested at ground level, which results in lots of fuzzy tip growth instead of full, lush canes covered in buds" — a stated cause-and-effect chain. Ludwig's Roses: unpruned interior canes are "not nourish[ed]," and "slowly dr[y] out completely and die" — a self-shading mechanism. Rose Society of Victoria: old stems "lost their vigour, evident by their production of weak spindly growths" — an age-based, not congestion-based, cause.

**Strengths.** The Chicago Botanic Garden finding is a genuine, useful causal link for the app's own bundling logic (why these two conditions are grouped as one observation in the Architecture).

**Limitations.** Rests on a single source for the congestion-causes-weakness link specifically; most sources jump straight from description to removal instruction without stating a cause at all.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether congestion is the primary cause of weak growth, one of several causes, or simply correlated with it was not addressed by any source in a systematic way.

---

### AF-3 — The boundary with dead/damaged wood is established only by listing, not by a dedicated test

**Question:** Q1
**Assessed Finding:** Sources consistently place "weak" alongside "dead" and "damaged" in the same removal instruction, implying it is a distinct, living category, but no source gives an explicit diagnostic test (comparable to the pith-colour test already approved for dead and damaged wood) tied specifically to confirming a cane is "weak" rather than dead.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** RHS: "Remove all dead and damaged wood as soon as it is seen. Also remove weak and crossing branches during routine pruning" — separated by timing (urgent versus routine), implying weak wood is alive and less time-critical. Clemson/UF/IFAS/Texas A&M: "Remove all dead, damaged or weak stems" — three items, implying three distinct states.

**Strengths.** Confirms and extends `FRD-BUSHROSE-DAMAGEDGROWTH-02`'s AF-3 (now `ARC-BUSHROSE-DAMAGEDGROWTH-01`'s AF-3), using an independent and larger set of sources.

**Limitations.** The distinction is inferred from sentence structure, never stated as an explicit rule.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None of substance.

---

### AF-4 — Congestion and crossing/rubbing stems are sequenced as two separate pruning steps

**Question:** Q1
**Assessed Finding:** Sources that address both conditions treat crossing/rubbing (contact between two specific stems) and congestion (overall bush density) as two separate steps in the pruning sequence, with congestion thinning typically conditional on the bush actually being crowded, rather than always performed.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** RHS: crossing/rubbing removal is unconditional ("cut out... rubbing and crossing stems"); congestion thinning is conditional ("if the bush is crowded cut out some old shoots"). Portland Rose Society gives crossing and "crowded close to each other" as two separate bullet instructions, the latter not requiring actual contact between canes.

**Strengths.** Two independent sources apply the same sequencing logic, supporting keeping the two observations conceptually distinct in Pip's own model, consistent with the Architecture's own separate listing of "crossing or rubbing stems" from "weak or congested growth."

**Limitations.** Not every source separates them this cleanly; Rose Society of Victoria's language blends the two concepts somewhat more than RHS or Portland's does.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None of substance.

---

### AF-5 — The "pencil thickness" threshold is near-universal in concept, disputed in exact measurement

**Question:** Q2
**Assessed Finding:** "Pencil thickness" is the standard, widely-repeated qualitative threshold below which a cane is considered weak and removed. Where sources translate this into an actual measurement, they disagree by nearly a factor of two.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** Clemson, UF/IFAS, Texas A&M, Illinois Extension, American Rose Society, Rose Society of Victoria: all use "pencil" as the threshold, without a number. Kansas City Rose Society: "diameter less than that of a lead pencil (1/4-inch)." Rose Society of NSW: "no less than 10mm." Orange County Rose Society supports the rule: it asks, rhetorically, "Why, you may ask, should you consider leaving stems smaller than a pencil?" and answers against leaving them ("Pencil-thick stems produce matchstick-thick stems..."). *[Corrected at v1.2, 29 September 2026. This previously read as questioning the rule's strictness, a misreading found during the Source Register check.]*

**Strengths.** Very broad agreement on the qualitative concept across many independent, different-region sources.

**Limitations.** The only two sources giving an actual number disagree substantially (see §5, this ARC). RHS gives no threshold at all for bush roses.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether the disagreement reflects a real difference in practice or just different reference pencils was not addressed by any source.

### AF-6 — Other weak-growth visual cues

**Question:** Q2
**Assessed Finding:** Beyond thickness, sources describe weak growth as twiggy or unproductive, associated with poor or absent bloom production, and producing "fuzzy," thin tip growth rather than full, lush canes.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** American Rose Society: "remove all weak or twiggy growth on main canes that is not capable of sustaining a reasonable thickness of stem," and "Hybrid tea blooms need a stem about pencil thickness or greater for support" (implying sub-threshold canes can't properly support blooms). Chicago Botanic Garden: "lots of fuzzy tip growth instead of full, lush canes covered in buds."

**Strengths.** Multiple independent sources agree on the general "thin, unproductive, twiggy" visual profile.

**Limitations.** No source describes weak canes as visually arching or flopping, a plausible additional cue that was searched for directly and not found.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None of substance beyond the arching/flopping gap noted above.

---

### AF-7 — Congestion's target retained-cane count genuinely varies by source and region

**Question:** Q2
**Assessed Finding:** Sources give a specific numeric target for how many main canes an established bush rose should retain after thinning, but the figure varies meaningfully across sources: from "one to four" (South Africa) to "four to eight" (several US sources), with "three to five" the most commonly repeated figure.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** Ludwig's Roses (South Africa): "The maximum number of stems or branches to remain is four and the minimum one." American Rose Society, Texas A&M, Clemson (shared phrasing): "three to five." Oregon State: "three to six." Kansas City Rose Society and NC State: "three to seven." UC ANR: "four to seven." University of Georgia: "five or six." Purdue: "four to eight." No source uses "odd number of canes" as a stated rule (searched directly, not found).

**Strengths.** Many independent sources give a specific figure, allowing the range and its regional pattern to be documented with confidence, even though no single figure is universal.

**Limitations.** The shared-text family (ARS/Texas A&M/Clemson) all give "three to five" using near-identical wording, so that specific figure has less independent weight than the range as a whole; other, independently-worded sources corroborate a broadly similar range with different numbers.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether the regional differences reflect real horticultural differences (climate, cultivar) or just differing convention was not addressed by any source.

---

### AF-8 — Congestion's non-numeric indicators are well corroborated; terminology should stay precise

**Question:** Q2
**Assessed Finding:** Beyond a numeric cane count, sources describe congestion as an explicit "if the bush is crowded" trigger condition, and describe the pruning goal as an open, spoke-like or vase-shaped architecture. "Twiggy," by contrast, is used by every source that uses it at all to describe weak individual-cane growth, never whole-bush congestion.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** RHS: "If the bush is crowded cut out some old shoots completely to keep the centre open." American Rose Society: canes should "emanate from the bud union like spokes of a wheel with no cross-overs or interferences." Rose Society of South Australia: "canes in a circle around an open centre." Chicago Botanic Garden: "thick and congested at ground level."

**Strengths.** Multiple independent sources agree on both the trigger condition and the target shape.

**Limitations.** None of substance.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None; the negative finding on "twiggy centre" is itself a useful precision for Pip's own wording, preventing a term used loosely elsewhere from being misapplied to this observation.

### AF-9 — No rose-specific source addresses judging cane thickness from a photo without a size reference

**Question:** Q3
**Assessed Finding:** No rose-specific source directly states whether cane thickness can be judged reliably from a photograph without an object of known size in the frame. Drawing on general extension diagnostic-photo guidance (which consistently recommends including a coin, pencil or ruler when submitting a photo for size-dependent diagnosis) and academic photogrammetry literature (showing that size-from-image-alone remains a hard problem even for specialised, purpose-built methods), the ROC's own synthesis is that a size reference materially improves confidence in a thickness judgment from a photo.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low** (a ROC synthesis of general, non-rose-specific evidence, extrapolated per EAS §2.10)

**Evidence.** Iowa State Extension: "One of the common recommendations when sending digital images for diagnosis... is to include a 'size reference' in the photo." Clemson Extension: "Include an object next to insects, small animals, and plant parts to provide some estimate of size." Alabama Extension: "Item for scale (hand, coin, dollar bill, measuring tape, ruler, etc.)." Academic photogrammetry literature on stem-diameter measurement notes that photography-only methods "achieve errors around 2-3 cm" even under near-ideal conditions, and purpose-built systems still rely on physical contact sensors rather than image data alone.

**Strengths.** Convergent, independent guidance across four extension services on the general photo-diagnosis practice, plus academic confirmation of the underlying difficulty.

**Limitations.** No source states this specifically for rose canes or for the pencil-thickness threshold; it is assembled from adjacent, general guidance. Mississippi State Extension's otherwise-similar photo guidance does not mention a size reference, showing the practice, while common, is not universal.

**Remaining uncertainty.** How much this matters in practice for a rose cane, viewed at typical close-up photo distance, was not addressed by any source.

### AF-10 — Congestion judgments are somewhat more distance-robust than size judgments, but occlusion limits any single photo

**Question:** Q3
**Assessed Finding:** One controlled academic study of digitised tree-crown images found density measurements markedly more robust to photographic distance than size/volume measurements, lending some support to the idea that congestion (a "big picture" judgment) is more assessable from a photo than fine cane-thickness measurement is. However, separate research on occlusion in plant imagery shows that even a good whole-plant photo, being single-angle, cannot rule out hidden crowding on the far or interior side of a bush.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low** (assembled from general academic and extension sources, not stated directly by any single source for this observation)

**Evidence.** Patterson et al. (2011), *Arboriculture & Urban Forestry* 37(4):173: "Crown density showed most repeatability errors of <1%," markedly better than size/volume measurements at greater distances in the same study. Arevalo-Ramirez et al. (2024): image-analysis models "fail when occluded trees or homogeneous crowns are hard to distinguish." Extension photo-diagnosis guidance (Iowa State, Clemson, Alabama) consistently recommends a whole-plant photo for overall assessment and close-ups for specific symptoms, without stating a rationale specific to density judgments.

**Strengths.** The Patterson et al. study is a genuine, if indirect, academic data point in the direction the app's design already assumes.

**Limitations.** The Patterson study concerns controlled digitisation of tree-crown images with human-placed reference lines, not casual viewing of a rose photo; its relevance is by analogy, not direct application.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether a typical gardener's single rose photo achieves anything close to the accuracy of the controlled study was not addressed by any source.

### AF-11 — No source addresses mistaking new vigorous growth for weak growth; a colour-based differentiator exists as a proxy

**Question:** Q4
**Assessed Finding:** No source explicitly warns that a beginner might mistake a vigorous, healthy new basal cane (naturally thin because it is young) for a genuinely weak, declining cane. The closest available differentiator found is not thickness at all, but thorn and bark colour by cane age.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low**

**Evidence.** Ludwig's Roses: "The colour of the thorns helps distinguish between this season's stems and older wood. Thorns turn grey on old wood, but are usually still brown or reddish on this season's stems," and "The bark, too, is grey and thick on older wood." A separate, lower-authority source cataloguing ten causes of thin canes (light, water, nutrients, technique, pests, disease, overcrowding, root health, weather/genetics) did not include "newly emerged, not yet thickened" among them — a further sign this specific confusion isn't addressed in the literature.

**Strengths.** The Ludwig's Roses colour/bark differentiator is a genuine, usable, photo-visible cue, even though it wasn't offered by its source for this specific purpose.

**Limitations.** No source directly addresses the confusion this question asks about; the differentiator is repurposed from an adjacent context (distinguishing this season's growth from older wood generally, for pruning-technique reasons, not specifically to avoid misjudging vigour).

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether colour/bark texture reliably prevents this specific confusion in practice was not tested by any source.

### AF-12 — Weak-growth removal is a binary, whole-cane rule

**Question:** Q5
**Assessed Finding:** Once a cane is judged below the recognition threshold, sources instruct removing it entirely, at its base or point of origin — not shortening it. This matches the "always remove fully" pattern already found for crossing/rubbing and inward-growing stems, and is explicitly unlike the severity-dependent trim-or-remove rule found for damaged growth.
**Evidence Confidence Level: High**

**Evidence.** Rose Society of Victoria: weak stems "must be removed at the base of the plant just above the bud union." Rose Society of South Australia: "Remove at their origins any growths that are less than a pencil thickness." American Rose Society, Illinois Extension, UC ANR, Kansas City Rose Society all independently instruct full removal once a cane is judged too thin, with no source describing a partial shortening for a weak cane.

**Strengths.** Broad, independent corroboration across UK, US, Australian and South African publisher types, unanimous in direction — no source describes trimming a weak cane back to a stronger point and leaving a stub.

**Limitations.** None of substance.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None; this is one of the two best-attested findings in this ARC, and is directly useful for keeping Pip's decision logic consistent with the pattern already built for crossing/rubbing and inward-growing stems.

### AF-13 — Congestion thinning is also whole-cane, tied to the "open, airy centre" goal

**Question:** Q5
**Assessed Finding:** Congestion is addressed by removing whole canes (never partially shortening a cane for density reasons), selected first by vigour/thickness, then by even spacing around the plant and outward-facing, non-central growth direction, generally favouring younger basal canes over older, less productive wood — explicitly tied to the "open, airy centre" goal already established across this project's other ARCs.
**Evidence Confidence Level: High**

**Evidence.** American Rose Society: "canes emanate from the bud union like spokes of a wheel with no cross-overs or interferences." Texas A&M: "Prune the bush to make it more open in the center — will increase air circulation and help prevent diseases." Illinois Extension: "The goal of spring pruning is to produce an open centered plant. This allows air and light to penetrate easily," and "Leave 3 to 5 healthy, stout canes evenly spaced around the plant." Rose Society of Victoria favours "younger branches that are spaced evenly from around the base" over old, vigour-lost stems.

**Strengths.** Very broad, independent corroboration on both the selection basis and the underlying goal, across every publisher type used in this dossier.

**Limitations.** The exact target number varies (AF-7, §5 this ARC); the underlying selection logic does not.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None of substance for the general rule; see §5 for the numeric variation.

### AF-14 — No explicit order-of-operations rule for a weak-and-congested cane, but sequencing implies one

**Question:** Q6
**Assessed Finding:** No source explicitly states a rule for a cane that is both weak and part of a congested cluster. However, every source that gives a step-by-step pruning sequence removes dead, damaged, weak and crossing canes before making the final congestion/cane-count decision — implying, without stating, that weak-cane removal narrows the field before congestion is finally assessed.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** Oregon State Extension gives an explicit numbered sequence: dead/dying/diseased first, then crossing/rubbing, then selecting and retaining the strongest canes (which folds in weak-cane removal), then cutting the survivors to height. Texas A&M's handout sequences weak-stem removal before the "three to five" congestion-resolving step. Chicago Botanic Garden and Rose Society of South Australia both sequence weak-growth removal early, before addressing mature/structural stems.

**Strengths.** Consistent ordering across multiple independent, differently-sequenced sources.

**Limitations.** The rationale (that removing weak canes narrows the field) is the ROC's own inference from the ordering, not a stated principle in any source.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None beyond the unstated rationale noted above.

### AF-15 — Young roses get leniency, but the exact age cutoff varies

**Question:** Q6
**Assessed Finding:** Multiple sources agree that a young, still-establishing rose should not have the weak-growth removal rule applied aggressively, but the stated age cutoff for this leniency ranges from one growing season to three years.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** Marin Rose Society and Clemson: prune first-year roses lightly. Illinois Extension: "In their first two or three seasons in the garden, shrub roses can be left unpruned." Texas A&M: "Roses 3 years old and younger should be only lightly pruned, if at all." Berkeley Horticultural Nursery: skip the first two pruning seasons for bare-root roses, aside from thinning and dead-wood removal.

**Strengths.** Broad agreement on the general principle (leniency for young plants) across independent sources.

**Limitations.** The exact duration varies by a factor of three (one to three years) across sources.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether this leniency period should track the project's existing recently-planted gate (`PKR-SGT-000002`) duration, or be treated as a separate, longer window specific to this observation, was not addressed by any source and is a genuine design question for the app team, not something the ROC can resolve from the evidence.

### AF-16 — Caution against heavy thinning is conditioned on plant vigour, not universal

**Question:** Q6
**Assessed Finding:** Where sources caution against thinning too heavily, the caution is specifically tied to the plant already being weak or small — not stated as a general limit that applies regardless of the plant's condition. Several society sources actively encourage confident pruning for an otherwise healthy plant.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** Illinois Extension: "If the plant is weak or small, you may not want to cut off as much material. Each time you remove this much wood you are removing a lot of the food-making ability of the plant." Clemson: "Prune weak-growing varieties lightly and vigorous varieties more severely." By contrast, Portland Rose Society: "A total lack of pruning will yield poorer results than any amount of over pruning you may do, so prune with confidence." Chicago Botanic Garden: "an otherwise healthy rose won't die because you pruned it."

**Strengths.** Both sides of this are well evidenced by independent, credible sources; the apparent tension resolves cleanly once the extension caution is read as conditional on plant vigour rather than universal.

**Limitations.** None of substance.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None; see §5 for how this is reconciled as a practical default.

### AF-17 — Eye protection and gauntlet gloves are specifically flagged for working inside a plant's canopy

**Question:** Q7
**Assessed Finding:** Beyond generic protective equipment already covered elsewhere in this project, sources specifically flag eye protection as important when working inside a plant's canopy, and gauntlet-style, long-cuffed gloves as appropriate for major work on plants with sharp protrusions, such as reaching into a congested rose bush.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** RHS: "It is wise to protect your eyes during gardening tasks that involve being inside the canopy of plants," and "When undertaking major work on plants with sharp protrusions, gloves with long cuffs (gauntlets) provide additional protection [to your wrists and lower arms]." UC ANR (San Luis Obispo Master Gardeners) independently lists "gauntlet-style gloves," "safety glasses or goggles" and "heavy long sleeves" as standard equipment for rose pruning.

**Strengths.** Two independent sources converge on the same additional equipment recommendation, specifically tied to reaching into dense growth rather than routine pruning generally.

**Limitations.** Neither source gives a specific reason tied to reduced visibility in a tangle; the reasoning given is general (protection from sharp protrusions, being inside the canopy).

**Remaining uncertainty.** None of substance.

### AF-18 — Over-opening a congested centre risks sunscald; a general one-third rule also applies

**Question:** Q7
**Assessed Finding:** In hot-summer climates, removing too much interior growth when thinning a congested centre can expose the graft/bud union to sunscald; sources recommend deliberately leaving some interior growth rather than clearing all congestion in a single pass. A separate, general (non-rose) caution against removing more than about a third of a plant's crown in any one year also applies.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** UC ANR (San Luis Obispo Master Gardeners): "In hot summer areas, leave some interior growth to shade the graft and prevent sunscald." NC State Extension (general pruning technique, not rose-specific): "Typically, you should not remove more than a third of the total crown of a plant in any one year."

**Strengths.** The sunscald finding is rose-specific and gives a clear, actionable mechanism; the one-third rule, while general, is a credible, independently-sourced complementary caution.

**Limitations.** The sunscald finding rests on a single rose-specific source; the one-third rule is not rose-specific and is not stated as applying to a single congestion-thinning session specifically, only to annual crown removal generally.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether the sunscald risk applies outside hot-summer climates was not addressed; the source frames it as a hot-climate-specific caution.

### AF-19 — A real cane-removal sequence exists, framed as workflow quality rather than injury-avoidance

**Question:** Q7
**Assessed Finding:** One source gives an explicit removal sequence — clear easy, accessible material (dead, damaged, crossing) first, then reach for weak or interior growth — though it frames this as good pruning workflow rather than explicit safety/injury-avoidance.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low**

**Evidence.** NC State Extension: "Take your time. Make the easy cuts first. Remove diseased, damaged, and crossing branches and then take out weak branches and those growing toward the middle of the plant."

**Strengths.** A concrete, usable sequence from a credible source.

**Limitations.** Rests on a single source, and that source does not connect the sequence to avoiding scratches or getting entangled, even though it plausibly reduces time spent reaching into dense growth.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether this sequence measurably reduces injury risk, as opposed to just improving pruning quality, was not addressed by any source.

## 5. Preserved Unreconciled Points of Variation

Reproduced from `FRD-BUSHROSE-WEAKCONGESTED-02` §6. Karla accepted all stated defaults for these (c_accept: "Yes, all of them") with no amendments.

### C-1 — The exact numeric threshold for "weak" (pencil thickness)

**What the sources say.** Nearly every source uses "pencil thickness" as the qualitative threshold for removing a weak cane. Of the two sources that translate this into an actual measurement, Kansas City Rose Society gives "1/4-inch" (about 6.4mm) while the Rose Society of NSW gives "no less than 10mm" — a difference of roughly 50%. Most sources give no number at all. *[Corrected at v1.2, 29 September 2026: this previously said one source (Orange County Rose Society) questions applying the pencil rule strictly. On checking, that source supports the rule. No source found questions it.]*

**Reasons given.** Not stated by either quantifying source for why their particular figure was chosen; the difference may simply reflect different pencils, or different local convention.

**Treatment.** Preserved as unresolved. AF-5 records the qualitative "pencil thickness" concept, which is broadly agreed, rather than either specific measurement.

### C-2 — The target number of canes to retain when thinning a congested bush

**What the sources say.** Retained-cane targets given across sources range from as low as "one to four" (Ludwig's Roses, South Africa) to as high as "four to eight" (several US extension sources), with "three to five," "three to seven" and "five to seven" all separately attested by different, mostly independent sources. Australian sources cluster tighter (three to five); the South African source is markedly more conservative than the US extension cluster.

**Reasons given.** Not stated by any source; the variation may reflect genuine regional differences in growing conditions, cultivar mix, or simply differing schools of practice.

**Treatment.** Preserved as unresolved. AF-7 records the range rather than a single figure.

### C-3 — How cautious to be about removing too much at once

**What the sources say.** University-extension sources (Illinois, Clemson) give an explicit caution tied to plant vigour: prune a weak or small plant lightly, and vigorous varieties more severely. Several rose-society sources (Portland, Orange County, Marin, Chicago Botanic Garden) instead emphasise that roses are resilient and encourage confident, decisive pruning, without qualifying this by the plant's condition.

**Reasons given.** The extension caution is reasoned (a weak plant has less food-making capacity to spare); the society sources' confidence is not specifically reasoned, more a general reassurance against excessive caution.

**Treatment.** Not a true contradiction — the society sources are not addressing an already-weak plant specifically, and the extension caution does not argue against confident pruning of a healthy one. A practical default reconciling both is stated at §6.

## 6. Approved Practical Defaults Carried Forward

Reproduced from `FRD-BUSHROSE-WEAKCONGESTED-02` §9. Each is the ROC's own recommendation under EAS §2.10, not a sourced finding; any Founder may override any of them at any time.

**C-1 default — Weak-cane threshold.** Describe the threshold as "about the thickness of a pencil," matching the overwhelming majority convention, rather than committing to either the 1/4-inch or 10mm figure specifically.

**C-2 default — Target cane count.** Present a range (roughly three to seven canes for a typical established bush, adjusting down for a modest or young plant) rather than a single fixed number, and avoid presenting any single source's figure as the definitive standard.

**C-3 default — Caution against heavy thinning.** Apply the extension sources' vigour-conditioned caution (prune lightly if the plant already looks weak or small) without extending it into general excessive caution for a plant that otherwise looks healthy, consistent with the society sources' reassurance.

**U-1 — Explicit cause of weak growth.** Not stated by any source in named terms. **Default:** describe weak growth by its recognition criteria and treatment, not by an unproven cause narrative.

**U-2 — Weak canes arching/flopping.** Not described by any source. **Default:** do not include this as a stated visual indicator.

**U-3 — "Twiggy centre" as a congestion term.** Not used this way by any source. **Default:** use "crowded" or "densely packed" for congestion in Pip's own wording; reserve "twiggy" for weak individual-cane growth, consistent with how sources actually use the term.

**U-4 — Distinguishing healthy density from overcrowding.** No source gives criteria. **Default:** rely on the existing physical-confirmation requirement and the "open, airy centre" goal (AF-13) as the operative standard, rather than offering a lookalike checklist the literature doesn't support.

**U-5 — Photo-based thickness/density judgment.** Not directly confirmed by any source. **Default:** where a photo leaves confidence low on a thickness judgment, request a photo that includes a common size reference (a coin or pencil) alongside the cane, consistent with general extension diagnostic-photo practice.

**U-6 — Why weak-cane removal narrows the field first.** No source states this rationale. **Default:** sequence Pip's own guidance the way sources consistently sequence it in practice — address weak, dead, damaged and crossing canes first, then make the final congestion/thinning decision on what remains.

**U-7 — Avoiding misidentifying a healthy cane as weak.** Not addressed by any source. **Default:** cross-refer to the existing "confirm on the physical rose before every cut" principle (Architecture §4.2) as the primary safeguard; no additional rose-specific caution is offered beyond that.

## 7. Downstream Note for KIT

This ARC's AF-12 (weak growth: always remove fully) and AF-13 (congestion: always remove fully, tied to the "open, airy centre" goal) both confirm the "always remove fully" pattern already used in `ARC-BUSHROSE-STEMCROSSING-01` and `ARC-BUSHROSE-INWARDGROWTH-01`. This is consistent with those ARCs and contrasts, as expected, with `ARC-BUSHROSE-DAMAGEDGROWTH-01`'s severity-dependent trim-or-remove rule — a genuine, already-approved point of difference between observations, not an inconsistency to reconcile.

With this ARC approved, all six MVP pruning observations named in Architecture §5.2 (dead wood, crossing/rubbing stems, inward-growing stems, damaged growth, weak/congested growth, whole-bush framework) now have an approved ARC or are governed by an existing one, together with the supporting gates (recently-planted, dormancy, basic care, rose-type scope-qualification).
