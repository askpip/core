## 1. Purpose and Scope

This ARC is the authoritative, structured representation of the Founder-approved information produced by the research commission on what a bush rose is and what its ordinary, non-pruning care involves across a year — classification (Q1), general seasonal care (Q2), care for a rose not yet ready for pruning (Q3), winter protection (Q4), and simple, non-diagnostic signs worth a gardener's attention (Q5).

It supports the Ask Pip journey for a gardener whose rose is not yet ready for the pruning journey (too recently planted, not dormant, or otherwise deferred at the readiness check), and the general "what is a bush rose, and what does caring for it involve?" content anticipated in the Learn area. This ARC does not address pruning mechanics, which remain the separate subject of `ARC-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01`, `ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01`, `ARC-BUSHROSE-RECENTPLANT-01` and `ARC-BUSHROSE-DORMANCY-01`.

This ARC is the source from which KIT will build the basic-care PKR content.

## 4. Approved Findings — Full Content

Each Assessed Finding is reproduced here faithfully from the Founder-approved Evidence Assessments in `FRD-BUSHROSE-BASICCARE-01` and `-02`, without new interpretation, per ROC Operations Manual §12.4.

### F1 — "Bush Rose" as a Classification Term

**Finding:** UK horticultural authority (RHS) uses "bush rose" to mean modern shrub-form garden roses, principally Hybrid Tea and Floribunda types, as distinct from climbing/rambling roses, standard (tree) roses, and species/shrub roses.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** A single source (RHS), but a highly authoritative national body; not independently corroborated by a second source using the same "bush rose" framing (see F1a).

**Sources:** Royal Horticultural Society, "Modern bush roses."

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Single-source finding; classification terminology outside the UK is addressed separately at F1a.

### F1a — "Bush Rose" as a UK-Weighted Usage (Observation, Not a Factual Finding)

**Finding:** None of the nine US extension sources consulted used "bush rose" as a category label; they referred to the same growth habit as "Hybrid Tea," "Floribunda," "Grandiflora," or simply "rose bush." Round 2 searching across further US and Australian sources continued to find no use of "bush rose" as a formal classification term outside UK/Commonwealth usage.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Not Assigned — a usage observation about terminology across sources, not a claim of horticultural fact.

**Rationale:** Absence held consistently across both research rounds and a wider source set in Round 2.

**Sources:** The full Round 1 source log (`FRD-BUSHROSE-BASICCARE-01` §4) by its absence; Round 2 additional US and Australian sources reviewed and found consistent (`FRD-BUSHROSE-BASICCARE-01_Round2_Research_Notes.md`).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Bears on gardener-facing terminology, a product/copy decision carried forward at §6, not resolved by this observation itself.

### F2 — Watering Pattern

**Finding:** Established bush roses are generally watered deeply and infrequently rather than shallowly and often, in the general range of 1–2 inches (or local rainfall equivalent) of water per week during the growing season when rainfall is insufficient, with closer attention needed in roughly the first two years after planting.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Four independent institutional sources (RHS; Illinois, Missouri and Cornell CCE Rockland extension services), consistent in substance.

**Sources:** RHS rose care advice profile; Iowa State University Extension; University of Missouri Extension; Cornell Cooperative Extension (Rockland County).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** "Roughly the first two years" concerns watering vigilance specifically and is a different question from RECENTPLANT's "three or more years" for pruning readiness — the two are compatible, not conflicting, but should be stated separately in downstream guidance so they are not conflated (confirmed by direct comparison against ARC-BUSHROSE-RECENTPLANT-01 in Round 2).

### F3 — Growing-Season Mulching

**Finding:** During the growing season, a 2–3 inch layer of organic mulch (bark, well-rotted manure, wood chips, pine needles) applied around the base and kept a few inches clear of the stem/crown is commonly recommended to conserve moisture and suppress weeds.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Four independent institutional sources, consistent in substance; correctly distinguished from the separate practice of winter mounding (F9), which uses a materially different depth for a different purpose.

**Sources:** RHS; Iowa State University Extension; University of Missouri Extension; Cornell Cooperative Extension (Rockland County).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** None material to the claim itself.

### F4 — Feeding Pattern

**Finding:** Feeding commonly follows a pattern of starting in spring as new growth begins, with further application(s) through summer, and stopping by mid-to-late summer so new growth can harden before winter. The exact cutoff date varies by source and region and is not itself treated as a single fixed fact.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High for the general pattern (six independent sources agree on shape); Not Assigned for any single cutoff date, since the evidence shows the date is region-dependent.

**Rationale:** Illinois Extension (15 August cutoff), Penn State Extension (stopping in August) and UGA Cobb County Extension (as late as September, a warmer, later-frost climate) track each region's own first-frost timing, not a disagreement about the underlying reasoning.

**Sources:** University of Illinois Extension; Penn State Extension; UGA Extension (Cobb County).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No single date is approved as a finding; downstream guidance should state the pattern, not a fixed calendar date.

### F5 — Deadheading Pattern

**Finding:** Deadheading (removing spent blooms) during the growing season is commonly recommended to encourage further flowering, and is commonly stopped in early-to-mid autumn to allow the plant to set hips and move toward dormancy. The exact cutoff date varies by source and region and is not itself treated as a single fixed fact.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High for the general pattern (five independent sources agree on shape); Low for any single cutoff date.

**Rationale:** Illinois Extension (1 October), UGA Cobb County (October generally) and Iowa State (stopping "in fall" without a specific date) share the same underlying reasoning (stopping deadheading signals dormancy) with different regional figures.

**Sources:** University of Illinois Extension; UGA Extension (Cobb County); Iowa State University Extension.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** No single date is approved as a finding, for the same reason as F4.

### F6 — General Pest/Disease Watchfulness

**Finding:** Commonly cited rose problems to watch for include blackspot, powdery mildew, aphids and spider mites; sources generally advise watering at the base, or in the morning if overhead watering is used, to reduce the foliage wetness associated with fungal disease.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Four independent institutional sources, consistent, stating a widely and plainly agreed category of guidance.

**Sources:** University of Missouri Extension; University of Maryland Extension; and two further sources in the Round 1 log (§4).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** None material to the claim itself. This finding does not extend to diagnosis of any specific pest or disease, which is out of scope.

### F7 — Newly Planted Rose: Minimal Cutting, Not Pruning

**Finding:** A rose planted within roughly the past year is generally not pruned the way an established rose is; sources recommend leaving as much foliage and stem growth as possible in that first season — limiting cuts to removing spent blooms rather than cutting stems for arrangements — because a newly planted rose relies on its foliage to photosynthesise and its energy to establish its root system.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** High.

**Rationale:** Three independent sources agree, two institutional (Missouri Extension, Cornell CCE Rockland) and one a named practitioner (Fine Gardening/Zimmerman) whose claim matches rather than stands alone against the institutional sources.

**Sources:** University of Missouri Extension; Cornell Cooperative Extension (Rockland County); Fine Gardening (citing rosarian Paul Zimmerman).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Consistent with, and does not duplicate, the already-approved RECENTPLANT findings on pruning readiness specifically (§4, F2, and confirmed by direct Round 2 comparison).

### F9 — Winter Protection: Soil Mounding

**Finding:** For bush roses in climates with hard winter frosts, common winter protection guidance is to wait until the plant has had several hard/killing frosts and entered dormancy, then mound soil, compost, or mulch to a height of approximately 10–12 inches around the base/crown (covering the graft union on grafted plants), removing the mound again in spring once hard-frost risk has passed.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Very High.

**Rationale:** Six independent institutional sources across five US states, strongly and specifically consistent on both method and figure.

**Sources:** UNH Extension; Penn State Extension; UGA Extension (Cobb County); University of Illinois Extension; University of Missouri Extension; Cornell Cooperative Extension (Rockland County).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** None material to the claim itself.

### F10 — Winter Protection: Collar and Wrap Methods

**Finding:** Additional or alternative winter protection methods described include building a collar (tarpaper, wire mesh, or similar) around the plant and filling it with insulating material (straw, pine bark, peat moss), and wrapping canes with burlap or evergreen boughs — described particularly for climbing roses or more exposed/colder sites.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Two independent institutional sources, consistent with each other but not as broadly corroborated across the full source set as F9.

**Sources:** UNH Extension; Penn State Extension.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Narrower evidentiary base than F9.

### F11 — Pre-Winter Watering and Debris Removal

**Finding:** Watering thoroughly after the first hard frost but before the ground freezes, so the plant enters winter well-hydrated, and clearing fallen leaf debris from around the plant before winter to reduce overwintering fungal spores, were both specifically described as part of winter preparation. A second independent source (Nebraska Extension, Lancaster County) specifies this more precisely: watering to a depth of 8–12 inches, every 2–3 weeks, continuing until the ground freezes even once the plant is dormant, with the identical reasoning for debris removal.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate (raised from Low in Round 2).

**Rationale:** Two independent institutional sources (Penn State Extension; Nebraska Extension, Lancaster County), in different states, consistent in substance and in the specific reasoning given for debris removal. Not raised to High: two sources, not the broader four-to-six-source corroboration behind F2/F3/F9.

**Sources:** Penn State Extension; Nebraska Extension (Lancaster County).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** None material beyond the confidence level already stated.

### F12 — General, Non-Diagnostic Signs Worth Noticing

**Finding:** Gardeners are commonly advised to regularly observe a rose's leaves, buds and stems through the growing season for general signs such as yellowing, spotting or other discoloration, leaf drop, stunted or reduced growth, distorted or curled leaves, and reduced or absent flowering — with noticing these signs early, before identifying a specific cause, described as the useful first step.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Moderate.

**Rationale:** Primary, explicit statement from one strong institutional source (University of Maryland Extension); the general symptom list is echoed loosely elsewhere, but the specific "notice without diagnosing first" framing rests on the one source. Round 2 reviewed three further institutional sources specifically for this question and found none offering the same general, non-diagnostic framing — a checked absence, not a gap in search effort, supporting rather than undermining this confidence level.

**Sources:** University of Maryland Extension.

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Confirmed as genuinely thin literature, not under-researched.

### F13 — Ordinary Conditions as the Most Common Cause of Decline

**Finding:** At least one source states explicitly that ordinary growing conditions and care — rather than pests or diseases — are the most common cause of a rose's decline, particularly in newer plantings, and specifically names inconsistent watering, poor drainage/soggy soil, and weather extremes as worth a gardener's first consideration when a rose looks unwell.

**Evidence Confidence Level:** Low–Moderate.

**Rationale:** Explicit causal claim from a single institutional source; indirectly supported by the general weight of Q2/Q3 evidence (care quality mattering), which is suggestive but not independent confirmation of this specific claim. Round 2 checked further and found no additional source stating this specific causal claim.

**Sources:** University of Missouri Extension (G6601).

**Limitations/uncertainty:** Single-source finding for the specific causal claim.

## 5. Preserved, Unreconciled Evidence and Standing Limitations

**Geographic diversity (`FRD-BUSHROSE-BASICCARE-02` §9, §10 Decision 2).** All institutional-quality sources underlying F2–F6 and F9–F11 are from the UK and continental US. Round 2 searched Australian, South African, New Zealand and continental European sources specifically for this commission and found the readily available material to be predominantly commercial or enthusiast in nature, not the government/university-extension-equivalent sources this commission otherwise relies on. This is recorded as a real scarcity of the relevant source type within the time available, not a failure of search effort. Closing this gap to the same evidentiary standard would require the Founders to accept a lower evidentiary bar for non-UK/US regions, commission a materially larger non-English-language search with translation, or accept the current evidence base with the gap disclosed. **This approval does not decide between those options** — it approves F1–F13 on the evidence as gathered, with this regional limitation disclosed rather than resolved. See §6.
