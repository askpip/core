## 1. Purpose and Scope

This ARC is the authoritative, structured representation of the Founder-approved information produced by the research commission `BUSHROSE-NOFLOWERS`: What causes do credible sources give for an established bush rose producing few or no flowers (Q1); What can a beginner observe that sources say distinguishes one cause from another (Q2); What do sources advise doing for each cause (Q3); Where do sources disagree, and what remains unanswered (Q4).

It is the source from which KIT will write Pip's answer to "Why isn't my rose flowering?", replacing the "still learning" entry in the app's common questions.

## 4. Approved Findings — Full Content

Reproduced from `FRD-BUSHROSE-NOFLOWERS-01` §7 as approved. Source codes refer to the Source Register at §8.

"Sign" gives the observation a source attaches to the cause, where one is stated; "None stated" means sources give the cause without a sign that separates it from others.

#### Light and site (Q1, Q2)

**AF-1 (established).** Too little sun, or too much shade, reduces flowering. TRE-SH: "the less sunlight a rose receives the less flowers and less vigorous the plant will be." ARS-RRP: "Roses growing in too much shade will not produce many blooms and develop disease." DA-BAS: in lighter shade, "often with a little fewer blooms". YAT-NZ: "Roses need a sunny position to grow and flower well." YAT-AU names "lack of sun" first among causes. RHS-BLIND links blindness with "an excessively shady area". Also UMD ("shady conditions"), AE-CSU, AE-ILL, CLEM, KINGS, NZRS-FAQ. Sign: none stated beyond the site itself; TRE-SH adds reduced vigour.

**AF-2 (supported interpretation).** Sources give different minimum hours of direct sun: four (DA-BAS), five (TUI, PALM, AE-ILL), six (TRE-SH, UCIPM-C, ILL-PREP, OKS-R, NMSU, UGA), and six to eight (AE-CSU, ARS-RRP, YAT-AU). Six is the most common figure. PALM adds that roses "will be fine with some afternoon shade" (C-1).

**AF-3 (supported interpretation).** Nearby trees and shrubs compete with roses for light, water and nutrients, and sources advise planting roses away from them. YAT-NZ: roses "should not be grown too close to other shrubs or trees that will compete with them for light, moisture and nutrients." ARS-RRP: "Tree roots will steal water and nutrients your rose needs." OKS-R: roses "seldom prosper in the vicinity of such fibrous rooted trees as birch, elm, maple, willow, oak, and others." ILL-PREP: "The roots of roses don't compete well with those of trees and other shrubs." NMSU: "Do not plant them under trees." Only YAT-NZ ties competition directly to flowering ("to grow and flower well"). Sign: none stated beyond nearby trees or shrubs.

**AF-4 (expert opinion, single source).** ARS-RRP: "A rose planted under a tree might be growing in sun at first. After a few years it will be in shade."

#### Feeding (Q1, Q2)

**AF-5 (established).** Too much fertiliser, especially nitrogen, gives leafy growth at the expense of flowers. UCIPM-C: "Too much nitrogen may shift plants into vegetative growth at the expense of flowers". AE-ILL: "Too much nitrogen in the soil also can cause excessive green growth, but little or no flowers." AE-CSU: too much fertilizer can "cause it to grow extra leaves and stems at the expense of blooms." UMD: "excessive lush growth from too much fertilizer". YAT-AU: "a high nitrogen and low potassium fertiliser." CLEM (general): "Too much nitrogen can cause plants to produce primarily leaves and stems." Sign: abundant leafy growth with few or no flowers.

**AF-6 (supported interpretation).** Too little feeding is also given as a cause of poor flowering, and feeding as a remedy. YAT-AU: "lack of nutrients". AE-CSU: inadequate foliage "may result from disease or too little fertilizer", and a bush without adequate foliage "can't produce the food it needs to make new flowers." RHS-BLIND: "Improve growing conditions by feeding with a proprietary rose fertiliser and mulching". KINGS: "Keep your roses well fed to ensure they stay happy, healthy, and full of flowers." RSNSW: "Roses are hungry plants". NMSU: "Nitrogen is the main nutrient responsible for this required cyclic growth." Sign: none stated, except AE-CSU's sparse foliage (C-2).

**AF-7 (expert opinion, single source, commercial).** YAT-AU: rose fertilisers "are rich in potassium, a key nutrient in promoting flowering", and names "a high nitrogen and low potassium fertiliser" as a cause of lack of flowers.

#### Water and weather (Q1, Q2)

**AF-8 (supported interpretation).** Too little water, or drought stress, reduces flowering. MARIN: roses "avoid water stress by dropping their leaves, producing small blooms, or becoming virtually dormant". AE-CSU gives too little water among reasons, with roses needing "at least an inch of water per week during the growing season." CLEM (general): "Flowers or flower buds dry and drop off when there is temporary lack of moisture in the plants." UCIPM-C: "Drought stress leads to defoliation and sunburn of canes". Sign: NMSU "Leaves are pale and limp when stressed for moisture."; MARIN, leaf drop and small blooms.

**AF-9 (expert opinion, limited).** Hot weather and weather stress can slow or stop flowering. UAEX-G, after an intense summer: "they slowed down or stopped blooming just to stay alive." MARIN: "the plant may not grow or produce many flowers during the hot summer months". UMD lists "Environmental (weather) stresses". Both UAEX-G and MARIN describe flowering resuming as temperatures cool. Sign: none stated beyond the weather.

**AF-10 (expert opinion, limited).** Cold, frost and winter damage are listed as causes of few flowers. UMD lists "winter-kill". CLEM (general): "Cold weather may kill flower buds or partially opened flowers." No rose-specific source describes frost damage to rose buds (§4.3).

#### Pruning, variety habit and age (Q1, Q2)

**AF-11 (supported interpretation).** Improper pruning is listed as a cause of poor flowering. UMD: "improper pruning (either too much, too little, or at the wrong time)". AE-ILL: "the likely culprit is over pruning and perhaps over fertilizing." OKS-R: "Too much spring pruning can weaken plants." TUI: yearly pruning will "improve flowering". No source states where "too much" begins for a bush rose (C-6).

**AF-12 (supported interpretation).** Harder pruning gives fewer, larger flowers; lighter pruning gives more, smaller flowers. UCIPM-C: "Less pruning preserves the size of plants and results in a greater number of smaller flowers". OKS-R: "If only a few large flowers are preferred, cut the plants more severely." UGA: weak-growing varieties "are pruned lightly while vigorous growing varieties are pruned more heavily."

**AF-13 (expert opinion, limited).** On repeat-flowering roses, late pruning delays the first flowers rather than preventing them. UAEX-P: "late pruning simply delays the first flowers" (of Knock Out roses), and "if you haven't pruned yet, do so immediately or it can delay the onset of the first blooms." TRE-FAQ: "It takes approximately 6-8 weeks for a rose to produce a flower." (said of timing a display after a light trim).

**AF-14 (established).** Once-flowering roses flower on older wood, and pruning them before they flower removes the flowers. ILL-PRUNE: once-blooming old roses "produce flowers on old wood, all pruning should be delayed until after flowering." AE-ILL: "Old garden roses and wild roses typically bloom once, and you should prune them in the summer after their bloom cycle has finished." AE-UVM, of a rambler: "those need to be pruned immediately after blooming." DA-FAQ: once-flowering roses "flower on older growth". CLEM: severe cutting, "such as with climbing roses, can remove all the flowering wood." This agrees with `ARC-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01` AF-19.

**AF-15 (expert opinion, limited).** Hybrid Tea, Grandiflora and Floribunda roses flower on the current season's growth. ILL-PRUNE: they "produce the best flowers on new or current season's wood." UAEX-P says the same of Knock Out shrub roses ("bloom on the new growth").

**AF-16 (supported interpretation).** A rose that flowers once, in late spring or early summer, and not again, may be a once-flowering variety rather than a repeat-flowering bush rose. AE-CSU lists a once-blooming variety: "it will bloom only once a year in the late spring or early summer." DA-FAQ, for a rose that hasn't flowered: "The most likely cause is that you have purchased a once-flowering variety." DA-ONCE: once-flowering roses "bloom just once, usually in early summer". AE-UVM identified non-flowering "rose bushes" as a once-flowering rambler: "It's definitely not a hybrid tea rose!" GU-H (New Zealand): heritage roses "many varieties only will bloom once". Sign: a single early-summer flowering each year.

**AF-17 (expert opinion, limited).** Repeat-flowering roses flower in flushes or waves rather than continuously. DA-ONCE: "They begin with an early summer flush, then return in waves, often continuing until the first frosts." NZRS-FAQ: "Roses flower in New Zealand from late October through to May", with a regional spring peak in November to mid-December.

**AF-18 (supported interpretation).** A newly planted rose may flower less in its first season. DA-FAQ: newly planted roses "often focus their energy on developing a strong root system during their first season", which "can result in fewer flowers". AE-CSU: "Don't expect too much from a plant during its first year." TRE-FAQ differs in degree: "Most roses will flower in their first spring / summer after planting." (C-5). This concerns recently planted roses, outside the scope of established-rose guidance.

**AF-19 (established).** Removing spent flowers from repeat-flowering roses encourages further flowering. UCIPM-C: it "allows the plant to conserve energy and leads to further flower production". ISU: deadheading "is done to encourage additional bloom on modern and other repeat-flowering roses." PALM: "remove spent flowers to conserve the plant's energy and encourage repeat blooms." KINGS: "Deadhead old flowers to encourage new growth and repeat flowering." This agrees with `ARC-BUSHROSE-BASICCARE-01` F5.

**AF-20 (expert opinion, single source).** ISU: "The development of rose hips (fruits) slows plant growth and helps prepare the plants for winter."

#### Blind shoots (Q1 to Q3)

**AF-21 (established).** A blind shoot grows and leafs normally but forms no flower bud at its tip. RHS-BLIND: "Flowering shoots develop normally, but fail to develop a terminal flower bud", and leaf and stem growth "can appear perfectly healthy". RHS-FAQ, answering "Most of the shoots of my rose have failed to produce flowers.": "This is often the result of a poorly understood problem known as rose blindness." VSRG-B: laterals "instead of producing a bud, they go 'blind'", in early spring and autumn growth. TANTAU: the shoot tip "is no longer visible or even dried up". AE-ILL: canes "that are called 'blind shoots.'" Sign: a healthy-looking shoot with no bud at its tip; TANTAU adds a bent or dried tip.

**AF-22 (supported interpretation).** The cause of blind shoots is not established. RHS-BLIND: "The cause is unknown", possibly "adverse weather conditions damaging the shoot tip" or excessive shade. VSRG-B: "A single proven and accepted cause is still unknown." AE-ILL: "not really known", with climate, "over-fertilization and lack of enough sunlight" suggested. TANTAU: "large temperature differences between day and night temperatures." (C-3).

**AF-23 (expert opinion, limited).** Some varieties are described as more prone. RHS-BLIND names 'Peace' (a Hybrid Tea) and the climber 'New Dawn'. VSRG-B: "particularly, for some reason, on red HTs", naming 'Mr. Lincoln', 'Chrysler Imperial', 'Peace' and 'Crimson Glory'. TANTAU: more often in "more frequently flowering and fast-shooting hybrid tea roses" and upright varieties.

**AF-24 (supported interpretation).** Blind shoots are cut back to prompt a new shoot that can flower. RHS-BLIND: "Cut a blind shoot back by half to a strong bud to stimulate further growth", which "should produce flowers later in the season". VSRG-B: "cut the lateral back to the next lower stipule"; a short blind shoot from old wood low on the plant is removed entirely ("It will never do anything."). TANTAU: prune "to the next fully formed five-leaf". The cut point differs (C-4).

#### Buds that form but fail to open (Q1 to Q3)

**AF-25 (supported interpretation).** In balling, buds form but don't open, usually after cool, wet weather; double, thin-petalled flowers are most affected. RHS-BALL: "flower buds develop normally but do not open"; "Cool, wet weather saturates the outer petals and then the sunshine dries and fuses them into a tight, papery shell"; "Thin-petalled, double flowers such as rose, peony and camellia cultivars are most susceptible to this condition." RHS-FAQ: balling is "usually caused by cool, wet weather." TRE-BOT: "Sometimes buds will ball and fail to open, leaving a head of brown petals." Sign (RHS-BALL): the bud "often feels soft and slimy", the outer petals turn brown, and "When the outer petals are prized apart, the inner petals appear normal."

**AF-26 (supported interpretation).** Balled or rotting buds are removed promptly. RHS-BALL: "Remove balled buds promptly before grey mould sets in as it can infect other blooms." TRE-BOT: "Cut off the offending bud, increase air circulation around the plant and do not overhead water." RHS-BALL states "There are no chemicals treatments for this disorder"; TRE-BOT recommends fungicide for botrytis (C-7).

**AF-27 (supported interpretation).** Botrytis (grey mould), favoured by cool, wet weather, spots petals and stops buds opening. TRE-BOT: botrytis "is more prevalent in cool, wet weather and generally only effects the flower head and neck", with first symptoms "pink/red spotting on the petals". UW (quoting UC Davis): "Affected plants have spotted flower petals and buds that fail to open, often with woolly gray fungal spores on decaying tissue." RHS-BALL notes grey mould following balling. Sign: spotted petals, then grey fuzzy growth.

**AF-28 (established).** Thrips streak, fleck or distort petals, and buds may fail to open. UCIPM-I: thrips "primarily cause injury to rose flowers, causing blossom petals to streak with brown or become distorted". UMD: "Irregular white or brown streaks on flowers". UGA: "Thrips enter developing flower buds to feed and cause petals to become flecked and discolored." UW (quoting the Olympia Rose Society): "Buds do not open, or flowers are deformed. Petals have brownish yellow streaks". AE-CSU names thrips among causes. Sign: brown or white streaks on petals; distorted flowers.

**AF-29 (supported interpretation).** Budworm caterpillars bore into buds, leaving holes, and the flowers fail to open properly. RSWA: "The caterpillars feed on leaves first, then burrow into buds, preventing flowers from opening properly." YAT-BUD: damage is "Holes in flower buds and fruit." UCIPM-I: "some caterpillars, like the tobacco budworm, may bore into flower buds". Sign: holes in buds; caterpillars.

**AF-30 (expert opinion, limited).** Rose midge larvae make buds blacken and wither. UMD: "Flower buds that do not form correctly, turn brown or black and wither" may indicate rose midge. UCIPM-I: larvae leave "the injured buds to wither, blacken, and die", and the pest has not been reported in California since 1996. No New Zealand or Australian source describing rose midge was located (§4.3).

#### Disease, rootstock and other causes (Q1, Q2)

**AF-31 (supported interpretation).** Black spot strips leaves and weakens the rose, and the rose produces fewer flowers. OKS-BS: "Repeated severe defoliation weakens the plant so that it produces fewer and poorer quality blooms". UMD: black spot "causes severe loss of foliage on susceptible rose cultivars". UAEX-G: "Black spot can weaken a rose bush". AE-CSU: a bush without adequate foliage "can't produce the food it needs to make new flowers." Sign: dark spots on leaves, yellowing and leaf drop. This agrees with `ARC-BUSHROSE-BASICCARE-01` F6.

**AF-32 (expert opinion, single source).** LBG: when a grafted rose "died during the winter, but its rootstock, a very different rose, survived and sent up shoots of its own", the "resulting roses are small-flowered roses", naming *Rosa canina* (single pink), *R. multiflora* (tiny single white) and 'Dr. Huey' (semi-double dark red). Sign: flowers unlike the rose bought. This agrees with `ARC-BUSHROSE-SUCKERS-01` AF-6 (sucker flowers differ) and AF-21 (suckers can take over).

**AF-33 (expert opinion, single source).** UMD lists pesticides among "possible reasons for poor flowering", without further explanation.

**AF-34 (expert opinion, limited).** Sources point first to site and growing conditions when an otherwise healthy rose won't flower. YAT-AU: "If the plant is generally healthy and there are no signs of pests and diseases", the cause "might include lack of sun, lack of nutrients," or high-nitrogen fertiliser. UGA: "Many rose failures can be linked directly to poor site location."

#### Photographs

No finding. No source located addresses what a photograph can establish about why a rose is not flowering (§4.3). `ARC-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01` AF-34 (Low) and AF-41 remain the project's general position on photographs.

## 5. Preserved, Unreconciled Points of Variation

Reproduced from `FRD-BUSHROSE-NOFLOWERS-01` §6. These are recorded ambiguity, not resolved.

#### 5.1 Conflicts between sources

**C-1 — Minimum hours of sun (AF-2).** DA-BAS: "at least four hours of direct sun". TUI ("at least five hours sun a day"), PALM ("Five hours of sunlight is adequate") and AE-ILL ("a minimum of five hours"). TRE-SH, UCIPM-C, ILL-PREP, OKS-R, NMSU and UGA: six hours. AE-CSU ("at least 6-8 hours"), ARS-RRP ("about 6-8 hours") and YAT-AU ("6-8 hours of full sun"). NZRS-FAQ and KINGS say "full sun" without a figure. The two New Zealand sources that give a figure give five. All sources agree that less sun means fewer flowers (AF-1). Unresolved.

**C-2 — Feeding as a cause or as a remedy (AF-5, AF-6, AF-7).** Six sources say too much fertiliser, especially nitrogen, gives leaves rather than flowers (AF-5). Other sources give too little feeding as a cause or recommend feeding to improve flowering: YAT-AU "lack of nutrients"; AE-CSU "too little fertilizer"; RHS-BLIND "feeding with a proprietary rose fertiliser"; KINGS "well fed... full of flowers"; RSNSW "hungry plants". NMSU calls nitrogen "the main nutrient" for the cycle of flowering growth. AE-ILL suspects "over fertilizing" in the same answer that lists blind shoots, for which RHS-BLIND recommends feeding. The positions concern different amounts and kinds of fertiliser; no source states a quantity separating enough from too much. Unresolved.

**C-3 — The cause of blind shoots (AF-22).** RHS-BLIND: "The cause is unknown", possibly weather damage to the shoot tip or excessive shade. VSRG-B: "A single proven and accepted cause is still unknown." AE-ILL: not really known, with climate, "over-fertilization and lack of enough sunlight" suggested. TANTAU: "large temperature differences between day and night temperatures." Sources agree the cause is not established and differ on the suspected factors. Unresolved.

**C-4 — Where to cut a blind shoot (AF-24).** RHS-BLIND: "back by half to a strong bud". VSRG-B: "back to the next lower stipule", and remove the whole lateral if it is a short one from old wood low on the plant. TANTAU: to "the next fully formed five-leaf". All agree on cutting back to prompt new growth. Unresolved.

**C-5 — Flowering in the first season (AF-18).** TRE-FAQ: "Most roses will flower in their first spring / summer after planting." DA-FAQ: newly planted roses "often focus their energy on developing a strong root system", which "can result in fewer flowers". AE-CSU: "Don't expect too much from a plant during its first year." The positions differ in degree (some flowers versus fewer flowers). Unresolved.

**C-6 — How much pruning is too much (AF-11, AF-12).** UMD lists pruning "too much, too little, or at the wrong time" and AE-ILL suspects "over pruning"; OKS-R says "Too much spring pruning can weaken plants." ILL-PRUNE, for Hybrid Teas, Grandifloras and Floribundas, describes "removing about one-half to two-thirds of the plant's height". UCIPM-C and OKS-R say harder pruning gives fewer, larger flowers. No source states the point at which pruning becomes too hard for a bush rose. Unresolved. (`ARC-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01` AF-17 records the common reduction as one-third to one-half.)

**C-7 — Whether balled buds are a weather disorder or a disease (AF-25, AF-27).** RHS-BALL treats balling as a disorder caused by cool, wet weather followed by sun, after which grey mould may set in, and states "There are no chemicals treatments for this disorder." TRE-BOT describes buds that "ball and fail to open" as a sign of botrytis, a fungus, and recommends fungicide. The two describe a similar sign with different explanations. Unresolved.
