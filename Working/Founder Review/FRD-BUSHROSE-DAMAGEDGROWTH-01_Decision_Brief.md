# Decision Brief — Damaged Growth on an Established, Dormant Bush Rose

This is the plain-language companion to `FRD-BUSHROSE-DAMAGEDGROWTH-01`. Like the project's more recent dossiers, it's built for one real decision, not a list of open questions.

## 1. What you are being asked to do

Read this Brief, then the Review Form asks for one thing: **approve the sixteen findings below as `ARC-BUSHROSE-DAMAGEDGROWTH-01`, approve with amendments, or decline.** Everything the research could close, it closed; everything it couldn't (a real, permanent gap rather than a research shortfall) has a stated ROC-recommended default instead of being left for you to rule on.

## 2. The short version

This is one of the two remaining unresearched observations from the app's six (the other being weak or congested growth, still to come). Deliberately kept as its own commission rather than combined with weak/congested growth on an assumption — the same lesson learned from the crossing/rubbing-and-inward-growth split.

The research came back strong. Two findings are among the best-attested in the whole project: canker (a fungal disease) has a remarkably consistent, well-documented set of visible signs across five independent sources, and there's near-universal agreement that once damage is confirmed, you cut back until you reach healthy tissue — checked by cutting into the cane and looking at the colour of the pith inside.

The one genuinely important thing to flag: **damaged growth doesn't follow the same "always cut it all the way out" rule as crossing/rubbing and inward-growing stems.** Instead, how far back you cut depends on severity — a small nick gets a small trim, but if there's no living tissue left below the damage, it comes out entirely, down to the base. That's a real finding from the evidence, not an inconsistency to be smoothed over.

## 3. What was researched

Q1: what counts as "damaged" and how it differs from dead wood, and what causes it. Q2: visible signs, by cause. Q3: what a photo can and can't show. Q4: what a beginner might mistake for damage. Q5: what to do once confirmed, and how far back to cut. Q6: when to wait rather than act immediately. Q7: safety specific to this task.

## 4. How to read the strength ratings

Same three levels used throughout this project: **High** (many independent sources, no serious contradiction), **Moderate** (fewer sources, or some contradiction), **Low** (a handful of sources, thin evidence, or extrapolated from outside rose-specific literature).

## 5. What the research found

**What counts as damage, and why (Q1).** Most sources treat damage as its own category, separate from dead wood — though a few blur the two together in a quick opening instruction, relying on a separate test to sort them out in practice. Several distinct causes are named: frost/winter injury, mechanical breakage, splitting, fungal canker, pest/borer damage, and sunscald. Usefully, weak/thin growth keeps showing up as its own separate category in the sources too — good independent confirmation that treating it as a separate future observation was the right call.

**Recognising it (Q2).** Canker is the standout here — five independent sources describe the same consistent picture (discoloured, sunken, cracked bark, sometimes with visible fungal growth). Frost damage shows as browning, but confirming it really means cutting in and checking the pith colour. Mechanical damage and broken canes are visible where visible, but there's no way to spot a hidden split without cutting — a genuine gap. Pest/borer damage shows as wilting tips and entry holes, with a nice catch: one commonly-cited source on "cane borers" is actually describing a harmless wasp reusing an old pruning cut, not real pest damage.

**Photo limits and lookalikes (Q3, Q4).** No rose source addresses photo-vs-in-person diagnosis directly, so the ROC drew on general academic and extension-clinic literature: a photo can plausibly show colour and shape, but not what's going on inside the cane. Harmless galls are a well-documented lookalike for damage (three sources agree they're basically harmless, with one caveat about heavy infestations). Canker's early colouring genuinely overlaps with normal new-growth colour — real enough that one Extension expert declined to diagnose a case from photos alone.

**What to do about it (Q5).** Near-unanimous: cut back to healthy tissue, confirmed by cutting in and checking the pith is white or pale green. How far back depends on severity, not the cause of damage — minor damage gets a small trim, severe damage or no living tissue below it means full removal to the base. Canker gets extra steps (bigger margin, disinfect tools); borer damage gets a sealed cut.

**When to wait (Q6).** Frost damage often isn't fully visible right away — several sources say wait until close to spring bud break before assessing, and a second round of pruning later in spring is sometimes needed.

**Safety (Q7).** Disinfecting tools for canker/disease-suspect wood is well supported (five sources, consistent guidance). Burning or trashing (not composting) disease-affected prunings is also well supported. Handling broken or tensioned canes safely has no rose-specific guidance — only a general arboriculture principle at a much bigger scale (storm-felled trees), given as an extrapolated caution rather than sourced fact.

## 6. Where the sources differ

**C-1 — Is "damaged" always kept separate from "dead" in the wording?** Mostly yes, but a few sources fold them into one quick opening instruction. Not a real disagreement about whether they're different things — just how tersely some sources are written. **Default:** treat them as distinct, per the app's own design.

**C-2 — What does "wait for spring" actually mean for frost damage?** Some sources mean "wait to literally see what survives"; others mean "wait until just before bud break, judging by colour." **Default:** the more conservative, colour-based timing, consistent with the app's existing dormancy guidance.

**C-3 — Photo diagnosis of canker: sometimes works, sometimes doesn't, even for experts.** Two real Extension cases with similar symptoms got different outcomes — one expert diagnosed from photos, another declined. **Treatment:** not a rule to resolve, just more reason to always state uncertainty rather than present a photo-based read as certain.
