### Recognising a sucker (Brief section 5, group 1)

#### AF-1 — Where the shoot starts is the main test | Confidence: High

> Where the shoot starts is the main test: below the bud union is a sucker; at or just above it is the rose's own shoot.

[[claim:af1|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af1m|Backing and caveats|Thirteen sources across the UK, US, Australia and NZ, including the RHS, three extension services and the NZ Rose Society. Works on a dormant plant only if the union can be seen. One nursery says a leftover rootstock stub above the union can also sucker (AF-14).]]

#### AF-2 — Suckers can start under the soil | Confidence: High

> A sucker can start under the soil, sometimes away from the plant; sources say trace it down or clear soil to see where it starts.

[[claim:af2|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af2m|Backing and caveats|Eleven sources, including the RHS and University of California extension. Checking may mean digging.]]

#### AF-3 — Sucker leaves often look different | Confidence: Moderate

> Sucker leaves often look different: paler, lighter, smaller, or a different number of leaflets.

[[claim:af3|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af3m|Backing and caveats|Six sources including the RHS and Illinois Extension, all hedged ('often', 'may'). Needs leaves, so not usable in winter.]]

#### AF-4 — Leaf clues can mislead | Confidence: Moderate

> Leaf clues can mislead: many roses have seven leaflets, some rootstocks have five, and 'Dr. Huey' looks like a Hybrid Tea.

[[claim:af4|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af4m|Backing and caveats|Five sources, mostly nurseries and single authors; no RHS or extension source says it.]]

#### AF-5 — New-shoot colour (red versus pale green) | Confidence: Low

> Two sources say the rose's new shoots are usually red or bronze and rootstock shoots pale green.

[[claim:af5|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af5m|Backing and caveats|Both hedge; David Austin treats reddish growth as a reason leaf clues mislead. Not usable in winter.]]

#### AF-6 — Sucker flowers differ | Confidence: Moderate

> Sucker flowers differ from the rose's own (small, single, often white, pale pink or dark red).

[[claim:af6|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af6m|Backing and caveats|Five sources, including two extension services. Only in flowering season.]]

#### AF-7 — Suckers are very vigorous | Confidence: Moderate

> Suckers are described as very vigorous, often straight and tall.

[[claim:af7|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af7m|Backing and caveats|Five sources of different kinds. Vigour can also make a sucker look like a healthy shoot (AF-13).]]

#### AF-8 — No thorn or cane rule | Confidence: Low

> Thorniness varies between rootstocks, and no source gives a thorn or cane rule for spotting a sucker.

[[claim:af8|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af8m|Backing and caveats|Two sources describing rootstocks, not sucker recognition.]]

#### AF-9 — Rootstocks differ by country | Confidence: Moderate

> Rootstocks differ by country. New Zealand mainly uses Rosa multiflora or strains of it.

[[claim:af9|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af9m|Backing and caveats|Five sources; the New Zealand statement is one columnist's.]]

#### AF-10 — No dormant-plant method | Confidence: Low

> No source explains how to recognise a sucker on a dormant, leafless rose.

[[claim:af10|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af10m|Backing and caveats|An absence of evidence after searches aimed at it.]]

### How reliable the signs are (Brief section 5, group 2)

#### AF-11 — Own-root roses: shoots are the same rose | Confidence: High

> On an own-root rose, shoots from the base or roots are the same rose as the rest of the plant.

[[claim:af11|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af11m|Backing and caveats|Four sources including the RHS agree; they disagree on whether to call such shoots suckers (C-3).]]

#### AF-12 — Bud-union depth varies | Confidence: Moderate

### Removing a sucker (Brief section 5, group 3)

#### AF-17 — Remove where it starts, not at soil level | Confidence: High

> Remove a sucker where it starts, not at soil level, or it regrows.

[[claim:af17|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af17m|Backing and caveats|Eleven sources across all regions and source types agree.]]

#### AF-18 — Tear versus cut | Confidence: Moderate

> Sources disagree: tear or pull it off, or cut it cleanly where it starts.

[[claim:af18|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af18m|Backing and caveats|The RHS and most sources tear or pull; four sources cut. No trial compares them (C-1).]]

#### AF-19 — Why tear: removes hidden buds | Confidence: Moderate

> The reason given for tearing is that it takes away the hidden buds at the base.

[[claim:af19|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af19m|Backing and caveats|Five sources including the RHS and University of California extension; one rosarian reports the opposite experience.]]

#### AF-20 — Remove suckers early | Confidence: High

> Remove suckers early, while young.

[[claim:af20|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af20m|Backing and caveats|Six sources including the NZ Rose Society. Fine Gardening prefers to identify at first spring flowering (C-5).]]

#### AF-21 — Why remove: they drain and take over the rose | Confidence: High

> Suckers are removed because they drain the rose and can take it over.

[[claim:af21|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af21m|Backing and caveats|Seven sources across all four regions.]]

#### AF-22 — Care points come from single sources | Confidence: Low

> Care points during removal (gloves, holding the stem, refilling soil) each come from one source only.

[[claim:af22|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af22m|Backing and caveats|Each point rests on one source.]]

#### AF-23 — Lifting a rose that keeps suckering | Confidence: Low

> For a rose that keeps suckering, one presenter lifted the whole rose in winter and cut away the suckering part of the roots.

[[claim:af23|Approve;Approve with a change;Flag;Do not approve]]
[[more:af23m|Backing and caveats|One presenter, one rose.]]

## Where the sources differ

Six points of disagreement are on record (Brief section 6): tear versus cut, how dependable leaf clues are, whether own-root shoots are called suckers, how deep the bud union is planted, when to act, and whether growth above the union is always the rose's own. Each is carried in the findings as it stands, without a resolution.

[[claim:conf|Accept as recorded;Commission research;Give a direction]]
[[more:confm|The six disagreements|C-1 Tear or pull (RHS and most sources) versus cut cleanly at the start point (four sources); all agree on removing at the start point, not soil level. C-2 Some sources say sucker leaves are clearly different; others say they can look the same. C-3 Own-root shoots are the same rose; sources differ on calling them suckers. C-4 Bud union at soil level (RHS), buried (Harkness, Fine Gardening), above soil (Eastcroft), or by climate (Oregon State). C-5 Remove as soon as spotted (most) versus identify at first spring flowering (Fine Gardening). C-6 Growth above the union is the rose's own (most) versus a leftover rootstock stub can sucker (Eastcroft).]]
