# Pip's Answers — Which Months to Do Each Rose Job in New Zealand

This is everything Pip would say about which months to prune, feed, deadhead, plant, move and spray a rose in New Zealand. **You are asked for one thing: approval of these answers.** Approving them also approves the research behind them and lets them be published. Nothing else needs reading.

## 1. What gardeners will see

A new question, "Which months do I prune my rose?", joins the list of questions, second, under "When should I prune my rose?". The other five questions open from its answer, under "More about when to do each job".

Every statement below is shown only for a rose in New Zealand. Where a statement is for a smaller place, that is marked, and only a rose in that place sees it. Each statement is shown with the label in brackets. A list of the sources sits under each answer for a gardener who wants it.

### Which months do I prune my rose?

- In New Zealand, the main winter pruning is done between June and August, depending on where you live. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[High confidence]*
- Prune later where it is colder: the north starts earlier than the south. One garden company says the opposite: that cooler places can prune earlier, once all the leaves have fallen. *[High confidence]*
- Up north, one source says pruning starts around early July. Another says it can start a little before July. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in the North Island only.**
- In the colder parts of the South Island, pruning gets under way in August. In Dunedin, one source prunes in late July. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners in the South Island only.**
- In Northland, one garden company says the end of July to early August is a good time to prune. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Northland only.**
- In Auckland, one garden service says to prune in July. One garden company says June to July, before the rose bursts into leaf, which can be as early as August in Auckland. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Auckland only.**
- In Whanganui, one garden centre manager says roses are best pruned in July. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near Whanganui only.**
- In Masterton, one garden company says to prune in July to August. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near Masterton only.**
- In Canterbury, one source says pruning can begin towards the end of July in milder parts and be completed there in August. Colder areas may prefer to wait until August, and colder inland districts prune once the worst frosts have passed. It says to finish winter pruning in September. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Canterbury only.**
- In Christchurch, one garden company says to leave pruning until late winter or early spring, after the last frost. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near Christchurch only.**
- In Dunedin, one source prunes its modern roses in late July, when they are most dormant and the worst frosts are over. In a frosty spot that can mean waiting until mid-August. One garden company says June or July, and August if you are unsure. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near Dunedin only.**
- Prune in winter, when the rose is dormant and not in active growth. In colder or frosty places, wait until the worst frosts have passed. *[High confidence]*
- Don't leave pruning too late: prune before the rose bursts into growth. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Don't prune too early, because frost can damage the new growth that follows. *[High confidence]*
- Two garden companies differ on a rose that still has its leaves or flowers in June. One says to wait until it is fully dormant, with no leaves left, which may mean pruning in July. The other says a rose can be pruned even if it is still flowering. Months are only a guide. *[Sources disagree]*

### When do I start and stop feeding?

- Start feeding in spring, as new growth appears. *[High confidence]*
- In New Zealand, the first feed is usually given in September. It can be as early as late August or as late as mid to late October. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]*
- In a frosty area, one source says it is better to wait until the last frosts before you feed your roses. *[Precaution · Low confidence]*
- Sources disagree on when to stop feeding. One says no later than the end of February, so that the canes (stems) can harden before winter. Others say March or early April, and some feed through autumn. Months are only a guide. *[Sources disagree]*

### When do I stop deadheading?

- Deadhead (remove the faded flowers) while the rose is flowering. Two garden companies say when to stop: one at the end of February, and one at the end of March, leaving the last flowers so that hips (the rose's fruit) can form. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]*
- In Auckland, one garden service says to stop deadheading from March, so that the rose can form hips and rest before winter. Another Auckland source says to keep removing dead flowers all through the flowering season. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Auckland only.**
- I couldn't find when to stop summer pruning (a light prune in summer to encourage more flowers) in New Zealand. A local rose society is the place to ask. *[No source found]*

### Which months do I plant or move a rose?

- In New Zealand, winter is the best time to plant a rose, and planting can go on through spring. One garden company says June and July are the best months. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]*
- New roses for winter planting reach garden centres from June. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]*
- In Canterbury, one source says to plant roses in June, July and August, while they are dormant. July and August are its months for bare-root roses, which are sold without soil on their roots. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Canterbury only.**
- In Auckland, one source says roses are best planted in autumn or winter. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Auckland only.**
- A rose in a pot can be planted at any time of year. Keep it well watered if you plant in spring or summer. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Move an established rose in winter, while it is dormant. One garden centre page says autumn instead. *[Moderate confidence]*
- For moving a rose, one garden centre says July, after pruning and once the risk of frost has passed. One garden company says June or July. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[Low confidence]*

### When do I clear up and spray in winter?

- Clear away fallen and diseased leaves in autumn and winter, and pick up the prunings and fallen leaves after pruning. Burn them or put them in the rubbish, not the compost. *[High confidence]*
- In Canterbury, one source says to remove diseased leaves from roses in May, June and July. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Canterbury only.**
- If you use a clean-up spray in winter (a spray meant to clear pests and diseases left on the plant), put it on at pruning or just after. Some put a first spray on in June, before pruning. Months are only a guide, and the label on the spray comes first. *[Moderate confidence]*
- In New Zealand, lime sulphur, copper and a spraying oil are used as winter sprays. Follow the label on the spray. *[Moderate confidence]*
- One source says lime sulphur strips the leaves from new growth, so if you use it as a winter spray, put it on at pruning time and don't leave it too late. Follow the label on the spray. *[Precaution · Low confidence]*
- If you use lime sulphur, never mix it with other sprays, and leave two to three weeks before you use a copper spray. One garden centre says to wait three weeks before any other spray. Follow the label on the spray. *[Precaution · Moderate confidence]*
- I couldn't find New Zealand advice on covering roses or mounding soil over the base for winter. A local rose society is the place to ask. *[No source found]*

### How do I allow for frost and my own garden?

- When to prune depends on where you live. Go by your own garden too: one source says the right time can differ even within one suburb, and that a frosty spot can mean waiting longer. *[High confidence]*
- Frosts are common in the cooler months in most of New Zealand, the national climate institute says. They are rare north of Waikato, less frequent near the coast, and most frequent at inland South Island places such as Alexandra. They can still happen anywhere. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Your garden is more likely to get frost if it is on flat ground where cold air can't drain away, or in a valley or basin, the national climate institute says. Frost can vary widely over a small area. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Your local rose society can advise on timing for your own climate. *[Moderate confidence]*

## 2. What you should know

- **Each statement is shown only in the place its source speaks of.** 27 are for all of New Zealand and 13 are for a smaller place: the North Island, the South Island, Northland, Auckland, Canterbury, Christchurch, Dunedin, Whanganui or Masterton. The app works out the rose's region and nearest town on the gardener's own phone, and nothing about the position is sent anywhere. Within 10 km of another region it asks the gardener which region the rose is in.
- **This needed a new piece in the app,** and it is included. It covers the regions of all five countries, so the Australian, UK, US and Canadian months can use it as they are approved. Until then, a gardener outside New Zealand who opens the new question is told Pip has nothing for where their rose grows yet, and the app notes that they asked.
- **Two of your decisions from this work are now in the rules** as standing decisions: places inside a country, and frost said in words.
- **"Up north" is shown in the North Island.** No source says where "up north" begins, so Pip keeps the source's words and a gardener in Wellington is left to judge.
- **Many statements rest on one source or on garden companies, and say so.** 16 are shown as Low confidence. That includes every month for a named region or town. Months for feeding, for stopping deadheading and for moving a rose come almost entirely from garden companies.
- **Where sources disagree, Pip says so.** Two statements are marked "Sources disagree": when to give the last feed, and what to do with a rose that still has its leaves or flowers in June. Others lead with the better-supported view and say who differs: whether colder places prune later, whether to move a rose in winter or autumn, and whether a winter spray goes on before or after pruning.
- **Where Pip couldn't find an answer, it says so and points to a local rose society.** There are two: when to stop summer pruning, and covering or mounding roses for winter.
- **Three precautions.** One company says to wait for the last frosts before feeding in a frosty area; the page gives no reason, and I have treated it as a precaution because it only asks the gardener to wait. One source says lime sulphur strips the leaves from new growth. Three sources say to keep lime sulphur apart from other sprays, by two weeks, three weeks, or never mixed.
- **The published Spraying answer needs bringing into line.** It gives only the two weeks. That is a small follow-up after this.
- **Five findings are left out.** When roses flower by area (not about a job). That no source draws a boundary between climate areas, and that none names bud swell (both about the research). A general line on New Zealand's climate (changes nothing a gardener does). Follow-up winter sprays (the sequences differ from page to page, and the Spraying answers cover them).
- **Rewritten in plain words after your comment on 7 October.** 21 of the 40 statements were reworded so that every sentence is about the rose or what to do. Sentences that only counted sources are gone. "One source says" stays where only one source says it. Nothing new is claimed.
- **The check corrected the research before you saw it.** Every recorded quotation was on its page, but most findings were worded more widely than any one page: 27 were reworded or had their level changed. Four were lowered a level (the north's start, the time to stop deadheading and its reason, and planting a potted rose at any time) and one was raised (asking a local rose society). 11 narrower findings were added, such as Auckland's and Northland's own months, and 3 disagreements the research had not recorded.
- **One term is not explained:** "modern roses", in the Dunedin statement. It is the source's own term and no page explains it.
- **Not checked:** the signed-in screens and a real phone. The place piece was tested on 65 towns and cities in and around the five countries, and for fourteen plants in a local copy of the app.
