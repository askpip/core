# Decision Brief — Weak or Congested Growth on an Established, Dormant Bush Rose

This is the plain-language companion to `FRD-BUSHROSE-WEAKCONGESTED-01`. Like the project's more recent dossiers, it's built for one real decision, not a list of open questions.

## 1. What you are being asked to do

Read this Brief, then the Review Form asks for one thing: **approve the nineteen findings below as `ARC-BUSHROSE-WEAKCONGESTED-01`, approve with amendments, or decline.** Everything the research could close, it closed; everything it couldn't (a real gap, or a real disagreement between sources on an exact number) has a stated ROC-recommended default instead of being left for you to rule on.

## 2. The short version

This is the sixth and last of the app's six supported observations to be researched — with this dossier, the whole observation-level research programme is essentially done. "Weak or congested growth" bundles two related things the app's own design already treats as one observation: **weak** growth (thin, spindly, unproductive individual canes) and **congestion** (too many canes crowded together, needing thinning to open up the centre). Unlike the crossing/rubbing-and-inward-growth situation, this wasn't an assumption on our part — the app's architecture always defined these two as one combined observation, so researching them together was simply following the given scope. The dossier still keeps weak-growth findings and congestion findings clearly labelled throughout, just in case.

The research came back strong on what to actually do: weak canes and excess congested canes both get removed entirely at the base — no partial trimming, matching the same simple rule already found for crossing/rubbing and inward-growing stems (and notably different from damaged growth's more nuanced severity-based rule).

Where the sources genuinely disagree is on exact numbers: how thin counts as "too thin," and how many canes a bush should end up with. Every region and society has slightly different figures. Rather than pick one arbitrarily, this dossier presents these as honest ranges with a stated default.

## 3. What was researched

Q1: what "weak" and "congested" actually mean, how they relate, and how they differ from conditions already researched. Q2: visible signs and numeric thresholds. Q3: what a photo can and can't show. Q4: what a beginner might mistake for either condition. Q5: what to do once confirmed. Q6: handling a cane that's both weak and congested, and when young plants get leniency. Q7: safety specific to this task.

## 4. How to read the strength ratings

Same three levels used throughout this project: **High** (many independent sources, no serious contradiction), **Moderate** (fewer sources, or some contradiction), **Low** (a handful of sources, thin evidence, or extrapolated from outside rose-specific literature).

## 5. What the research found

**What they are, and how they differ (Q1).** Weak growth is about one cane; congestion is about the whole bush. Sources consistently keep these separate, even when mentioned in the same sentence. There's a nice finding here too: congestion at the base may actually be a *cause* of weak growth further up the plant (poor light reaching the interior) — useful context for why the app treats these as one bundled observation.

**Recognising them (Q2).** "Pencil thickness" is the near-universal shorthand for a weak cane, though the two sources that put an actual number on it disagree by nearly double (6.4mm vs 10mm) — this dossier goes with the general "about pencil thickness" description rather than picking one figure. For congestion, sources give a target cane count that genuinely varies by region — from as few as one to four canes (South Africa) up to four to eight (several US sources), with three to five the most commonly repeated number.

**Photos and lookalikes (Q3, Q4).** No source directly addresses judging cane thickness or bush density from a photo, so the ROC drew on general extension and academic literature: a size reference (coin, pencil) in the photo genuinely helps with thickness judgments. No source addresses the risk of mistaking a healthy new young cane (naturally thin) for a weak old one — the closest useful cue found is that new-season canes have brown/reddish thorns while older wood has grey thorns and grey bark, a colour-based clue rather than a thickness-based one.

**What to do about it (Q5).** Very clean finding: both weak canes and excess congested canes are removed entirely at the base, never just trimmed. This matches the "always remove fully" rule already found for crossing/rubbing and inward-growing stems — not damaged growth's more nuanced severity-based rule.

**Multiple conditions and young plants (Q6).** No source states an explicit rule for a cane that's both weak and congested, but every source that gives a step-by-step sequence removes weak canes before making the final congestion decision — so removing weak growth first naturally narrows things down. Young, still-establishing roses get leniency on the weak-growth rule, though sources disagree on exactly how long that leniency lasts (one season to three years).

**Safety (Q7).** Eye protection and gauntlet-style gloves are specifically recommended for reaching into a dense bush interior, beyond routine gloves and goggles. In hot climates, over-thinning the centre risks sunscald to the graft — sources suggest leaving some interior growth rather than clearing everything in one pass.

## 6. Where the sources differ

**C-1 — Exact weak-cane threshold.** "Pencil thickness" is agreed in concept; the two sources that quantify it disagree by nearly 2x (6.4mm vs 10mm). **Default:** describe it as "about pencil thickness" rather than committing to either figure.

**C-2 — Target cane count for congestion.** Ranges from one-to-four up to four-to-eight, depending on source and region. **Default:** present as a range (roughly three to seven for a typical bush, fewer for a modest or young plant).

**C-3 — How cautious to be about heavy thinning.** Extension sources say go lightly on a plant that's already weak or small; rose societies encourage confident pruning generally. **Default:** apply the caution specifically to a plant that already looks weak or small, without being unduly conservative for a healthy one — these aren't actually contradictory once read this way.

## 7. What's genuinely left open, and what's been given a default instead

Nothing is left open without a stated default. The seven questions the research couldn't fully answer (the exact cause of weak growth; whether weak canes visually flop; whether "twiggy centre" is even the right term for congestion — it turns out not to be; distinguishing healthy density from overcrowding; photo-based thickness judgment; why removing weak canes first helps; avoiding cutting the wrong cane by mistake) each get a plain, clearly-labelled ROC recommendation in FRD §9.
