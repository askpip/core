# Decision Brief — Recognisable Rose-Type Labels on Retail Stock (New Zealand, Australia, UK, US)

This is the plain-language companion to `FRD-BUSHROSE-ROSETYPE-LABELS-01`. It adds no evidence, changes no finding and changes no confidence level. If the Brief and the FRD differ, the FRD governs.

## 1. What you are being asked to do

Read this Brief, then the Review Form asks for your decision on each of the 29 findings, on the ways the evidence conflicts or stays open, on five further-research items, and on four other decision points — finishing with one overall decision: **accept these findings for incorporation into `ARC-BUSHROSE-ROSETYPE-01`, accept with amendments, request further work, or decline.**

## 2. The short version

This commission answers a gap Shaphan identified in the earlier rose-type research (`FRD-BUSHROSE-ROSETYPE-01`): that dossier described how horticultural authorities distinguish rose classes, but not what a gardener would actually see printed on a tag, listing or product name when buying one. Because Ask Pip's location feature and undecided launch market mean any of New Zealand, Australia, the United Kingdom or the United States could be relevant, this commission researched all four, in that priority order, keeping each market's findings separate.

The headline result: **no market is consistent, and no retailer's own site is always consistent with itself.** "Bush rose" ranges from a word some retailers avoid altogether, to a precise bucket matching Hybrid Tea and Floribunda almost exactly, to a loose catch-all that includes groundcover, patio, shrub and climbing roses under the same label — sometimes on the very same retailer's website. Two internal inconsistencies were found and are recorded, not resolved: Bunnings New Zealand's own "Bush Roses" page gave two different pictures on two fetches, and one Australian nursery's product page tags the same rose as both "Hybrid tea" and "Grandiflora" at once.

"Grandiflora" shows the sharpest market split found in either commission: it is a standard, explicitly named category at every United States retailer checked, essentially absent from New Zealand and Australian retail, and inconsistently present in the United Kingdom. This bears directly on a decision point already open and undecided in `FRD-BUSHROSE-ROSETYPE-01` — whether Grandiflora stays in Ask Pip's approved scope at all.

The one question this commission could not answer in any of the four markets, despite a genuinely thorough search (especially in the United States, where it seemed most likely to be findable): **what is actually printed on the physical tag or pot label a gardener holds in their hand.** That gap, first flagged in the earlier dossier for New Zealand alone, is now confirmed across all four markets.

## 3. What was researched

**Q1.** What type-related words appear on physical tags/labels in each market (largely unanswered — see above). **Q2.** What type-related words appear in each market's retailer listings and product descriptions (the bulk of what was found). **Q3.** Where a term like "bush rose" is used inconsistently, what distinguishes its precise and loose uses in practice. **Q4.** Where markets or retailers differ, is that recorded explicitly rather than blended away. **Q5.** Assembling all of this, market by market, into a definitive list mapped to the approved scope, and recording what remains unresolvable.

Not covered, by design: cultivar identification, pruning technique, image sourcing, questionnaire wording, and any market beyond these four.

## 4. How to read the strength ratings

| Level | Meaning in this dossier |

| High | Reliable and generally consistent across independent sources |
| Moderate-High | Reliable, with one specific limitation noted |
| Moderate | Reasonably supported, with notable limitations or some inconsistency |
| Low-Moderate | Real but thin evidence — one source, or a single short statement |
| Not Assigned | A negative finding (nothing was found), or the ROC's own reasoned synthesis across several findings, which is never scored as if it were a directly sourced fact (Evidence Assessment Standard §2.10) |

## 6. Where the sources conflict, and what stays open

**C-1 — Bunnings NZ's own page gave two different pictures (AF-3).** One fetch suggested groundcover/climbing are separate from "Bush Roses"; another showed them co-mingled on the same page. Not resolved — needs a manual check.

**C-2 — One Australian nursery tags a rose as two classes at once (AF-11).** Wagner's "Beautiful Girl" is filed under "Hybrid tea" and "Grandiflora" simultaneously. Preserved as an example, not resolved.

**C-3 — Retailer-level inconsistency, not market-level uniformity, is the dominant pattern (AF-26).** Within every single market, at least one retailer is comparatively precise and at least one is comparatively loose. No simple "this market is precise, that market is loose" rule is supported by the evidence.

## 8. Where the type comes from: what this means for the app

This section describes what the findings could support later. It is not part of the research and decides nothing.

- A **stated type** — a precise word like "Hybrid Tea," "Floribunda" or "Grandiflora" appearing on a tag, receipt, listing or product title — is the strongest signal found in any market (AF-1, AF-8, AF-21, AF-22, AF-23, AF-24).
- The word **"bush" alone**, without an accompanying named type, is not a reliable signal anywhere: every market has at least one retailer where it means something looser than Hybrid Tea/Floribunda (AF-3, AF-4, AF-11, AF-15, AF-16, AF-18).
- **Grandiflora's market-dependent visibility** (AF-27) may matter more for how Pip behaves in the US than in NZ/Australia, pending the still-open decision on whether Grandiflora is in scope at all.
- **Questionnaire wording, the qualifying gate and any comparison-image list** follow from an approved ARC and are separate work.
