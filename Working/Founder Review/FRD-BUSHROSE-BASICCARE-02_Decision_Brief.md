## 2. The short version

**The research.** We asked what a bush rose is, what ordinary care across a year looks like apart from pruning, what care suits a rose not yet ready for pruning, what winter protection involves, and what simple, non-diagnostic signs of poor health are worth a gardener's attention. Round 1 read 11 sources; Round 2 added a 12th and checked several further candidates that didn't pan out (see section 7).

**The result, in numbers.** 13 findings (F1–F13, F1a a usage note rather than a finding). Confidence, after Round 2: **1 Very High, 6 High (some only for a general pattern, not a specific figure), 4 Moderate, 1 Low, 1 Low–Moderate**, plus F1a (no rating — an observation, not a claim of fact). F11 moved from Low to Moderate this round; nothing else changed rating.

**What the sources agree on strongly.** Winter protection by mounding soil, compost or mulch 10–12 inches around the base after hard frost (F9, Very High). Deep, infrequent watering (F2); growing-season mulching 2–3 inches deep (F3); general pest and disease watchfulness (F6); the general pattern of feeding starting in spring and stopping by mid-to-late summer (F4); the general pattern of deadheading stopping in early-to-mid autumn (F5); and that a newly planted rose should be left largely uncut in its first season so it can establish (F7) — all High. **New this round:** pre-winter watering and debris removal (F11) now has two independent sources, one of them specifying watering to 8–12 inches every 2–3 weeks — raised to Moderate.

**Where the ground is thinner.** Exactly what "bush rose" means as a term appears to be UK-specific (F1, Moderate, and see F1a below — Round 2 checked further and found the same pattern). Additional winter-protection methods like collars and wrapping (F10, Moderate). Simple, non-diagnostic signs of poor health, and the idea that ordinary care matters more than pests or disease (F12, F13, Moderate and Low–Moderate) — Round 2 checked three further sources specifically for this and found nothing that changes the picture; this appears to be a genuine thinness in the published literature, not a gap in how hard anyone looked.

**A genuine and useful observation, not a finding.** None of the US or Australian sources checked (Round 1 or Round 2) used "bush rose" as a category term at all. This may affect the words Ask Pip should use with gardeners outside the UK (see Decision 5 below).

**No real disagreement found.** Three things that looked at first like disagreements between sources (feeding cutoff dates, deadheading cutoff dates, mulch depth) turned out, on inspection, to be regional variation or two different practices being conflated, not genuine conflict. Details in section 5 below.

## 3. What was researched

**The commission.** Five questions about basic bush rose care, commissioned by Shaphan on 22 September 2026, because a rose not yet ready for pruning still has a gardener who wants to do something for it. A second round, commissioned the same day, pursued five follow-up items directly rather than leaving them for a future commission.

**In scope.** What a bush rose is; ordinary year-round care apart from pruning; care for a rose not yet ready for pruning; winter protection; simple, non-diagnostic signs of poor health.

**Out of scope (both rounds).** Pruning mechanics (a separate, already-progressed commission); diagnosis of specific diseases or pests; product or chemical brand recommendations; planting a new rose; climate-specific schedules presented as universal.

**The sources.** 12 sources total: the Royal Horticultural Society (UK), nine US university extension services (Iowa, Illinois, Missouri, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Georgia, New York, Maryland, Nebraska), plus one trade publication (Fine Gardening, citing a named professional rosarian) used only because its claim is independently corroborated by two institutional sources.

**A limitation that remains, checked directly in Round 2.** All 12 sources are from the UK or the continental US. Round 2 specifically searched for sources from other regions and climates and found what's readily available there is mostly commercial or enthusiast content, not the kind of institutional source this commission otherwise relies on. This is now a real decision for you (Decision 2, section 9) rather than an open-ended wish.

## 4. How to read the strength ratings

This commission uses the full six-level Evidence Confidence Level scale (Very High, High, Moderate, Low, Very Low, Not Assigned), the same scale defined by the Evidence Assessment Standard and used across the Knowledge Curation System. Each finding carries its own rating, on its own; none is averaged or blended with another.

| Rating | Roughly means |
|---|---|
| **Very High** | Many independent, reliable sources, strongly and specifically consistent. |
| **High** | Multiple independent, reliable sources agree. |
| **Moderate** | Plausible support, but limited independent corroboration, or some inconsistency. |
| **Low** | A single source, or weak corroboration. |
| **Very Low** | Minimal or contradictory evidence. (Not used in this commission.) |
| **Not Assigned** | The evidence doesn't allow a rating — for example, because the underlying pattern is real but the specific figure itself varies by region rather than being one fixed fact. |

A rating is a judgement, not a formula, and it says how firmly recognised sources support a statement — not whether the statement is right for Pip to use.

## 5. What the research found

The same findings, with the same IDs, are in the Review Form. The formal wording of each is in FRD section 7.

### What a bush rose is (F1, F1a)

| ID | What sources say | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | UK authority (RHS) uses "bush rose" to mean modern shrub-form roses — chiefly Hybrid Tea and Floribunda — distinct from climbing/rambling, standard, and species/shrub roses. | **Moderate** |
| F1a | *(Usage note, not a finding.)* None of the US or Australian sources checked (both rounds) used "bush rose" as a category term; they used "Hybrid Tea," "Floribunda," "rose bush," etc. directly. This appears to be a UK-specific term rather than a universal one — Round 2 checked further and found the same pattern. | *Not rated* |

### Ordinary care across a year (F2–F6)

| ID | What sources say | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| F2 | Established bush roses are watered deeply and infrequently, roughly 1–2 inches a week when rainfall is short, with extra care in the first two years. | **High** |
| F3 | A 2–3 inch layer of organic mulch, kept a few inches clear of the stem, is commonly recommended during the growing season. | **High** |
| F4 | Feeding commonly starts in spring and stops by mid-to-late summer so growth can harden before winter — the general pattern is well supported, but the exact cutoff date varies by region and isn't a single fact. | **High** (pattern) / **Not Assigned** (any one date) |
| F5 | Deadheading is commonly stopped in early-to-mid autumn to let the plant set hips and head toward dormancy — again, the pattern is solid, the exact date varies. | **High** (pattern) / **Low** (any one date) |
| F6 | Common problems to watch for: blackspot, powdery mildew, aphids, spider mites; water at the base or in the morning to reduce fungal disease. | **High** |

### A rose not yet ready for pruning (F7)

| ID | What sources say | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| F7 | A rose planted in roughly the past year is generally left largely uncut through its first season — only spent blooms removed, not stems for arrangements — so it can put its energy into establishing. | **High** |

*Round 2 checked this against the already-approved RECENTPLANT and DORMANCY findings directly (previously an open recommendation). No conflict — but worth knowing: RECENTPLANT's "three or more years" is about when a rose is ready for full pruning; F2's "roughly two years" here is about how long watering needs extra attention. Different questions, easily confused if not kept distinct.*

### Winter protection (F9–F11)

| ID | What sources say | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| F9 | After several hard frosts and dormancy, mound soil, compost or mulch 10–12 inches around the base (covering the graft union), removed again in spring. | **Very High** |
| F10 | Additional methods — a filled collar, or wrapping canes with burlap/evergreen boughs — especially for climbers or exposed sites. | **Moderate** |
| F11 | Watering thoroughly after first frost but before the ground freezes, and clearing fallen leaf debris, as part of winter prep. **New this round:** a second source specifies watering to a depth of 8–12 inches every 2–3 weeks, and gives the same "overwintering fungal spores" reasoning for debris removal. | **Moderate** *(raised from Low)* |

### Simple, non-diagnostic signs of poor health (F12–F13)

| ID | What sources say | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| F12 | Regularly noticing yellowing, spotting, leaf drop, stunted growth, distorted leaves or reduced flowering is described as the useful first step — before trying to identify a specific cause. Round 2 checked three further sources specifically for this framing; none offered it independently of the source below. | **Moderate** |
| F13 | At least one source states plainly that ordinary care and conditions — not pests or disease — are the most common cause of a rose's decline, especially in newer plantings. | **Low–Moderate** |

## 6. Where evidence looked like it might disagree

Three things were investigated because they looked, at first, like disagreements. None turned out to be a real conflict.

| What it looked like | What it actually is |
|---|---|
| Different feeding cutoff dates (mid-August vs. September) | Regional variation tracking each area's own first-frost timing, not disagreement about the underlying reasoning. |
| Different deadheading cutoff dates | Same — regional variation, same underlying reasoning (let the plant set hips and go dormant). |
| Very different mulch depths (2–3 inches vs. 10–12 inches) | Not a disagreement at all — the small figure is ordinary growing-season mulch, the large figure is the separate practice of winter mounding. Kept as two separate findings (F3, F9) so they aren't confused. |
