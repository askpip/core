## 0. Note on This Revision

**Why this is one of two dossiers, not one.** This dossier and its companion, `FRD-BUSHROSE-INWARDGROWTH-01`, were researched together under a single Research Commission Record, because the Founder's working expectation was that an inward-growing stem would often turn out to be one that crosses another. That expectation is not itself evidence, and the ROC tested it directly rather than assuming it (Q1, below). The research found — like a related question tested during the pruning-framework commission — that no source anywhere states an occurrence rate for this, and that only direct, real-world observation over time could ever settle it; it is recorded as a permanent limitation, not a pending search item. Since the premise for combining the two observations did not hold up, and since the Bush Rose V1 Architecture always treated crossing/rubbing stems and inward-growing stems as two separate supported observations, each needing its own Observation PKR, the research is presented as two separate dossiers rather than one combined one. This required reorganising already-completed findings, not new research. A small number of findings that genuinely apply to both observations (photographic limits, AF-4; general deferral timing, AF-8) are stated here and in the companion dossier alike, rather than arbitrarily assigned to just one.

**What was commissioned and done.** See the Research Commission Record (§2) for the full commissioning history. In brief: four parallel research passes covered definitions and indicators; photographic limits and lookalikes; pruning choices and stem selection; and task-specific safety, across the same vetted source pool used successfully elsewhere in this project, supplemented by general arboriculture and plant-diagnosis literature where rose-specific sources were expected to be thin.

**Outcome, in brief, for this dossier's subject (crossing or rubbing stems).** The core question — what to do once a crossing or rubbing stem is confirmed — is answered with the strongest evidence in either dossier (AF-5, High). Recognition and photographic-assessment questions came back thinner. Two genuine, close-to-complete gaps were found (what a rubbing wound actually looks like; task-specific safety for reaching into tangled growth) and each is closed here with a stated ROC-recommended default (§9) rather than left open.

## 6. Managing Conflicting Evidence and Uncertainty

### C-1 — Whether an open, uncrowded bush centre is a universally desirable goal

**What the sources say.** The great majority of sources treat an open, airy centre — achieved partly by removing crossing/rubbing stems — as self-evidently desirable, for air circulation and disease prevention. One source, the Rose Society of South Australia's "Pruning Roses: A Home Gardener's Guide" (Doug Gregory), explicitly argues against deliberately opening the centre, on the basis that a healthy plant fills gaps on its own and interior canes provide beneficial shading — a genuine disagreement recorded within the same society's own publications (its other page endorses the conventional open-centre shape).

**Reasons given.** The majority's reason (air circulation, disease prevention) is stated repeatedly; the dissenting source's reasoning (self-filling, beneficial shading) is stated but not tested against the majority's rationale by any source.

**Treatment.** Preserved as unresolved. AF-1 records the majority position, on the strength of the many sources supporting it. A practical default is stated at §9.

### C-2 — Selection criterion for which of two crossing canes to remove

**What the sources say.** Of roughly sixteen sources instructing removal of crossing/rubbing canes, thirteen give no selection criterion at all. Two give an explicit, vigour-based criterion: UF/IFAS Leon County Extension says to remove "the weakest"; the Portland Rose Society guide, addressing the closely related case of crowded canes, says to keep "the larger one of each crowded pair." `FRD-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-04` (AF-33) separately records a further tie-break rule from a different source.

**Reasons given.** Not given by any source.

**Treatment.** Preserved as a genuine thinness in the evidence. AF-7 records the shared direction (favour vigour) at Low confidence. A practical default is stated at §9.

### C-3 — Standard (tree-form) roses versus bush roses on crossing canes

**What the sources say.** The Rose Society of South Australia's "Rose Pruning – Main Points" states that for standard (tree-form) roses, crossing canes in the head are "generally not a problem," in contrast with the conventional bush-rose instruction to remove them, because growers are prioritising bloom count over an open structure.

**Treatment.** A scope boundary, not a live conflict within this dossier: standard/tree-form roses are outside the bush-rose scope this dossier and `FRD-BUSHROSE-SCOPE-01` both define. Recorded as AF-3 for completeness.

## 8. Evidence Assessment

### AF-1 — Crossing and rubbing stems are treated by most sources as a related pair

**Question:** Q1
**Assessed Finding:** Where sources address crossing and rubbing stems at all, the great majority treat them as a linked pair — often in the same sentence or instruction — rather than as two fully independent conditions with separate rationale.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** RHS (both pages): "dead, diseased, rubbing and crossing stems" / "spindly and crossing stems." Kansas City Rose Society names both, with rubbing's disease-entry consequence stated explicitly. Oregon State University Extension / University of Illinois Extension (shared text, not independent): "Remove any crossing or rubbing canes. These tend to promote damage to the canes, and that encourages disease." Texas A&M AgriLife (general, with a rose section): "remove one of two canes which may be rubbing one another."

**Strengths.** Independent institutional sources across UK, US and general-arboriculture publisher types converge on treating the two as linked.

**Limitations.** Several frequently-cited sources (Clemson, Arkansas) address crossing but never use the word "rubbing," so the pairing is not universal.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether sources that omit "rubbing" consider it implied, or genuinely do not address stem-to-stem contact, was not tested.

---

### AF-2 — Rubbing contact damages cane tissue and creates a disease-entry point

**Question:** Q1/Q2
**Assessed Finding:** Where sources give a reason for removing rubbing stems, the reason given is that contact between canes damages the cane tissue, which allows disease to enter.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** Kansas City Rose Society: "Canes that rub against each other cause damage to the cane tissue which allows for disease entry." Oregon State University Extension / University of Illinois Extension (shared text): "These tend to promote damage to the canes, and that encourages disease." A non-institutional lead, growingthehomegarden.com, independently describes rubbing as bark-on-bark contact causing wounds — consistent with, but not counted toward, the institutional finding.

**Strengths.** Two independent institutional statements agree on the same causal mechanism.

**Limitations.** Only two independent institutional sources; most sources that instruct removal give no reason. No source describes what the resulting damage actually looks like (U-1).

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether the disease-entry risk has been measured, or is a stated but untested rationale, was not addressed.

---

### AF-3 — For standard (tree-form) roses, one source treats crossing canes as acceptable

**Question:** Q1/Q5
**Assessed Finding:** For standard (tree-form) roses specifically — outside this dossier's bush-rose scope — one source states that crossing canes in the head are "generally not a problem," because growers are prioritising bloom count over an open structure.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low** (single source)

**Evidence.** Rose Society of South Australia, "Rose Pruning – Main Points" (Tony Hanna): "canes that are crossing over here are generally not a problem, because we are looking for maximum effect in the number of blooms," contrasted with the same source's bush-rose guidance.

**Strengths.** Stated clearly and with a reason.

**Limitations.** Single source; concerns a rose growth-form outside this dossier's and `FRD-BUSHROSE-SCOPE-01`'s scope.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None beyond scope; recorded for completeness and future reference.

### AF-4 — A single photograph has real limits for judging structural questions of this kind

**Question:** Q3
**Assessed Finding:** No rose-specific source addresses what a photograph can or cannot establish about crossing or rubbing stems — the literature is uniformly written for a gardener standing in front of the plant. Drawing on general professional pruning practice and one directly relevant academic study, the ROC's own synthesis is that a single, fixed-viewpoint photograph has real limits for judging whether two stems that appear to intersect are actually in contact — the kind of 3D structural question professional practice addresses by physically viewing a plant from multiple sides before cutting. **This finding is stated identically in the companion dossier, `FRD-BUSHROSE-INWARDGROWTH-01`, since the underlying evidence concerns structural photo-assessment generally, not this observation specifically.**
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low** (a ROC synthesis of general, non-rose-specific evidence, extrapolated per EAS §2.10)

**Evidence.** NC State Extension, "General Pruning Techniques" (Barbara Fair): "step back and look over the plant from all sides." University of Missouri Extension, Master Gardener Core Manual Ch. 8: "Walk around the plant to view it from all sides," in the same passage instructing gardeners to look for crossing/rubbing wood. Berland et al. (2019), *Forests* 10(4):349, a peer-reviewed urban-forestry study: "virtual surveys are not appropriate for detailed assessments of tree risk because the analyst cannot examine the tree up close or from all angles," with structural measures found less reliable from single-viewpoint imagery.

**Strengths.** The extension guidance independently and consistently treats multi-angle physical viewing as standard practice; the academic study directly tests the underlying principle, even though it concerns urban trees, not roses.

**Limitations.** No source — rose-specific or general — states this principle for crossing/rubbing stems specifically. The academic study concerns tree risk assessment broadly. Whether a second photograph specifically resolves the ambiguity was not tested (U-5).

**Remaining uncertainty.** How much this matters for a rose bush (far smaller and more easily viewed from multiple angles than an urban tree) was not addressed by any source.

---

### AF-5 — Once confirmed, a crossing or rubbing stem is removed as routine dormant-season maintenance

**Question:** Q5
**Assessed Finding:** With near-unanimous support across the source base, once a crossing or rubbing stem is confirmed, it is removed as ordinary dormant-season pruning — not left, deferred, or referred to a professional.
**Evidence Confidence Level: High**

**Evidence.** Supported by approximately sixteen sources across independent publisher types (UK national charity, US national and regional/local societies, US university extension services, a federal forestry agency, general arboriculture sources), including RHS, Kansas City Rose Society, Clemson, University of Arkansas, Virginia Cooperative Extension, Texas A&M AgriLife, the American Rose Society's "Ten Principles" (Robert B. Martin Jr.), USDA Forest Service, University of Missouri Extension, UF/IFAS Leon County, and Johnson County K-State Extension. All instruct removal in essentially the same terms.

**Strengths.** Broad, independent corroboration across every publisher type relied upon in this project, unanimous direction.

**Limitations.** Several sources give no reasoning beyond the instruction itself. Standard (tree-form) roses are a stated exception, outside scope (AF-3, C-3).

**Remaining uncertainty.** None of substance; this is the best-attested finding in either dossier from this commission.

---

### AF-6 — Removal is a full cut back to the stem's point of origin, not a shortening

**Question:** Q5
**Assessed Finding:** Where sources specify where the cut is made, a crossing or rubbing stem is removed entirely — back to the ground, the bud union, or the larger cane it originates from — not merely shortened.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** Kansas City Rose Society: "removed to the ground or back to the next outward facing bud." American Rose Society's "Ten Principles" (Martin): "flush with the bud union or... flush with the cane from which they have decided to grow." Portland Rose Society: "removed entirely back to the bud union, or back to the major cane from which they originated." University of Missouri Extension (G6870) and University of Arkansas Extension (FSA-6149) both define this as a "thinning" or "removal" cut in their general shrub-pruning terminology, applied to crossing branches specifically. USDA Forest Service concurs for general crown thinning.

**Strengths.** Six independent sources, unanimous in direction, including two general-terminology sources that define the cut type explicitly.

**Limitations.** Not every source that instructs removal specifies exactly where the cut is made.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None beyond what is stated; consistent with, and does not duplicate, `FRD-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-04`'s general cut-mechanics findings.

---

### AF-7 — Where an explicit tie-break rule is given for two crossing canes, it favours the stronger/more vigorous cane

**Question:** Q5
**Assessed Finding:** The great majority of sources that instruct removing a crossing or rubbing cane give no criterion at all for choosing which of the two. Among the small number that do, the criterion is vigour- or size-based — remove the weaker, keep the stronger.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low**

**Evidence.** UF/IFAS Leon County Extension (Taylor Vandiver): "the weakest of those branches should be removed." Portland Rose Society (addressing the closely related case of crowded canes): "usually leaving the larger one of each crowded pair." A professional tree-care source (Glacier's End Forestry, not counted toward the source total) illustrates that even arboriculture professionals apply case-by-case judgement rather than one settled rule. This corroborates and extends `FRD-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-04`'s AF-33, which records a tie-break rule from a further, different source not duplicated here.

**Strengths.** Two independent sources agree on the general direction, corroborating a third, separately-recorded finding in the companion pruning-framework dossier.

**Limitations.** Only two sources give any explicit rule at all; of roughly sixteen sources addressing crossing-cane removal, thirteen give none. Neither source frames the rule in terms of growth direction toward the centre, which was the framing this commission's background section originally expected; no source using that framing was found.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether "weakest" and "growing toward the centre" often coincide in practice was not addressed by any source and is the ROC's own plausible inference, not a finding.

---

### AF-8 — Deferral guidance in the literature concerns general pruning timing, not this observation specifically

**Question:** Q6
**Assessed Finding:** Where sources advise waiting or deferring a pruning decision, the reason given is general dormant-season timing (avoiding cold-damage dieback, waiting until buds break enough to distinguish live from dead wood) — not anything specific to a confirmed crossing or rubbing stem. **This finding is stated identically in the companion dossier, `FRD-BUSHROSE-INWARDGROWTH-01`.**
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** Oregon State University Extension: recommends waiting until mid-month or later, or until after severe freezes depending on region. Iowa State University Extension (Linn County): "Wait until the leaf buds are a half inch or so long so it's easier to decipher what's dead and what's not." Colorado State University Extension / Ask Extension: recommends a phased approach — light pruning in autumn, comprehensive structural pruning deferred to spring.

**Strengths.** Three independent university-extension sources agree on the general principle and its reasoning.

**Limitations.** None of the three ties deferral to crossing or rubbing specifically.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether a gardener should ever defer specifically because they are unsure which of two crossing canes to remove was not addressed by any source (U-3, U-4).

## 9. Recommendations for Further Research, and Practical Defaults

Each unresolved question from §7.2 is given a stated, ROC-recommended practical default rather than left open. Each is clearly the ROC's own recommendation under EAS §2.10, not a sourced finding; any Founder may override any of them at any time; none blocks approval.

**U-1 — What an actual rubbing wound looks like.** No source describes this. **Default:** treat visible bark damage, wear, or a flattened contact face where two canes visibly touch as sufficient confirmation of "rubbing"; where stems are close but show no visible contact or wear, treat the condition as "crossing" rather than "rubbing." This draws on ordinary meaning and one non-institutional lead, not strong evidence.

**U-2 — Beginner lookalikes and confounders.** No source names any. **Default:** no specific confounder list is offered; the existing requirement to confirm any observation on the physical rose before acting is the primary safeguard.

**U-3 — Priority when a stem is both crossing/rubbing and inward-growing.** Not addressed by any source. **No default is needed**, because this is not, in practice, a live conflict: every source found that addresses either condition recommends the same action (cut).

**U-4 — Whether professional/experienced help is ever specifically warranted.** No source ties escalation to this observation specifically. **Default:** governed by the app's general uncertainty/confidence gates, not by a rule specific to crossing or rubbing.

**U-5 — Whether a second photograph from a different angle would help.** Not addressed by any source. **Default:** where a first photograph leaves confidence low on a structural judgment of this kind (AF-4), request a second photograph from a different angle as reasonable operational practice.

**U-6 — Task-specific safety for reaching into congested/tangled growth.** Close to entirely absent. **Default:** cross-refer to `ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01`'s approved generic safety content as the primary guidance; additionally, by analogy from the one adjacent finding available (Clemson, climbing roses): take care not to damage a stem being kept while removing one being discarded from tangled growth, and consider cutting a tangled stem in sections rather than pulling it whole. Explicitly extrapolated from a non-bush-rose context, Low-confidence by nature.
