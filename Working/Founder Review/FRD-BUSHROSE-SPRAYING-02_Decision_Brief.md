# Decision Brief — Spraying Roses: Whether, When and How Often (Bush Roses)

This is the plain-language companion to `FRD-BUSHROSE-SPRAYING-02`. It adds no evidence, changes no finding and changes no confidence level. If the Brief and the FRD differ, the FRD governs.

## 1. What you are being asked to do

## 2. What Pip would say

This is what the Pip Knowledge Rules give from the findings, for a gardener who asks "Should I spray my roses?" Pip presents each point as what sources say, names the sources, and shows the rating. A rating shows how strong the evidence is, not how much the point matters.

**Do I need to spray?**

- Roses can be grown well with little or no spraying, including in New Zealand. (**High**)
- Choosing a disease-tolerant variety is a main way to reduce disease. (**High**) Claimed resistance may not last, and may vary by region. (**Moderate**)
- Many popular Hybrid Teas and Floribundas are susceptible to disease. (**Moderate**)
- Several sources treat pesticides as a last resort, after other measures. (**High**)
- **Sources disagree.** The better-supported view is the one above: roses can be grown well with little or no spraying, and pesticides are a last resort. Against it, an American Rose Society article, the University of Maryland Extension and the Rose Society of NSW say susceptible roses need regular fungicide to stay healthy. (**Moderate**) The RHS goes the other way and recommends that gardeners don't use fungicides on roses at all. (**High**, as one organisation's position)

**What to do before thinking about a spray**

- Clear fallen leaves and infected stems, especially before and over winter. This reduces disease the next season. (**Very High**)
- Remove infected leaves and shoots as soon as they are seen. (**High**)
- Put diseased leaves and prunings in the rubbish, not the compost. (**Moderate**) The RHS also allows burying fallen leaves under mulch.
- Water at the base and keep the leaves dry. (**High**) Powdery mildew is the exception: water on the leaves can reduce it, though leaves that stay wet for long may favour other diseases. (**Moderate**)
- Sun, spacing and air movement reduce leaf disease. (**High**)
- Well-watered, well-fed roses are described as better at resisting disease. (**Moderate**)
- Black spot is the most common fungal disease of roses, and it depends on leaves staying wet. (**Moderate**)

**Aphids**

- Tolerate low numbers, squash them or hose them off, and rely on natural enemies. (**High**)
- Insecticidal soaps and horticultural oils are described as lower-impact sprays for aphids. (**Moderate**)
- Broad-spectrum insecticides harm natural enemies and can lead to repeat outbreaks. (**High**)

**Which sprays the sources name for leaf disease**

- **Oils (including neem oil), sulfur and potassium bicarbonate.** Three US universities name these lower-toxicity types for black spot and powdery mildew. (**High**)
- **Copper.** Two US universities and ABC Gardening Australia name copper sprays for black spot. (**Moderate**) The University of California doesn't list it.
- **Synthetic fungicides.** Three US universities name them, most often chlorothalonil. (**High**) Pip says these are US sources, and that the research did not establish which are approved for home gardeners in other countries.
- **Home-made sprays. Sources disagree,** and neither view is clearly better supported, so Pip gives both. Clemson University and ABC Gardening Australia suggest a baking soda mix. The RHS says home-made products are not recommended, because they are unregulated and usually untested. (**Moderate**)
- The RHS recommends not using fungicides on roses at all, organic types included. (**High**, as one organisation's position)
- Pip names the type and never a brand or an amount. It tells the gardener to use only a product labelled for roses where they live, and to follow its label.

**If the gardener chooses to spray**

- Fungicides for leaf diseases protect leaves before infection. They don't cure it. (**High**)
- Where sources describe regular spraying, it runs from the first new leaves after winter through the growing season. (**Moderate**) More is needed in wet weather, where there is a history of disease, or in a heavy outbreak. (**Moderate**)
- Sources give repeat intervals from weekly to about every two weeks. The product label sets the interval. (**Moderate**)
- Sources that spray advise alternating products, to slow resistance. (**High**)
- **Sources disagree** on whether to spray regularly through the season or only when a problem appears. Neither view is clearly better supported, so Pip gives both.

**A winter spray after pruning: shown to New Zealand and Australian gardeners only**

- Rose sources in New Zealand and Australia describe a winter clean-up spray after pruning. (**Moderate**) It goes on once prunings and fallen leaves have been cleared, onto the plant and the ground around it. (**Moderate**)
- **The sprays they name:**
  - **lime sulphur**: the New Zealand Rose Society, ABC Gardening Australia and three rose nurseries (**Moderate**);
  - **copper, often with a spraying oil**: the New Zealand Rose Society, Tui, South Pacific Roses and Ross Roses (**Moderate**);
  - **a spraying oil**, with or after the other sprays: the same four sources (**Moderate**).
- How strongly sources put it ranges from "highly recommended" (New Zealand Rose Society) to "you might also like to" (ABC Gardening Australia).
- No source was found that tests whether a winter spray makes a difference. Pip says so.
- **UK and US gardeners:** the UK and US institutional sources checked don't describe a spray after pruning. Pip says so, and gives the winter clear-up only. For US gardeners, Clemson University says copper fungicides can be used on dormant bushes; Pip says only one source was found. (**Low**)

**Safety, whenever a spray is used**

- Follow the product label for which product, how much and how often. In the UK this is a legal requirement. (**Very High**)
- Don't spray open flowers, or when bees are foraging. One New Zealand source says to spray only in the evening. (**High**)
- Which products may be sold and used is set by each country's regulator, and approvals change. (**Moderate**)
- Precautions. Each is rated **Low** because few sources give it, and Pip includes it because it is a precaution:
  - wear gloves and safety equipment, and clean the sprayer after use (the RHS and one nursery);
  - don't spray in the heat (two nurseries);
  - don't use lime sulphur and copper within two weeks of each other (New Zealand Rose Society);
  - don't mix lime sulphur and oil, or spray them together (one New Zealand rose nursery);
  - don't put oil on a plant that is short of water, or within two weeks of a sulfur spray (University of California).

**Where no source was found**

- **Which disease-tolerant varieties suit New Zealand.** Pip names none and points the gardener to a local rose society.
- **Whether the need to spray differs between New Zealand regions.** Pip says no source compares regions, and that sources tie leaf disease to wet, humid weather.
- **Which spray types are currently approved for home gardeners in each country.** Pip tells the gardener to use only a product labelled for roses where they live, and to follow its label.

## 3. What Pip would not say, and why

- **Four findings stay on record only.** Each is rated Low, and is not local information, a precaution or a gap:
  - that trying to keep a rose completely disease-free can mean more spraying than is justified (three brief statements, each about a different situation);
  - what a winter spray is said to achieve (two broadcast segments; no source measures it);
  - that a gardener should ask a local rose society or garden centre before spraying (one general-interest website);
  - that copper washes off in rain and may need reapplying (one nursery).
- **Brand names and mixing amounts.** The research doesn't collect them. The label governs them. This includes the amounts in a home-made mix, which has no label.
- **Sprays for other pests.** Two US university pages also name sprays for spider mites, scale, thrips, caterpillars and beetles. The research was commissioned on black spot, powdery mildew, rust and aphids, so these were not collected.
- **Four questions the ROC raised from its own reasoning.** They are not evidence. None is used.

## 4. What was researched

Q1: does a rose need spraying at all, and what about resistant varieties? Q2: spray regularly to prevent, or only when a problem appears? Q3: what to do about black spot, powdery mildew, rust and aphids. Q4: winter clean-up sprays after pruning. Q5: when and how often. Q6: options other than spraying. Q7: safety, including labels, bees and the law.

The types of spray are recorded as the sources name them. Brand names and mixing amounts are not.

## 6. Where the sources differ

Each disagreement is recorded as it stands. The rules decide how Pip presents it.

- **C-1 Spray or not.** RHS: don't use fungicides. Maryland Extension, an American Rose Society article and the Rose Society of NSW: susceptible roses need regular spraying. *Better supported:* that roses can be grown well with little or no spraying, and that sprays are a last resort (both High). The need for regular spraying is Moderate.
- **C-2 Prevent or react.** Some sources spray regularly through the season; others spray only when there is a problem, or "minimally". *Neither is clearly better supported.* Pip gives both.
- **C-3 How long resistance lasts.** RHS: it usually doesn't last. Auckland Botanic Gardens: chosen varieties have stayed healthy without spraying for years. *Better supported:* choosing a tolerant variety (High). That resistance may not last is Moderate, and Pip says it alongside.
- **C-4 Water on the leaves.** Bad for black spot; for powdery mildew, the RHS and UC say it can help. The two diseases differ, so Pip states both.
- **C-5 Winter spray.** Common in New Zealand and Australian sources; the UK and US institutional pages checked don't describe a spray after pruning. Pip shows it to New Zealand and Australian gardeners only.
- **C-6 Order of winter sprays.** June (NZ Rose Society), July (Ross Roses), lime sulphur a few days after pruning, oil two weeks later, then copper at first spring growth (South Pacific Roses). The order is rated Low and kept on record. Pip uses the two precautions on combining them.
- **C-7 How often.** Weekly, 7 to 14 days, about 15 days, or every two weeks; Auckland Botanic Gardens formerly sprayed every two to three weeks. All agree the label decides. Pip gives the range and says the label decides.
- **C-8 Home-made sprays.** Clemson and ABC Gardening Australia suggest a baking soda mix. The RHS says home-made products are not recommended. A New Zealand plant doctor calls them "a safer alternative rather than a panacea". *Neither is clearly better supported.* Pip gives both.
