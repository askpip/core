## 0. Note on This Dossier

**Why this observation was researched on its own, not combined with weak or congested growth.** Damaged growth and weak or congested growth are the two remaining unresearched observations in the Bush Rose V1 Architecture (§5.2). It would be easy to assume they often overlap — a weak cane can also be a damaged one, and vice versa — but this project has already tested exactly that kind of assumption once, for crossing/rubbing stems and inward-growing stems, and found no evidence to support it (see `FRD-BUSHROSE-STEMCROSSING-01` and `FRD-BUSHROSE-INWARDGROWTH-01`, §0 of each). Rather than repeat that pattern, this commission was scoped to damaged growth alone from the outset. Where this research surfaced evidence bearing on weak or congested growth specifically, it is recorded as a lead for that future commission (§5.2 of the RCR), not assessed here.

**A genuinely different decision-logic shape than the prior three dossiers.** `FRD-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01` (approved), `FRD-BUSHROSE-STEMCROSSING-01` and `FRD-BUSHROSE-INWARDGROWTH-01` (submitted) all converge on a simple rule once an observation is confirmed: remove the affected stem entirely, back to its point of origin. Damaged growth does not follow that pattern. The evidence here (AF-12) shows a severity-dependent branch instead: minor damage is shortened only as far as needed to reach healthy tissue — a partial cut, not a full removal — while severe damage, or the absence of any living tissue below it, results in a full cut to the base. Founders should read this dossier with that distinction in mind; it is a genuine finding from the evidence, not an oversight or inconsistency with the project's other dossiers.

**What was commissioned and done.** See the Research Commission Record (§2) for the full commissioning history. In brief: four parallel research passes covered definitions, causes and indicators; photographic limits and lookalikes; pruning choices, severity and deferral; and task-specific safety, across the vetted source pool used successfully elsewhere in this project, supplemented by general arboriculture, plant-pathology and academic literature where rose-specific sources were expected to be thin.

**Outcome, in brief.** The core recognition and pruning-choice questions came back well supported, including two of the strongest, most broadly-corroborated findings in this project to date (AF-4 on canker's visible signs, and AF-11 on the near-universal "cut to healthy pith" method). A small number of genuine, narrow gaps were found (a non-destructive test for a hidden split; splinter/tension-handling safety; a couple of lookalike categories) and each is closed here with a stated ROC-recommended default (§9) rather than left open.

## 6. Managing Conflicting Evidence and Uncertainty

### C-1 — Whether "damaged" is cleanly distinguished from "dead" wood in source language

**What the sources say.** Kansas City Rose Society, RHS and Dunedin Botanic Garden all name damaged growth as its own distinct category alongside (not folded into) dead wood — Dunedin explicitly frames this as "the 3 D's: Dead, Diseased and Damaged." Other sources (Iowa State, and the opening lines of Clemson and the TAMU/El Paso handout) open with a single combined instruction — "remove all dead, damaged or weak stems" — without separately stating how to tell the categories apart in that sentence, relying on a separately-stated pith test to do the actual sorting.

**Reasons given.** Not stated by any source for why some combine the instruction and others don't — likely a difference in how concisely each source is written, not a substantive disagreement about whether the categories are real.

**Treatment.** Preserved as unresolved terminological variation, not a substantive disagreement about whether damaged and dead wood are genuinely different things (no source argues they are the same). A practical default is stated at §9.

### C-2 — What "wait for spring" means for assessing frost/winter damage

**What the sources say.** Two subtly different timing philosophies appear under the same general "wait for spring" advice. UF/IFAS (Okaloosa County) and a non-tier-1 source (Melinda Myers) frame the right moment as after new growth confirms what survived — literally waiting to see live growth emerge. NDSU and N.C. Cooperative Extension instead frame the right moment as the point just before bud break, assessed by cane colour (brown/black versus green) rather than by waiting for new growth itself.

**Reasons given.** Both approaches share the same underlying concern (avoid pruning away tissue that isn't actually dead, and avoid exposing fresh cuts to further cold), but differ on how much certainty to wait for before pruning.

**Treatment.** Preserved as unresolved. A practical default favouring the more conservative, already-approved-adjacent timing is stated at §9.

### C-3 — Real-world inconsistency in whether a photo alone is enough to diagnose canker

**What the sources say.** Two Ask Extension cases, addressing very similar red/purple cane colouring, reached different outcomes: in one, the expert explicitly declined to diagnose from photos ("couldn't diagnose a disease, if any, of your roses from the description or from the pictures"); in another, an expert did identify "stem canker" from photographs with no request for a physical sample.

**Reasons given.** Not stated — this is not a disagreement about principle so much as inconsistent real-world outcomes from case to case, likely reflecting how clear each individual photo happened to be.

**Treatment.** Not treated as evidence that photo-based diagnosis is reliable or unreliable in general; treated instead as direct support for stating uncertainty honestly rather than presenting any single photo-based observation as certain (already a core Architecture principle, §4.5). No default is needed beyond that existing principle.

## 8. Evidence Assessment

### AF-1 — "Damaged" is treated as conceptually distinct from "dead," though some sources blur the two procedurally

**Question:** Q1
**Assessed Finding:** Several sources name damaged growth as its own category, distinct from dead wood, with its own causes and indicators. Other sources combine "dead, damaged or weak" into a single opening removal instruction, relying on a separately-stated pith test to distinguish them in practice.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** Kansas City Rose Society treats dead, damaged and diseased as three separately-worded categories, each with its own removal instruction. Dunedin Botanic Garden: "Remove the 3Ds – Dead, Diseased and Damaged stems." RHS: "Remove all dead and damaged wood as soon as it is seen," naming both but distinguishing "physical damage" from dead tissue elsewhere on the same page. By contrast, Clemson: "Remove all dead, damaged or weak stems leaving only the most vigorous, healthy canes" as a single first step; Iowa State similarly opens with dead/damaged combined.

**Strengths.** Multiple independent institutional sources, across different publisher types, converge on treating damage as a real, distinct category — the disagreement is about how concisely instructions are written, not about substance.

**Limitations.** Several frequently-cited sources do not spell out, in the same sentence, how a reader is meant to tell "damaged" from "dead" — they rely on a separately-given pith test.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether sources that combine the instruction consider the distinction unimportant, or simply assume the reader will apply the pith test that follows, was not tested. See C-1.

### AF-2 — Multiple distinct causes of damage are named across sources

**Question:** Q1
**Assessed Finding:** Sources collectively name several distinct causes of damaged growth: frost/winter injury, mechanical breakage (including wind, rodent activity during winter-protection removal, and crossing/rubbing-cane contact), splitting/cracking, fungal canker, borer or other pest damage, and sunscald.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** Frost/winter injury: RHS ("Browning and dieback of the tips of young shoots in spring (usually the result of frost damage)"), Kansas City Rose Society. Mechanical/wind: University of Missouri IPM ("weak, thin canes... can be damaged by winter winds"), Kansas State MF799 (rodent damage under winter mulch). Crossing/rubbing contact: RHS, Kansas City Rose Society. Canker: RHS, Clemson, University of Maryland, University of Illinois (RPD 626), Kansas State MF799. Borer/pest: Marin Rose Society, PlantTalk Colorado, Oregon State (rose stem girdler). Sunscald: Rose Society of Victoria.

**Strengths.** Each cause is independently named by at least one credible rose-specific source, and several (canker, frost injury) are corroborated by multiple independent sources.

**Limitations.** Not every cause is evidenced equally — sunscald rests on a single source (see AF for lookalikes/U-7); rodent and snow-load damage are each named by only one source and not corroborated elsewhere.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether this list is exhaustive, or whether other causes exist that simply weren't named in the sources searched, was not addressed by any source.

### AF-3 — Weak/thin/unproductive growth is treated as its own category, separate from damage

**Question:** Q1
**Assessed Finding:** Where sources address stem vigour or thickness as a pruning criterion, they treat it as a distinct category from damage — usually a size/vigour threshold (e.g., "thinner than a pencil") rather than a description of physical injury.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** RHS lists "spindly and crossing stems" separately from "dead and damaged wood." Clemson: "Keep any weak, small or short stems pruned away. Generally with hybrid teas, any cane thinner than a pencil should be removed" — presented as its own criterion, addressed separately from the dead/damaged/diseased passage on the same page. Kansas State MF799 similarly addresses "anything smaller than the thickness of a pencil" in its own section, separate from its canker and dead-wood sections.

**Strengths.** Three independent institutional sources apply the same separation, supporting this project's decision to research damaged growth and weak/congested growth as two distinct future observations rather than one.

**Limitations.** No source explicitly discusses whether a stem can be both weak and damaged at once, or how that overlap (if any) should be handled — left for the future weak/congested-growth commission.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None beyond scope; this finding is offered mainly as reassurance that the project's scoping decision (§0) is well supported, not as a novel discovery.

### AF-4 — Canker gives the best-corroborated, most consistent visible-indicator picture of any damage cause

**Question:** Q2
**Assessed Finding:** Multiple independent sources converge on a consistent set of visible canker symptoms: discoloration ranging from tan to dark purplish-brown, a sunken texture, cracked margins where tissue falls away, girdling of the cane leading to dieback above the lesion, visible fungal fruiting bodies or spore masses, and sometimes a target-shaped ring pattern on larger stems.
**Evidence Confidence Level: High**

**Evidence.** Clemson: "Cankers usually appear as dead or discolored areas on rose canes and vary in color from light tan to dark purplish-brown." University of Maryland: "Young cankers are slightly darker in color than adjacent healthy tissue and appear slightly sunken," and "may cause the tissue along the edges to crack and fall away, exposing the dead tissue underneath," with "target-shaped areas on main stems with concentric rings of dead tissue" on larger canes. Kansas State MF799 (independent source) similarly describes cankered tissue becoming "brown, sunken and cracked." University of Illinois (RPD 626): "Small yellow, dark red, or purple spots that gradually enlarge and may girdle or partially girdle the cane... Complete girdling results in dieback... of the parts above the affected area," and "Minute black fruiting bodies of the canker-causing fungus... are visible in the cankered areas." RHS independently corroborates the fruiting-body detail for one canker-causing organism.

**Strengths.** Five independent institutional sources, across UK, US university-extension and specialist-society publisher types, converge on the same symptom picture with no contradiction, including two independently-published sources describing the same sunken-and-cracked pattern.

**Limitations.** No source describes oozing for rose cane canker specifically (oozing was found only in a non-rose bramble source, used for corroboration, not as rose-specific evidence).

**Remaining uncertainty.** None of substance; this is one of the two best-attested findings in this dossier.

### AF-5 — Frost/winter damage shows visually, but definitive confirmation requires a destructive pith test

**Question:** Q2
**Assessed Finding:** Frost/winter-damaged growth is described as showing browning or blackening of shoot tips and general cane discoloration, but the boundary between damaged and dead tissue is confirmed only by cutting into the cane and inspecting the pith colour beneath — not by a non-destructive visual or flex check.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** RHS: "Browning and dieback of the tips of young shoots in spring (usually the result of frost damage)." Kansas City Rose Society: canes "discolored from cold damage" are pruned "back to healthy tissue, where the pith in the center of the cane is pearly white or light tan." Texas A&M/El Paso handout: "Pith (located in the center of the stem) should be creamy white, not brown or gray, on healthy, live wood."

**Strengths.** Multiple independent sources agree that surface discoloration is the visible cue, and separately agree on the pith test as the confirmation method.

**Limitations.** The pith test requires cutting into the cane, which cannot be done from a photograph and is, strictly, a physical rather than purely visual confirmation step.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether surface discoloration alone is ever a reliable enough indicator to skip the pith test was not addressed by any source.

### AF-6 — Mechanical/crossing-contact damage and broken canes are visually identifiable where visible; no non-destructive test exists for a hidden split

**Question:** Q2
**Assessed Finding:** Where a cane shows visible contact-wear from crossing/rubbing, or is visibly broken, sources treat this as sufficient visual confirmation. No source, rose-specific or general, describes a non-destructive test (such as flexing the cane) for detecting a split or crack that is not yet visible from outside.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low**

**Evidence.** RHS: "Physical damage, for example that created when crossing branches rub together." Kansas City Rose Society: "Any canes that have been broken or are discolored from cold damage need to be pruned back to healthy tissue," listing "broken" as its own visible state. A dedicated search for a flex/bend test (rose-specific and general arboriculture) found none.

**Strengths.** Two independent sources confirm visible contact-wear and visible breakage as recognised indicators.

**Limitations.** Only visible damage is covered; a genuinely hidden split has no described detection method short of a destructive pith cut.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether a hidden split is common enough to matter in practice, or a rare edge case, was not addressed by any source. See U-1.

### AF-7 — Borer/pest damage shows as wilting tips, an entry hole and swelling, with a notable caveat

**Question:** Q2
**Assessed Finding:** Where sources describe borer or stem-girdler damage, the indicators given are a suddenly wilting new cane tip, a visible entry hole, and sometimes a slight swelling near the affected area. One commonly-cited "cane borer" source, however, actually describes a beneficial hunting wasp that nests inside an already-existing pruning cut rather than a pest causing damage to live tissue — a caveat worth keeping in mind before treating every such sign as harmful.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low**

**Evidence.** Marin Rose Society: "that succulent new cane tip that suddenly wilts and dies," and "The telltale sign of a borer is an entry hole usually found along the dead cane." PlantTalk Colorado (#1463): describes "a slight swelling" near a wasp-nest area, but clarifies: "most rose cane damage comes from hunting wasps... rather than harmful borers," which "actually provide pest control by feeding on aphids." Oregon State (rose stem girdler): a true girdling pest produces "a swollen node where the spiral tunnel is located," bark that may be "discolored, more woody, or cracking," and stems that "tend to break easily."

**Strengths.** Two to three independent sources on the indicators themselves; the wasp-versus-borer distinction is a genuinely useful, source-documented nuance that prevents over-flagging a harmless sign as damage.

**Limitations.** Sawdust/frass, often associated with borer damage in other plants, is not confirmed by any rose-specific source searched (U-2). Evidence is thinner than for canker or frost damage.

**Remaining uncertainty.** How commonly a gardener would actually encounter the harmful girdler versus the harmless wasp was not addressed by any source.

### AF-8 — A photograph can plausibly capture external signs but not internal tissue condition; physical examination is the accepted fallback

**Question:** Q3
**Assessed Finding:** No rose-specific source directly addresses photo-versus-physical diagnosis of damaged growth. Drawing on general/academic literature and extension diagnostic-clinic guidance, the ROC's own synthesis is that a photograph can plausibly capture external, gross-morphological signs (colour, shape, obvious splits, visible fruiting bodies, swelling location) but cannot establish internal tissue condition (pith colour, the true extent of a hidden split or tunnel). Where a photo is ambiguous, the accepted professional fallback is a physical sample or an in-person visit, not a better photograph. Canker's classic external signs are, in principle, more photo-assessable than internal-only conditions — but real diagnostic outcomes are inconsistent even among trained experts (C-3), so confidence should always be stated rather than presented as certain.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low** (a ROC synthesis of general, non-rose-specific evidence, extrapolated per EAS §2.10)

**Evidence.** Berland et al. (2019), *Forests* 10(4):349: "Virtual surveys are not appropriate for detailed assessments of tree risk because the analyst cannot examine the tree up close or from all angles," and "should not replace field inspections by qualified professionals for the purposes of identifying pest/pathogen problems." Kennard et al. (1996): "External signs of decay may be misleading, or trees may show no signs of internal defects" — even trained in-person assessment has real limits. Mississippi State Extension: "photos do not provide the information necessary to make a diagnosis... diagnosis depends upon laboratory examination and/or testing of a physical sample." Colorado State Extension (Douglas County): when a photo doesn't resolve a diagnosis, "a site visit may be needed." Alabama Extension recommends multiple photo angles as a mitigation, without claiming it fully closes the gap. Rose-specific: Marin Rose Society describes cutting into the cane to check pith as the way to find the true extent of borer damage — explicitly not visible from outside.

**Strengths.** Convergent conclusion across peer-reviewed academic literature, multiple independent extension diagnostic-clinic sources, and rose-specific practice, even though no single source addresses the question in full.

**Limitations.** No source frames this exactly as a "photo-based cane-damage diagnosis" question; all evidence is assembled from adjacent contexts (tree risk assessment, general plant-disease photo submission, and rose sources that only implicitly show the internal/external distinction).

**Remaining uncertainty.** How much of this general limitation matters for a garden rose specifically (much smaller and more easily viewed from multiple angles than an urban tree) was not addressed by any source.

### AF-9 — Harmless insect galls are a well-documented lookalike for damage

**Question:** Q4
**Assessed Finding:** Certain insect galls on rose canes (for example, the rose bedeguar gall/"Robin's pin-cushion," and mossy rose gall) look alarming but are generally harmless to the plant, and could plausibly be mistaken for damage by a beginner. Heavy or prolonged gall infestation can, however, occasionally weaken or kill tissue above the gall, so this is not an unconditional reassurance.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** RHS: "The galls are not harmful to the rose... no action should be taken," and "the feeding activities of the gall wasp grubs do not have any real adverse effect on the rose." Washington State University (Hortsense): spiny rose gall "do[es] not appear to harm the plant"; mossy rose gall "appears harmless to the plant." Iowa State University Extension: "Most insect galls do not seriously affect the vigor of healthy plants... Mossy rose galls formed on the leaves of the rose are harmless," but adds: "a heavy or prolonged gall infestation on the rose stems may weaken or kill portions of a plant above the gall."

**Strengths.** Three independent institutional sources agree on the general harmlessness of galls, which is valuable as a lookalike category to prevent unnecessary cutting.

**Limitations.** The caveat about heavy infestation comes from only one of the three sources and is not itself given a clear severity threshold.

**Remaining uncertainty.** How a gardener would tell an ordinary gall from a "heavy or prolonged" infestation worth acting on was not addressed by any source.

### AF-10 — Canker's early colouring genuinely overlaps with normal new-cane pigmentation

**Question:** Q4
**Assessed Finding:** Early canker symptoms are described as small yellow, dark red or purple spots — colours that also occur naturally in new rose-cane growth in many cultivars. This is a real, source-documented confounder, serious enough that a trained Extension diagnostician declined to resolve one real case from photographs alone.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low** (a single documented case plus one descriptive source; genuine but thin)

**Evidence.** University of Illinois (RPD 626): "The first symptoms on the canes are small yellow, dark red, or purple spots that gradually enlarge." A gardener's own Ask Extension question captures the confusion directly: "the more generalized spread of the red/purple/black color on the canes seems a bit different from examples I see online of rose canker" — to which the responding expert declined to diagnose from the photos provided and suggested that if the plant "continue[s] growing and blooming," ordinary care may be all that's needed.

**Strengths.** A concrete, real-world documented instance of exactly the confusion this question asks about, from a credible extension source.

**Limitations.** Rests on one descriptive source and one case, not a systematic study of how often this confusion occurs.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether there is any reliable visual cue (beyond the "sunken texture" noted in AF-4) to distinguish canker-red from ordinary new-growth-red was not fully resolved by any source.

### AF-11 — Once damage is confirmed, sources near-unanimously instruct cutting to healthy tissue, verified by pith colour

**Question:** Q5
**Assessed Finding:** With near-unanimous, broad support across the source base, once damaged growth is confirmed, it is cut back until healthy tissue is reached — verified by cutting into the cane and inspecting the exposed pith colour (white or pale green indicates living tissue; brown, black or grey indicates dead tissue).
**Evidence Confidence Level: High**

**Evidence.** American Rose Society: "Always prune cut to good healthy tissue easily recognized by the green bark on the outside of the cane and white pith core revealed after the cut is made." RHS: "Prune dieback to healthy white pith." Kansas City Rose Society, Clemson, Oregon State, Iowa State, Texas A&M/El Paso, PlantTalk Colorado and University of Arkansas all independently give essentially the same instruction and confirmation method.

**Strengths.** Broad, independent corroboration across nine or more institutional sources of every publisher type used in this project, unanimous in direction and method.

**Limitations.** None of substance; a small number of sources are less procedural (stating only "remove damaged wood" without the pith detail).

**Remaining uncertainty.** None; alongside AF-4, this is the best-attested finding in this dossier, and is consistent with, and builds directly on, the live/dead pith test already approved in `ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01`.

### AF-12 — How far back to cut depends mainly on severity, not cause — a materially different rule from prior dossiers

**Question:** Q5
**Assessed Finding:** Sources give an explicit severity-based branch: minor or slight damage is shortened only as far as needed to reach healthy tissue (a partial cut); severe damage, or the absence of any living pith/buds below the damaged area, results in full removal of the cane to its base. This differs materially from the "always remove fully to point of origin" pattern already recorded for crossing/rubbing and inward-growing stems, where the removal decision does not depend on degree.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** Purdue Extension gives the clearest statement: "Cut back badly damaged or dead canes down to the base of the plant... Slightly damaged canes only need to be cut back to healthy tissue." Corroborated independently by Clemson ("If no live buds remain, remove the entire cane"), Texas A&M/El Paso ("If there are no live buds, remove the entire branch or cane to the base of the plant"), Iowa State ("Remove the entire cane if there is no sign of life"), University of Georgia ("Occasionally, the pith will become darker with each successive cut, and the cane will have to be removed at the bud union"), and an Ask Extension case on Knock Out roses ("stems that appear to be dead with no new growth should be cut to the ground").

**Strengths.** Six independent sources agree on the same severity-dependent branching logic, with Purdue stating it most explicitly.

**Limitations.** "Slight" versus "severe" is not given a precise, universal threshold by any source; the boundary is described qualitatively.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Exactly how a gardener judges "slight" versus "severe" damage in an ambiguous middle case was not addressed by any source; the practical mechanism given throughout is simply to keep cutting progressively further down the cane until healthy pith is found (AF-11), which functions as the operational answer regardless of how the damage is initially categorised.

### AF-13 — Cause adds specific extra steps beyond the general healthy-tissue rule

**Question:** Q5
**Assessed Finding:** Beyond the general "cut to healthy tissue" rule (AF-11, AF-12), canker/disease-suspect damage gets two extra steps not given for plain mechanical or frost damage: a larger safety margin (commonly 2-3 inches below the visibly infected area, or "well below" it) and tool disinfection between cuts. Borer/pest damage gets a different extra step: sealing the fresh cut to deter re-infestation.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** University of Illinois (RPD 626): "cut back to a strongly growing shoot or branch at least 2 to 3 inches below the infected portion," plus "dip the shears in a disinfectant solution... before each pruning cut." An independent Ask Extension response (UF/IFAS Orange County): "Make all pruning cuts well below the diseased areas," plus tool disinfection with a 1:9 bleach solution. Marin Rose Society (borer): "Cut each cane below the wilted portion and examine the pith – continue to make short cuts until the pith is white," described as "destroy" rather than salvage. PlantTalk Colorado (borer): "Seal this cut... with water-insoluble glue or nail polish to prevent more nests of eggs." By contrast, Kansas City Rose Society's own treatment of plain "broken or discolored from cold damage" canes states only the healthy-tissue rule, with no disinfection or sealing step.

**Strengths.** Two independent sources for the canker margin/disinfection step; two independent sources for the borer/sealing step; a clear contrast within a single source (Kansas City Rose Society) between how it treats plain damage versus disease.

**Limitations.** The exact margin for canker (2-3 inches, or "well below") is not perfectly consistent in wording between the two sources, though the direction (larger margin than plain damage) is.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None beyond what is stated; this finding is closely related to, but distinct from, the safety findings at AF-15.

### AF-14 — Frost/winter damage's full extent is often not visible immediately; a second pruning pass may be needed

**Question:** Q6
**Assessed Finding:** Several sources advise waiting until at or near spring bud break to assess and prune frost/winter-damaged canes, since the full extent of damage may not be apparent right away, and note that a second, later pruning pass can be needed as further dieback becomes visible.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** Portland Rose Society: "It takes time for freeze damage to develop so it is possible that some undamaged looking canes will need to be pruned again later in the spring." UF/IFAS (Okaloosa): "Pruning immediately after a freeze will cut away live wood" unnecessarily, and dieback "will show up in spring and early summer." NDSU: "Rose pruning is best accomplished in spring, after the worst of winter's cold is likely past, but before new growth begins." N.C. Cooperative Extension: "Limb and twig die-back can be removed with proper pruning before bud break in the spring," and "Pruning early in the winter removes energy reserves the plant needs to survive the dormant period."

**Strengths.** Four independent sources agree on the general "wait, don't prune immediately after cold damage" principle.

**Limitations.** Sources differ subtly on exactly what "waiting" means — see C-2.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether a specific still-attached, partially damaged cane might recover if left unpruned (as distinct from waiting to assess the whole bush) was not addressed by any tier-1 source. See U-3.

### AF-15 — For canker or disease-suspect damage, sources recommend disinfecting pruning tools between cuts

**Question:** Q7
**Assessed Finding:** Beyond generic "keep tools clean and sharp" advice already approved for general pruning, multiple independent sources recommend disinfecting the cutting blade specifically when working on canker- or disease-suspect wood, using 70% rubbing alcohol or a diluted bleach solution (commonly around 1:9 to 1:10), at a frequency ranging from "before each cut" to "at minimum between plants."
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** University of Illinois (RPD 626): "Before each pruning cut, dip the shears in a disinfectant solution of 70 percent rubbing alcohol, or liquid household bleach (1 part of bleach to 9 parts of water)." Oklahoma State: "Disinfect pruning tools after use on a diseased plant," with a "10 percent dilution" for crown gall. Clemson: "Disinfect cutting tools after use on a diseased plant in a solution of 1 part household bleach to nine parts water," and, for rose rosette virus specifically, notes sap on pruners is contaminated and tools "must be disinfected... before being used on uninfected plants." Iowa State: "At minimum when removing disease-infected plant material, pruners should be disinfected between plants. Ideally, they would be disinfected between each cut," giving both an alcohol-wipe method and a 10-minute bleach-soak method. RHS: recommends a commercial disinfectant "left for 15-20 minutes before wiping off," naming bacterial canker explicitly as a reason for tool hygiene.

**Strengths.** Five independent institutional sources, across plant-pathology extension, general extension and RHS, converge on the same additional step.

**Limitations.** Exact frequency and dilution vary somewhat between sources (a range, not a single universal number).

**Remaining uncertainty.** None of substance; this is a well-evidenced addition to the project's existing generic tool-cleanliness guidance.

---

### AF-16 — Disease- or canker-affected prunings should be burned or trashed, not composted

**Question:** Q7
**Assessed Finding:** Sources consistently advise disposing of canker- or disease-affected prunings by burning them or discarding them with household trash, rather than adding them to an ordinary compost pile, because home composting rarely reaches temperatures sufficient to kill plant pathogens in slow-to-break-down woody material.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate**

**Evidence.** RHS: "Although compost bins rarely reach temperatures sufficient for killing pathogens, they are suitable for disposing of material infected by some less-persistent diseases," but explicitly excludes "any woody material that will be slow to break down," and separately states bonfire disposal "is ideal for branches affected by canker." University of Illinois (RPD 626): "Promptly prune out, remove, and burn or haul away with the trash all infected, dead, and weak parts of canes." Oklahoma State: "Prune out and burn all infected portions of canes." Clemson, on rose rosette virus specifically: infected material should be "immediately removed, then burned or bagged for trash pickup."

**Strengths.** Four independent institutional sources agree on the same disposal guidance, with RHS explicitly explaining the underlying reason (composting temperatures).

**Limitations.** Guidance is specific to disease/canker-affected material; sources do not address disposal of plain mechanically-damaged prunings, which presumably need no special disposal treatment.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None of substance for disease-affected material; disposal of non-disease damage was not addressed by any source, consistent with it not being a special case.

## 9. Recommendations for Further Research, and Practical Defaults

Each unresolved question from §7.2 is given a stated, ROC-recommended practical default rather than left open. Each is clearly the ROC's own recommendation under EAS §2.10, not a sourced finding; any Founder may override any of them at any time; none blocks approval.

**U-1 — No non-destructive test for a hidden split.** No source describes one. **Default:** treat any visible sign (a bulge, a visible crack line, discoloration, or wilting beyond a point on the cane) as sufficient trigger for closer physical examination of that spot; absent any visible sign, rely on the existing requirement to physically examine the rose rather than adding a special test.

**U-2 — Sawdust/frass as a borer indicator.** Not confirmed for roses. **Default:** do not include this as a stated indicator until sourced; rely on the confirmed indicators (wilting tip, entry hole, swelling near the site).

**U-3 — Whether a compromised but still-attached cane might recover unpruned.** No tier-1 source supports this for an individual cane. **Default:** once damage is confirmed on a specific cane, guide toward the severity-based cutting rule (AF-12) rather than an indefinite "leave it and see" for that cane; "Decide later" remains available at the whole-plant/whole-decision level, consistent with the deferral guidance at AF-14 and C-2, not as a claim that a specific damaged cane will heal itself.

**U-4 — Whether professional help is ever specifically warranted because of damage severity.** No source ties escalation to this. **Default:** governed by the app's general uncertainty/confidence gates, the same convention adopted for the other pruning-choice dossiers in this project; the evidence if anything argues against a "give up" outcome, since sources frame roses as resilient even after severe cutback.

**U-5 — Safety for handling broken/splintered canes or canes under tension.** No rose-specific source. **Default:** cross-refer to `ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01`'s approved generic safety content as primary guidance; additionally, by analogy from general arboriculture sources (explicitly extrapolated from storm-timber/chainsaw contexts, not rose-specific evidence): handle a broken or hanging cane carefully, and anticipate that a bent or trapped cane may move unexpectedly when cut, cutting a little at a time rather than all at once where a cane appears to be under tension.

**U-6 — Further beginner lookalikes (thorns, leaf/bud scars, graft-union swelling).** No source names any of these as damage lookalikes. **Default:** no specific confounder list is offered for these; the existing requirement to confirm any observation on the physical rose before acting remains the primary safeguard.

**U-7 — Recognising sunscald with enough detail to distinguish it from disease.** Thin, single-source evidence with no visual descriptor. **Default:** treat sunscald as a low-confidence possibility only, not a confidently recognisable category from a photograph; rely on physical confirmation rather than photo-based sunscald diagnosis.
