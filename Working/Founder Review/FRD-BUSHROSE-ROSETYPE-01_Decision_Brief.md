# Decision Brief — Recognising a Supported Rose Type (Bush Rose Scope Qualification)

This is the plain-language companion to `FRD-BUSHROSE-ROSETYPE-01`. It adds no evidence, changes no finding and changes no confidence level. If the Brief and the FRD differ, the FRD governs.

## 1. What you are being asked to do

Read this Brief, then the Review Form asks for your decision on each of the 24 findings, on four further-research items, and on two scope questions, and finishes with one overall decision: **approve as `ARC-BUSHROSE-ROSETYPE-01`, approve with amendments, request further work, or decline.**

## 2. The short version

Ask Pip needs to know whether a gardener's rose is a supported bush rose. The research found that class descriptions are largely consistent for the main cases (Hybrid Tea, Floribunda, Grandiflora, shrub, miniature, standard, patio), with seven findings at High confidence and thirteen at Moderate. It also found that the word **"bush"** on a New Zealand label does not reliably mean an included type, that the same cultivar can be classed differently by different authorities, and that **no source tells a beginner how to place a rose from a dormant plant alone**. Four findings are at Low confidence, and the further-research items R-1 and R-2 rest on that gap.

Grandiflora is rarely named in the New Zealand sources checked. The New Zealand Rose Society directory has no Grandiflora type, and two New Zealand nurseries have no Grandiflora category.

## 3. What was researched

Q1: how sources describe Hybrid Tea, Floribunda and Grandiflora. Q2: how they describe the excluded types. Q3: labels, names and marketing terms used in New Zealand and Australia, and where a class can be checked. Q4: boundary cases (patio, Grandiflora against tall Hybrid Tea, shrub roses sold as bush roses, climbing sports). Q5: features on a dormant plant and what photographs can show. Q6: what to do when the type is unknown. Q7: what comparison images would need to show.

Not covered, by design: cultivar identification from appearance, pruning technique, disease, sourcing images, questionnaire wording and application changes.

## 4. How to read the strength ratings

| Level | Meaning in this dossier |
|---|---|
| High | Reliable and generally consistent across independent sources |
| Moderate | Reasonably supported, with notable limitations or some inconsistency |
| Low | Limited evidence or significant limitations |

Each finding has its own rating. None is averaged into an overall figure.

## 5. What the research found

### Included classes (Brief section 5, group 1)

| ID | Finding | Confidence | Worth knowing |
|---|---|---|---|
| AF-1 | Hybrid Tea roses bear single or few blooms per stem, Floribundas bear clusters, and Grandifloras bear Hybrid Tea-form blooms in clusters. Every class description consulted uses this feature first. | High | Nine sources across the UK, US, Australia and NZ agree (RHS, ARS, Rose Society of NSW, NZ Rose Society, Yates, Illinois Extension, Laidback Gardener, Heirloom, Clemson). Blooms are not visible on a dormant plant. |
| AF-2 | Sources that give heights describe Grandiflora as the tallest of the three included classes (Yates up to 1.8 m; ARS 6 to 8 feet or more; Heirloom 5 to 7 feet). | High | Five sources of three kinds. Heights vary with climate; actual New Zealand heights are unknown. |
| AF-3 | Height ranges for Hybrid Tea and Floribunda overlap across sources, no source gives a separating height, and one source (Oregon State) describes Floribunda as the larger plant. | Moderate | Six sources. The Oregon State statement sits in a pruning guide. Height is therefore not a dependable way to separate the two. |
| AF-4 | Grandiflora is described by ARS, Illinois, Yates, Clemson and Heirloom. It is not in the NZ Rose Society type list, the Rose Society of NSW list, or the category lists of two NZ nurseries. | Moderate | Absence from a menu is not absence from gardens, and menus were read through an automated extraction. Real frequency in NZ is unknown. |
| AF-5 | Some individual cultivars are placed in different classes by different authorities. 'Queen Elizabeth' is listed as both Hybrid Tea and cluster-flowered (Floribunda and Grandiflora). | High | Illinois Extension, the NZ Rose Society directory and a plant database each show or state it. How often it happens was not measured. |
| AF-6 | Oregon State groups 'Hybrid Tea-Grandiflora' for pruning, a nursery sheet gives one cut height for all three classes, and Heirloom advises lighter cutting for Grandiflora. RHS has no Grandiflora group. | Low | Two commercial or nursery sources and one extension source, none with evidence beyond practice. This is the question FRD-BUSHROSE-SCOPE-01 reserved for later. |

### Excluded types (Brief section 5, group 2)

| ID | Finding | Confidence | Worth knowing |
|---|---|---|---|
| AF-7 | Shrub roses are described as larger, open, spreading or arching bushes (RHS 'open and spreading', ARS 'sprawling', Rose Society of NSW 'lax arching habit'). | High | Typical, not universal descriptions. |
| AF-8 | The shrub class is a mixed one: Illinois calls it a 'catch all' class, Yates gives 0.5 to 6 m, David Austin says 'quite short to tall', and one NZ nursery lists a shrub-category rose with habit 'Rose Bush'. | Moderate | Size and habit alone do not reliably separate shrub roses from bush roses. Four sources of differing kinds. |
| AF-9 | Patio roses are a separate category from Hybrid Tea and Floribunda in RHS (small bush roses, 30 to 75 cm, Pruning Group 22), the NZ Rose Society type list, and NZ nurseries. | High | Rose Society of NSW lists no patio category. Category lists were read through an extraction. |
| AF-10 | Sources differ: with miniatures and polyanthas (RHS), a down-sized floribunda (Wairere), closer to dwarf floribundas and not miniatures (Peter Beales), or a general term for any container rose (Jackson & Perkins). | Moderate | Four sources, four positions. No formal NZ authority was found. |
| AF-11 | Miniature roses are described as small, with stated upper heights of about 50 to 75 cm. | High | Six sources agree in substance; several combine miniatures with minifloras. |
| AF-12 | Miniflora roses are distinguished from miniatures and floribundas by bloom and foliage size, which cannot be seen on a dormant plant. | Moderate | Two society sources, both short statements. |
| AF-13 | Groundcover roses are a separate category, yet the Flower Carpet marketer calls its rose 'a low, dense and compact bush' that becomes 'a slightly larger ... shrub' when left unpruned. | Moderate | The category is well supported; the appearance evidence rests on one marketer. |
| AF-14 | Climbing sports of bush-form Hybrid Teas exist (long canes of 6 to 10 feet, flowers on shorter laterals) and are catalogued as climbing types, including in the NZ Rose Society type list. | Moderate | Naming convention inferred from examples. No source says whether a climbing sport reverts to bush form. |
| AF-15 | A standard rose has a single tall bare stem carrying a grafted crown. Weeping standards use 1.5 to 1.8 m stems. | High | RHS, Rose Society of NSW and NZ nurseries agree. |
| AF-16 | Old Garden Roses and species roses are defined by date, lineage and flowering habit (for example once-flowering), not by plant form. | Moderate | ARS, Rose Society of NSW and the NZ Rose Society type list agree. Old Garden Roses are diverse. |

### Labels, names and marketing terms (Brief section 5, group 3)

| ID | Finding | Confidence | Worth knowing |
|---|---|---|---|
| AF-17 | NZ retailers use 'bush' in more than one sense, including for patio, shrub-category and groundcover roses. The NZ Rose Society type list has no 'Bush' value. | Moderate | Six sources; a commercial sample, not systematic. |
| AF-18 | NZ online nursery listings organise roses by type (Hybrid Tea, Floribunda, Patio, Shrub, Climbing, Standard and others). | Moderate | Online listings only. What physical plant tags print was not found. |
| AF-19 | The NZ Rose Society Rose Finder is a searchable directory that gives a type for each entry and notes that some roses fit more than one category. | Moderate | One directory. How many commonly sold NZ cultivars it holds is unknown. |

### Identification and uncertainty (Brief section 5, group 4)

| ID | Finding | Confidence | Worth knowing |
|---|---|---|---|
| AF-20 | Sources describe identifying an unlabelled rose as difficult and dependent on many plant parts (foliage, prickles, habit, flowers), and often needing an experienced rosarian. | Moderate | Three sources of different kinds. One concerns older roses and one is a single sentence. |
| AF-21 | One Australian nursery states that, with years in the business, it can identify well over 90% of the varieties it grows from bare canes. | Low | Single commercial source, about experienced staff and the nursery's own varieties, with no method given. |
| AF-22 | The only located evidence on photograph-app accuracy concerns 55 tree species: genus level 71.8% to 97.3%, species level 40.9% to 83.9%, and bark pictures much less accurate than leaves. | Low | Not roses, not cultivars, not leafless plants. |
| AF-23 | The RHS advises that if you inherit an overgrown rose and do not know its type (and do not want to wait for flowering), it is best to prune as for a Hybrid Tea. | Low | One source, one country, and it concerns pruning, not scope qualification. |
| AF-24 | Sources point an unsure gardener to a local rose society, a rosarian or a second opinion. | Moderate | Three sources of different kinds; two do not address classification directly. |

**Q7 (comparison images).** No source says what an image for rose class must show. The features sources use to tell types apart are the starting content for a later sourcing brief; that brief is production work drawn from approved findings.

**Photographs.** No new finding. The earlier pruning-framework dossier (FRD-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-04) already records that physical examination is favoured over photographs alone (AF-34, Low). AF-20 and AF-22 add that identifying an unlabelled rose uses plant parts and seasons one dormant photo does not show.

## 6. Where the sources differ

**C-1 — Height of Hybrid Tea against Floribunda (AF-3).** Yates, Laidback Gardener, Illinois and Heirloom put Hybrid Tea taller. Oregon State describes Floribunda as the larger plant. Unresolved.

**C-2 — Nearest class for a patio rose (AF-10).** RHS groups patio with miniatures and polyanthas. Wairere calls it a down-sized floribunda. Peter Beales says closer to dwarf floribundas and not miniatures. Jackson & Perkins call it a general term for any container rose. Unresolved.

**C-3 — Whether Grandiflora belongs in the New Zealand picture (AF-4).** Five sources describe it; the New Zealand Rose Society type list, the Rose Society of NSW list and two New Zealand nurseries' category lists do not include it. Unresolved.

## 8. Where the type comes from: what this means for the app

This section describes what the findings could support later. It is not part of the research and decides nothing.

- A **stated type** can come from a nursery listing or the New Zealand Rose Society Rose Finder (AF-18, AF-19). The word "bush" alone does not state one (AF-17).
- **Structural exclusions** with the clearest descriptions are standard roses (AF-15), miniatures (AF-11) and climbing types (AF-14).
- **Uncertain or overlapping cases** (patio, shrub, groundcover, Grandiflora, disputed cultivars) are where the findings support an "unknown" outcome (AF-5, AF-8, AF-10, AF-13).
- **Questionnaire wording, the qualifying gate and the comparison-image list** follow from an approved ARC and are separate work.
