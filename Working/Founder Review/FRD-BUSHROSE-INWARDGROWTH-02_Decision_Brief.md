# Decision Brief — Inward-Growing Stems on an Established, Dormant Bush Rose

This is the plain-language companion to `FRD-BUSHROSE-INWARDGROWTH-02`. It is one of two dossiers arising from one research commission, alongside `ARC-BUSHROSE-STEMCROSSING-01`.

## 1. What you are being asked to do

Read this Brief, then the Review Form asks for one thing: **approve the six findings below as `ARC-BUSHROSE-INWARDGROWTH-01`, approve with amendments, or decline.** Everything the research could close, it closed; everything it couldn't (a real, permanent gap rather than a research shortfall) has a stated ROC-recommended default instead of being left for you to rule on.

## 2. The short version

This is the thinner of the two dossiers from the same research effort. Inward-growing stems get noticeably less dedicated coverage in the literature than crossing or rubbing stems do — most sources address inward growth only as something to prevent going forward (cut to an outward-facing bud) rather than as an existing defect to diagnose and remove. Only three sources name it as its own recognisable thing. That thinness is itself a finding, not a research shortfall — the same broad source pool was searched for both dossiers. It doesn't mean the guidance is contested; no source disagrees with removing a confirmed inward-growing stem, there's just less written about it specifically. No source states whether the two conditions commonly occur together on the same bush — only direct observation over time could settle that, not published guidance.

## 3. What was researched

Q1: how sources define inward growth, and its relationship to crossing/rubbing. Q2: visible indicators. Q3: what a photograph can and can't establish. Q4: what a beginner might confuse it with. Q5: what to do once confirmed. Q6: how a stem with more than one condition is handled, and when to wait. Q7: safety specific to this task.

## 4. How to read the strength ratings

Same three levels used throughout this project: **High** (many independent sources, no serious contradiction), **Moderate** (fewer sources, or some contradiction), **Low** (a handful of sources, thin evidence, or extrapolated from outside rose-specific literature).

## 5. What the research found

**Definitions (Q1).** Most sources address inward growth prospectively — cut to an outward bud so future growth points away from the centre — rather than treating an existing inward-growing stem as its own named defect the way crossing and rubbing are treated (AF-1, Moderate). Only three sources name it as its own recognisable category.

**Visible indicators and photographic limits (Q2, Q3).** Where an indicator is stated, it's simply growth direction toward the centre — a soft, subjective standard, not a hard rule (AF-2, Low, only three sources). No rose source addresses photographic limits; the ROC's own synthesis, drawing on general pruning practice and one academic study, is that a single photo has real limits for judging growth direction (AF-3, Low, clearly labelled as extrapolated — this same finding also appears in the companion dossier, since it applies equally to both observations).

**Pruning choices (Q5).** Where sources do name it, they're unanimous: remove it, on the same footing as crossing or rubbing stems (AF-4, Low — fewer sources than the crossing/rubbing equivalent, but no source disagrees). Removal is a full cut to the point of origin, not a shortening (AF-5, Moderate).

**Multiple conditions and deferral (Q6).** No source says which condition wins when a stem is both inward and crossing — not a live conflict in practice, since both point to the same action. Deferral guidance that exists is about general timing, not this observation specifically (AF-6, Moderate).

**Lookalikes (Q4) and task-specific safety (Q7).** Both close to complete gaps. One plausible but unsourced caution is noted (a stem curving toward light could be mistaken for genuine inward growth). Given a plain default each rather than left open.

## 6. Where the sources differ

**C-1 — Should the centre always be opened up?** Almost everyone says yes, for air and light. One source (Rose Society of South Australia, in one of its own two published guides) argues the opposite. This is the same disagreement recorded in the companion dossier, since it bears equally on both observations. **Default:** follow the majority open-centre guidance.

## 7. What's genuinely left open, and what's been given a default instead

The six questions the research couldn't answer (lookalikes; priority between conditions on one stem; whether professional help is ever specifically warranted; task-specific safety; whether a second photo would help; whether the two observations co-occur) each get a plain, clearly-labelled ROC recommendation in FRD §9.
