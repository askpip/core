## 3. What was researched

The original commission asked nine questions: why roses are pruned (Q1); what growth is removed (Q2); the order of work (Q3); how removal decisions interact when a stem fits more than one category (Q4); the shape and framework aimed for (Q5); how much is taken off, including renewal of an overgrown bush (Q6); staged renovation (Q7); what can be judged from a photograph (Q8); and cut mechanics — distance, angle, tools, sealing (Q9). All nine were addressed to some degree in the original round. The later rounds went back to widen the source base, close remaining gaps under each question, and — where a gap turned out to be permanent rather than closable — say so plainly instead of leaving it open by omission.

## 4. How to read the strength ratings

Unchanged from the original Brief. Three levels are used:

- **High** — many independent sources agree, across different types of publisher (charity, society, university extension, and so on), with no serious contradiction.
- **Moderate** — several sources agree, but fewer of them, or fewer independent publisher types, or some contradiction exists.
- **Low** — only a handful of sources, or the evidence is thin, contested, or extrapolated from outside rose-specific literature.

A rating describes how well-attested a finding is — not how important it is to correct rose pruning. One entry, AF-38, documents both a corroborating source and a newly found disagreement (C11) in the same entry; because a documented disagreement between sources isn't itself evidence for either side, it carries no strength rating of its own.

## 5. What the research found

### 5.1 Why we prune

**AF-1 (High).** Sources agree pruning a dormant bush rose removes unhealthy wood, opens the bush to light and air, and encourages strong flowering growth, while keeping size and shape controlled. No source ranks these purposes against each other.

### 5.2 What comes out

Damaged or diseased wood (**AF-2**, High), crossing or rubbing stems (**AF-3**, High), inward-growing or centre-crowding stems (**AF-4**, Moderate), and weak, thin or spindly growth (**AF-5**, High) are the four best-attested removal categories. A pencil-thickness rule of thumb separates weak from sound canes (**AF-6**, Moderate, American sources only). Old or unproductive canes are removed (**AF-7**, Moderate) — **AF-36** (Moderate, new in Revision 3) now adds how old wood is actually recognised: grey, scaly, streaked or crusty bark, sometimes with deteriorating prickles, rather than a number of years. Rootstock suckers on grafted roses are removed (**AF-8**, Moderate). The canes kept are the strong, vigorous, generally younger ones (**AF-9**, Moderate); new basal shoots are a sign of health and a way of renewing the framework (**AF-10**, Low). Two reasons are given for removing crossing stems — wound-and-disease risk, and blocked air circulation (**AF-11**, Low).

### 5.3 The order of work

Dead, damaged and diseased wood is dealt with first (**AF-12**, High); all unwanted growth is removed before the remaining canes are shortened (**AF-13**, High). **AF-31** (Moderate, new in Revision 3) confirms, from a peer-reviewed general-arboriculture source with independent corroboration, that a removal cut (taking a stem off at its origin) and a shortening cut (back to a bud) are genuinely different techniques, not interchangeable — closing a question the original round left thin.

### 5.4 The shape to aim for, and what stays

The target is an open-centred bush, canes spread evenly round the base (**AF-14**, High). A small number of strong canes is kept, counts ranging three to eight across sources (**AF-15**, Moderate — see C7). Several sources frame the task as choosing which canes to keep rather than removing categories of unwanted growth (**AF-16**, Moderate) — Revision 4 read the existing evidence again and found these are two *stages* of one process, not competing methods: remove the unwanted categories first (AF-12/13), then choose the framework from what's left. That's recorded as the ROC's own reading of already-approved evidence, not a new finding, but it closes the practical question. **AF-32** (Low, new in Revision 3) adds that on a congested or overgrown bush, keeper canes are identified in a set sequence rather than all at once. **AF-33** (Low, new in Revision 3) gives an explicit tie-break rule, from one source, for which of two crossing canes to remove.

### 5.5 How much to take off

The kept canes are shortened by about a third to a half of their length (**AF-17**, Moderate — final heights themselves vary widely and are not reconciled, C1). Hybrid teas are pruned lower than floribundas (**AF-18**, Moderate); **AF-37** (Moderate, new in Revision 3) adds that grandifloras are generally pruned like hybrid teas, adjusted for height. Once-flowering roses (old garden, rambling, rugosa) are pruned differently and are outside this dossier's scope (**AF-19**, High — already excluded by `FRD-BUSHROSE-SCOPE-01`). Multi-year renewal removes about a third of the old canes each year (**AF-20**, Low) — **AF-40** (Low, new in Revision 4) backs this with a well-attested general-arboriculture rule: remove no more than about a third of any woody plant's growth in one session, for the same reason (stress, depleted reserves, uncontrolled regrowth), giving a genuine working answer to how hard a single session can go and how staged renovation should be paced. **AF-39** (Low, new in Revision 3) adds that a badly overgrown, weak or sickly bush is sometimes better replaced than renovated — no source gives a threshold for that call.

### 5.6 How the cut is made

The cut is made above an outward-facing bud (**AF-21**, High), about a quarter of an inch above it (**AF-22**, Moderate — Texas A&M's own page explains the tradeoff behind the quarter-inch/half-inch split, added in Revision 4: too close and the bud dies, too far and the wood above it dies back), slanted at roughly 45 degrees (**AF-23**, Moderate), sloping — most sources say — away from the bud (**AF-24**, Low; wording is genuinely ambiguous between sources, unresolved despite a further look in Revision 4). The tool is chosen by stem thickness (**AF-25**, High); hand pruners give way to loppers at roughly three-quarters of an inch by the majority figure (**AF-26**, Low). Tools should be sharp and clean (**AF-27**, High) and disinfected between plants (**AF-28**, Moderate). A removed stem is cut at its point of origin, no stub left (**AF-29**, Moderate). Sealing larger cuts is advised by some sources and contested by others (**AF-30**, Low) — **AF-35** (Moderate, new in Revision 3) explains the apparent contradiction: the sources for sealing and the source against it are answering different questions (protection against cane-boring insects, versus wound healing generally), not disagreeing about the same thing. **AF-38** (unleveled, new in Revision 3) adds a second RHS page corroborating the cut-mechanics findings above, and also surfaces a genuine new disagreement — two Southern Hemisphere sources say the cut's angle and bud direction don't matter at all (new conflict **C11**, §6).

### 5.7 What can be judged from a photograph

This didn't have its own group in the original Brief; Revision 3 and 4 gave it enough material to warrant one. **AF-34** (Low, new in Revision 3) reports that general plant-diagnostic guidance, outside rose literature, favours physical examination over photographs alone. **AF-41** (Low, new in Revision 4) goes further with a rose-specific, ROC-assembled breakdown: a photograph can support judging the bush's overall shape and outline, and crossing canes; it cannot reliably support judging cane vigour (a bend/flex test) or true internal health (pith colour, visible only after a cut is made) — and a cane can look sound from outside while being compromised inside. This isn't claimed to be a complete list, but it's the first citable answer this project has had to "what can Pip tell from a photo."

## 6. Where the sources disagree, and the default now adopted

Eleven disagreements are on record. None is resolved in the sense of picking a winner — the FRD preserves each as a documented conflict, per this project's standing practice. What's new in Revision 4 is that each one now carries a stated, usable default, so a Founder doesn't have to rule on it before Pip can act.

| # | What differs | Default adopted for use |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | Final height quoted from about 4 in. to 42 in.; fractions of one-third to two-thirds also given | Use the class-based mid-range already on record (AF-17/18): roughly a third to a half of last season's growth, lower for hybrid teas, higher for floribundas — not a fixed number |
| C2 | Cut distance above the bud: a quarter inch (11 sources) vs. a half inch (3 sources) | A quarter inch as the majority default; up to a half inch is a reasonable alternative for a gardener who wants to minimise dead-snag risk over bud-death risk |
| C3 | Slant direction wording: "away from the bud" vs. "same direction as the bud" (may be one geometry described two ways, or two different ones — genuinely unresolved) | Slant away from the bud, roughly 45°, as the majority wording; treat the minority wording as the same technique unless shown otherwise |
| C4 | Rose sources advise sealing larger cuts; one general woody-plant text advises against wound dressings | Not really a live conflict any more — AF-35 shows they're answering different questions. Seal cuts roughly pencil-thickness or larger, against cane borers |
| C5 | How boldly to prune: "start conservatively" vs. "when in doubt, cut it out" vs. "underpruning is the most common cause of an unproductive rose" | No default needed beyond what's already reflected in R-4's category-priority default below |
| C6 | Age of wood due for removal: RHS says three years; others give no figure or a wider one (a Revision 3 source pushed the range even wider) | Use the visual markers (AF-36) as the primary test; where a number is wanted, treat canes older than about three years (RHS) as candidates |
| C7 | Number of canes kept: ranges from 3–5 up to 6–8 depending on source | Four to six as a reasonable working default within the three-to-eight range |
| C8 | Hand-pruner-to-lopper threshold: three-quarters of an inch vs. one-half inch | Three-quarters of an inch (the majority, rose-specific figure) |
| C9 | Bypass vs. anvil pruners | Bypass as the default; anvil acceptable for dead wood only |
| C10 | Disinfectant named: 70% alcohol, alcohol wipes, Lysol, methylated spirits, bleach mixture, or just "clean, sterile tools" | 70% alcohol between plants, as a simple, well-supported default — a precaution, not a strict requirement |
| C11 | *(new)* Whether cut angle/bud direction matter at all: majority convention (45°, outward bud) vs. two Southern Hemisphere sources saying it doesn't matter | Follow the majority convention as the default; the dissenting view is noted, not adopted |

**The one real judgement call.** Separately from the eleven conflicts above, Revision 4 checked the formal ANSI A300 arboriculture standard directly at source to see whether it ranks pruning objectives — it doesn't; it leaves the call to practitioner judgement in every case. So when a stem is both a removal candidate (weak, thin) *and* one of the few canes carrying the plant's structure, no source anywhere settles which consideration wins. This is real, it will recur, and it is the one place the ROC is asking you to make a call rather than just noting a default: **the ROC's own recommendation is to lean conservative and retain the stem, reassessing at the next pruning** — consistent with the "start conservatively" convention (C5) and the preference for framework-carrying canes (AF-9). This is offered as a recommendation under EAS §2.10, not as a sourced finding, and you're free to direct something different at any time; it does not block approval either way.

## Appendix B: Glossary

**ROC** — Research Origin Curator, the role that gathers and assesses evidence, currently acted by Claude under Founder direction.
**EAS** — Evidence Assessment Standard, governing how findings are sourced, worded and leveled.
**Assessed Finding (AF-N)** — a single claim, with its evidence, strengths, limitations and remaining uncertainty stated.
**Evidence Confidence Level** — High, Moderate or Low; how well-attested a finding is, not how important it is.
**Conflict (C-N)** — a documented disagreement between sources, preserved rather than resolved by picking a winner.
**Practical default** — new in Revision 4: a stated, usable working recommendation for a conflict or gap that is genuine, permanent variation rather than a research gap. Clearly labeled as the ROC's own recommendation (EAS §2.10), not as sourced fact, and always open to Founder override.
**FRD** — Founder Review Dossier, the governed research document.
**ARC** — Approved Research Compilation, the governed knowledge record created once an FRD is approved; it keeps the same Assessed-Finding structure, just approved and renamed.
