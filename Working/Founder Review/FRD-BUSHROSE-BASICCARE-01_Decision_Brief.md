## 2. The short version

**The research.** We asked what a bush rose is, what ordinary care across a year looks like apart from pruning, what care suits a rose not yet ready for pruning, what winter protection involves, and what simple, non-diagnostic signs of poor health are worth a gardener's attention. We read 11 sources — the RHS (UK) and eight different US university extension services, plus one practitioner source corroborated by two institutional ones.

**The result, in numbers.** The research produced 13 findings (the FRD calls them Assessed Findings, F1 to F13, with F1a as a usage note rather than a finding). Confidence spans the full scale used elsewhere in the Knowledge Curation System: **1 Very High, 6 High (some only for a general pattern, not a specific figure), 3 Moderate, 2 Low, 1 Low–Moderate**, plus F1a which carries no rating at all (it's an observation about terminology, not a claim of fact). There is no single rating for the whole piece of research.

**What the sources agree on strongly.** Winter protection by mounding soil, compost or mulch 10–12 inches around the base after hard frost (F9, Very High). Deep, infrequent watering (F2); growing-season mulching 2–3 inches deep (F3); general pest and disease watchfulness (F6); the general pattern of feeding starting in spring and stopping by mid-to-late summer (F4); the general pattern of deadheading stopping in early-to-mid autumn (F5); and that a newly planted rose should be left largely uncut in its first season so it can establish (F7) — all High.

**Where the ground is thinner.** Exactly what "bush rose" means as a term appears to be UK-specific (F1, Moderate, and see F1a below). Additional winter-protection methods like collars and wrapping (F10, Moderate). Simple, non-diagnostic signs of poor health, and the idea that ordinary care matters more than pests or disease (F12, F13, Moderate and Low–Moderate) — this is the thinnest-evidenced part of the commission, because most published material on rose health is diagnostic by nature rather than general-awareness. Pre-winter watering and debris removal (F11, Low — only one source).

**A genuine and useful observation, not a finding.** None of the nine US sources used "bush rose" as a category term at all — they said "Hybrid Tea" or "Floribunda" directly. This may affect the words Ask Pip should use with gardeners outside the UK (see Decision 5 below).

**No real disagreement found.** Three things that looked at first like disagreements between sources (feeding cutoff dates, deadheading cutoff dates, mulch depth) turned out, on inspection, to be regional variation or two different practices being conflated, not genuine conflict. Details in section 5 below.

## 3. What was researched

**The commission.** Five questions about basic bush rose care, commissioned by Shaphan on 22 September 2026, because a rose not yet ready for pruning still has a gardener who wants to do something for it. At the time of commissioning, zero published PIP Knowledge Records existed in the live system.

**In scope.** What a bush rose is; ordinary year-round care apart from pruning; care for a rose not yet ready for pruning; winter protection; and simple, non-diagnostic signs of poor health.

**Out of scope.** Pruning mechanics (a separate, already-progressed commission); diagnosis of specific diseases or pests; product or chemical brand recommendations; planting a new rose; climate-specific schedules presented as universal.

**The sources.** 11 sources: the Royal Horticultural Society (UK), and university extension services from Iowa, Illinois, Missouri, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Georgia, New York and Maryland — plus one trade publication (Fine Gardening, citing a named professional rosarian) used only because its claim is independently corroborated by two institutional sources.

**A limitation worth naming up front.** All sources are from the UK or the continental US. No source from a different climate zone or gardening tradition was consulted.

## 4. How to read the strength ratings

This commission uses the full six-level Evidence Confidence Level scale (Very High, High, Moderate, Low, Very Low, Not Assigned), the same scale defined by the Evidence Assessment Standard and used across the Knowledge Curation System. Each finding carries its own rating, on its own; none is averaged or blended with another.

| Rating | Roughly means |
|---|---|
| **Very High** | Many independent, reliable sources, strongly and specifically consistent. |
| **High** | Multiple independent, reliable sources agree. |
| **Moderate** | Plausible support, but limited independent corroboration, or some inconsistency. |
| **Low** | A single source, or weak corroboration. |
| **Very Low** | Minimal or contradictory evidence. (Not used in this commission.) |
| **Not Assigned** | The evidence doesn't allow a rating — for example, because the underlying pattern is real but the specific figure itself varies by region rather than being one fixed fact. |

A rating is a judgement, not a formula, and it says how firmly recognised sources support a statement — not whether the statement is right for Pip to use.

## 5. What the research found

The same findings, with the same IDs, are in the Review Form. The formal wording of each is in FRD section 7.

### What a bush rose is (F1, F1a)

| ID | What sources say | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | UK authority (RHS) uses "bush rose" to mean modern shrub-form roses — chiefly Hybrid Tea and Floribunda — distinct from climbing/rambling, standard, and species/shrub roses. | **Moderate** |
| F1a | *(Usage note, not a finding.)* None of the nine US sources used "bush rose" as a category term; they used "Hybrid Tea," "Floribunda" etc. directly. This may be a UK-specific term rather than a universal one. | *Not rated* |

### Ordinary care across a year (F2–F6)

| ID | What sources say | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| F2 | Established bush roses are watered deeply and infrequently, roughly 1–2 inches a week when rainfall is short, with extra care in the first two years. | **High** |
| F3 | A 2–3 inch layer of organic mulch, kept a few inches clear of the stem, is commonly recommended during the growing season. | **High** |
| F4 | Feeding commonly starts in spring and stops by mid-to-late summer so growth can harden before winter — the general pattern is well supported, but the exact cutoff date varies by region and isn't a single fact. | **High** (pattern) / **Not Assigned** (any one date) |
| F5 | Deadheading is commonly stopped in early-to-mid autumn to let the plant set hips and head toward dormancy — again, the pattern is solid, the exact date varies. | **High** (pattern) / **Low** (any one date) |
| F6 | Common problems to watch for: blackspot, powdery mildew, aphids, spider mites; water at the base or in the morning to reduce fungal disease. | **High** |

### A rose not yet ready for pruning (F7)

| ID | What sources say | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| F7 | A rose planted in roughly the past year is generally left largely uncut through its first season — only spent blooms removed, not stems for arrangements — so it can put its energy into establishing. | **High** |

### Winter protection (F9–F11)

| ID | What sources say | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| F9 | After several hard frosts and dormancy, mound soil, compost or mulch 10–12 inches around the base (covering the graft union), removed again in spring. | **Very High** |
| F10 | Additional methods — a filled collar, or wrapping canes with burlap/evergreen boughs — especially for climbers or exposed sites. | **Moderate** |
| F11 | Watering thoroughly after first frost but before the ground freezes, and clearing fallen leaf debris, as part of winter prep. | **Low** (single source) |

### Simple, non-diagnostic signs of poor health (F12–F13)

| ID | What sources say | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| F12 | Regularly noticing yellowing, spotting, leaf drop, stunted growth, distorted leaves or reduced flowering is described as the useful first step — before trying to identify a specific cause. | **Moderate** |
| F13 | At least one source states plainly that ordinary care and conditions — not pests or disease — are the most common cause of a rose's decline, especially in newer plantings. | **Low–Moderate** |

*(Note: the FRD's F8, an early draft connecting idea, was withdrawn during drafting and routed to Recommendations for Further Research instead — see section 7 — because it was ROC's own reasoning rather than something a source states directly.)*

## 6. Where evidence looked like it might disagree

Three things were investigated because they looked, at first, like disagreements. None turned out to be a real conflict.

| What it looked like | What it actually is |
|---|---|
| Different feeding cutoff dates (mid-August vs. September) | Regional variation tracking each area's own first-frost timing, not disagreement about the underlying reasoning. |
| Different deadheading cutoff dates | Same — regional variation, same underlying reasoning (let the plant set hips and go dormant). |
| Very different mulch depths (2–3 inches vs. 10–12 inches) | Not a disagreement at all — the small figure is ordinary growing-season mulch, the large figure is the separate practice of winter mounding. Kept as two separate findings (F3, F9) so they aren't confused. |

## 7. What's left open — Recommendations for Further Research

These are ROC's own observations, not findings. None carries a confidence rating and none should be treated as approved on the strength of this dossier.

- **Connecting to already-approved knowledge.** This commission's findings on establishment-period care (F7 and the general pattern in F2–F6) look complementary to the already-approved RECENTPLANT and DORMANCY findings, but the connection hasn't actually been checked against those findings' own source basis.
- **Geographic diversity.** Every source is from the UK or the continental US. A source from a different climate tradition would strengthen several findings.
- **Q5 depth.** Non-diagnostic "signs of poor health" content is thin in the wider literature. Further, more targeted research could strengthen F12 and F13.
- **The "bush rose" terminology gap (F1a).** Worth its own small research pass: is "bush rose" ever used as a formal term outside the UK, or is it consistently absent?
- **F11's single-source status.** A second independent source would let this move out of Low confidence.
