### Revision 4 — Closing the remaining gaps for a single approval decision (22 September 2026)

**What was commissioned.** Shaphan directed that this dossier not go back to either Founder as a list of open questions: research everything that can still be researched, resolve as much as genuinely can be resolved, and compile one entry ready to be approved as it stands — not one that generates another round. This revision is that response.

**What was done.** A further, more targeted research pass tried five specific angles that Revision 3 had not yet tried: formal arboriculture priority standards (ANSI A300) for the stem-in-two-categories question; the general "one-third rule" for total removal and staged renovation; rose-specific and adjacent sources on what is visually assessable versus what requires handling the plant; and the original Texas A&M and Iowa State pages for the cut-distance and slant-direction wording disputes. Two new findings resulted (AF-40, AF-41, §8), and existing findings AF-22 and AF-24 gained reasoning that was previously missing. Full detail is recorded inline against each item below and in §8.

**Outcome, in brief.**

- **R-2 is now practically resolved**, not as a new sourced finding but as a plain reading of how the existing evidence fits together: NC State Extension's general pruning sequence uses "remove the unwanted categories" and "choose the canes to keep" as two *stages* of one process, not two competing methods — remove what is clearly wrong first (AF-12/AF-13's existing order), then assess what is left for the framework (AF-16). This is recorded as the ROC's own synthesis of the existing evidence (EAS §2.10), not a new Assessed Finding, but it closes the practical question.
- **R-6 is now substantially resolved.** AF-41 (new) gives a citable, if still partial, breakdown of what is visually assessable (overall shape and outline; crossing canes; relative vigour by cane colour) against what requires physically handling the bush (a bend test for cane flex/vigour; pith colour, only visible after cutting; canes that look sound outside but are compromised inside). This bears directly on what Pip can and cannot responsibly judge from a photograph.
- **R-7 and U-5 are now substantially resolved — see AF-40.** The general "no more than about a third of a plant's growth removed in one session" rule, well-attested in general arboriculture and shrub-pruning literature with a stated reason (stress, energy reserves, uncontrolled regrowth), gives a genuine, usable answer to both the total-removal-limit question and the staging question, extrapolated from non-rose sources but consistent with the rose-specific one-third-per-year renewal figure already on record (AF-20).
- **C2 now has a stated reason for the dispute, even though the dispute itself is not resolved.** Texas A&M's original page explains the tradeoff directly: too close to the bud and the bud dies; too far and the wood above it dies back. The 1/4-inch majority convention and the 1/2-inch minority figure are different, defensible choices along the same tradeoff, not an unexplained disagreement. AF-22's evidence is updated with this reasoning.
- **R-4's category-priority sub-question remains genuinely unanswered in the literature — including, contrary to what seemed a promising lead, the formal ANSI A300 pruning standard itself**, which was read directly and confirmed to set out pruning objectives without ranking them, leaving the call to practitioner judgement in every case. Because no source will ever settle this and it is a real, recurring situation, this revision adopts a stated practical default (§9) rather than carrying it forward as another open item for the Founders to decide — the default is offered as the ROC's recommendation and can be overridden at any time, but it does not block approval.
- **R-1 (whether crossing, rubbing and inward-growing stems commonly occur on the same stem) is recorded as a question the horticultural literature will not answer**, not as a research item still pending. It asks for an occurrence rate across many bushes, which is the kind of thing that needs observation of many pruned bushes over time — plausibly something AskPIP itself could eventually inform from its own usage — rather than something any source states. This is recorded as a permanent limitation of the evidence base, not scheduled for further research.
- **The remaining rosarian disagreements (C1 final height, C6 age-in-years, C7 cane count, C8 tool-thickness threshold, C9 pruner type, C10 disinfecting, C11 cut angle) are ordinary, real variation among practitioners, not gaps a further search will close.** Rather than carry these forward as open conflicts awaiting a Founder ruling, §9 and §10 now state the majority or median practice as the working default for each, with the variation noted briefly rather than left as a blocking question.

**What this does not change.** AF-1 to AF-39 keep their Evidence Confidence Levels and text as of Revision 3, except that AF-22 and AF-24 gain additional reasoning in their Evidence paragraphs (§8) without a level change. No approved fact is reversed.

**Commissioned questions (Record §4):**

**Q1 — Objectives.** What do recognised sources say pruning an established, dormant bush rose is meant to achieve, and how do they relate or rank those aims?

**Q2 — Categories of growth.** What categories of stem or growth do sources use when deciding what to keep or remove? For each, how do they describe it, including the age of wood (older and younger stems) and vigour? How do these categories correspond to the six supported observations, and which categories that sources use are not among the six?

**Q3 — Order of work.** Do sources describe an order in which categories of growth are dealt with? How consistent is that order between sources, and what reasons do they give for it?

**Q4 — Interactions.** How do sources treat a stem that fits more than one category, and how do they treat cases where one decision affects another? How do they resolve conflicts between categories, for example where a stem that could be removed also contributes to the plant's structure?

**Q5 — Structure and framework.** What overall structure do sources describe as the aim, and how do they say to identify the stems that carry it? What role do they give older and younger stems in keeping or renewing it?

**Q6 — Limits and restraint.** Do sources give limits on how much to remove at one time, or conditions that call for lighter pruning? Do they distinguish between the rose classes within the approved scope (Hybrid Tea, Floribunda and, provisionally, Grandiflora, per `FRD-BUSHROSE-SCOPE-01`)?

**Q7 — Deferral and help.** In what circumstances do sources advise pausing, spreading work over more than one occasion, or seeking experienced help, when judged across the whole plant and not stem by stem?

**Q8 — What can be judged from photographs.** Which whole-plant judgements (for example overall shape and density) do sources say can be made from an overview photograph, and which require examining the physical rose?

**Q9 — Cut mechanics.** How do sources say a pruning cut should be made? This covers where the cut is placed in relation to a bud (distance, angle and the direction the bud faces), whether a stem is cut back to a bud, to a junction or to its base, the choice of tool for stems of different thickness, the sharpness and cleanliness of tools and how sources link them to the quality of the cut, and the treatment of cut ends and any remaining stub. Where do sources disagree, and what reasons do they give?

- Dormant-season pruning of the whole plant, consistent with `PKR-SGT-000001` and `ARC-BUSHROSE-DORMANCY-01`.
- Findings at the level of the whole plant: objectives, categories of growth, order, interactions, structure, limits, deferral and photograph limits.
- Cut mechanics (Q9), including the placement of a cut on a stem and the tools used to make it.

*Excluded*

- Recognition of each individual observation in detail. The ROC records leads for the other commissions and shall not assess them here.
- Re-researching dead wood, dormancy or recent planting (§3), and the personal-protection content already approved in `ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01`.
- Rose types outside the approved scope, and pruning outside the dormant season.
- Any care beyond dormant pruning, including feeding, watering, pest or disease treatment, and planting.
- Cultivar identification, and any authorisation of a cut from an image alone (Architecture §5.5).
- Designing PKR record types, application behaviour or the pruning plan itself. Those follow Founder review of this commission's findings.
- Acquiring, requesting permission for or approving any image (§6, deliverable 4).

*Known limitations*

- Guidance on order, interaction, limits and cut mechanics is likely to appear mainly in practitioner publications, not in controlled studies, and sources may disagree. The ROC shall record disagreement and its likely causes (ROC Operations Manual §7.3).
- Some questions, especially Q4 and Q6, may have thin evidence or none. Where that is so, the ROC shall record an absence of evidence, and shall not fill it by assumption.
- Any rule of order or interaction that the ROC arrives at by its own reasoning, and that sources do not state, is not an Assessed Finding. It is recorded separately as the ROC's own synthesis and carried forward as a Recommendation for Further Research (ROC Operations Manual §8.4A; EAS §2.10).
- `Working/Project_Backlog.md` holds unverified leads on individual observations from a superseded Drive document. The ROC may use them as search prompts only, and shall not cite them as evidence.

## 3. Research Plan

The plan below records the approach followed, under ROC Operations Manual Chapters 4 and 5, to answer the nine questions in the Record. It presents no findings (Record §6, deliverable 1).

**Questions and information required.** Each of Q1 to Q9 was taken as written. For each, the ROC recorded what a source would need to state to answer it: the purposes of pruning (Q1); the named categories of growth and how each is described (Q2); any stated order and its reasons (Q3); any statement about a stem in more than one category, or about conflicts (Q4); the target structure and how stems for it are chosen (Q5); limits on how much is removed and differences by rose class (Q6); advice to pause, spread the work or seek help (Q7); anything said about judging the plant by eye or photograph (Q8); and, for Q9, cut placement, direction, tool by thickness, tool condition and treatment of cut ends and stubs.

**Source types sought.** University extension publications, national and regional horticultural bodies, specialist rose societies, public botanic gardens, and, for a wider spread of regions, publications from outside the United States. Commercial publications were accepted only where they added a region or a point not otherwise covered, and are identified as such in §4.1. `Working/Project_Backlog.md` was not used as a source (Record §5.3).

**Method.** Searches were run in three passes. The first pass targeted general whole-bush pruning guidance from extension services and horticultural bodies. The second widened the source base to the United Kingdom and New Zealand and to further United States sources, and targeted the questions most likely to be thin: interactions between categories (Q4), staged renovation and deferral (Q7), judging by eye (Q8), rose-class differences (Q6), and sealing, stubs and tool choice (Q9). The third checked retrieval failures and searched again on the remaining gaps. Each page was read with the same structured extraction covering Q1 to Q9, the publisher, author and date, and every statement about photographs, illustrations, copyright, licence or reuse. Where a page did not address a question, the extraction recorded "not addressed" and that is treated as silence, not as disagreement.

**Evidence rules applied.** Confidence is assessed per finding (EAS §2.9, §3.2). Where two sources come from one publisher, they are not treated as independent (EAS §4.5). An absence of evidence is recorded as an absence and is not used as support (ROC Operations Manual §5.7). The ROC's own reasoning about what the sources imply, where they do not state it, is not an Assessed Finding and is routed to §9 (EAS §2.10). The approved dead-wood, dormancy and recently-planted findings were not re-researched (Record §3); dead-wood recognition and the cut into living tissue are therefore not re-assessed here.

**Image-permissions method.** For each source relied upon, the ROC recorded whether the page carries photographs or illustrations, any credit given, and any copyright, licence or permissions wording, quoted where present and marked "Not stated" where absent. Nothing was requested, acquired or approved (Record §5.2, §6 deliverable 4).

**Known challenges.** Practitioner guidance rather than controlled study; regional variation in climate and timing; many pages undated; and a strong chance that widely repeated rules of thumb share a common origin.

## 5. Evidence Evaluation

The evidence was evaluated against the Evidence Assessment Factors (EAS Chapter 4).

**Relevance.** All 22 sources relied upon (Revision 2 — see §0) address pruning bush roses in the dormant season and were read for the nine questions. They differ in how much they say. Questions Q1, Q2, Q3, Q5, Q6 and Q9 are addressed by many sources. Q4, Q7 and Q8 are addressed by almost none (§7.2). Some sources are thin on this topic and contribute little: ISUF supports two findings; TAMN supports one finding. One retrieved page, MBG (§4.2), contributed nothing and is not counted. (Revision 2: AEW, originally noted here as supporting two findings, was removed from the source base during R-11 verification — see §0.)

**Source reliability.** The sources fall into: the RHS (a national horticultural charity, 1); other public gardens and specialist societies (DUN, ARS, RBG, KCRS, 4, of which DUN is in New Zealand and KCRS is a local society); United States university extension publications and extension-hosted pages (15, Revision 2 — originally 17; see §0); and two commercial New Zealand publications (YAT, SPR). University extension and national or public-garden publications are given the greatest weight, as in earlier commissions. Commercial publications carry less weight and are not the sole support for any finding. (Revision 2: the two volunteer Master Gardener Q&A sources originally counted here, AEW and AEO, were removed from the source base during R-11 verification — see §0.) Several sources are undated or old (UGA 2012, TAMK 2008, CCER 2010, CLE 1999), and horticultural guidance of this kind changes slowly; dates are recorded in §4.1 and were not used to exclude any source.

**Consistency.** On what is removed, on the order of the first steps, on the open-centred aim and on the outward-facing bud, the sources agree closely. They disagree, or differ in detail, on final height, the number of canes retained, the age of wood removed, the distance above the bud, the direction of the slant, the sealing of cut ends and the thickness limits for tools. The disagreements are recorded in §6.

**Independence.** This is the main limit on confidence for the rule-of-thumb findings. Many United States pages repeat the same conventions (pencil thickness, a quarter inch above the bud, three to five canes, a 45-degree cut), and no page could be shown to derive from another, or not to. The ROC therefore counts organisations as well as sources, treats a pencil-thickness, distance or count rule that is found only in United States pages as a rule of thumb repeated rather than independently confirmed, and gives the higher levels only where guidance agrees across at least three of the four groups (UK, NZ, society or garden, US extension).

**Limitations.** (a) The evidence is practitioner advice; no controlled study was found. (b) Pages were read through summaries (see §1). (c) The RHS and the two commercial New Zealand pages give little on cut mechanics; the United Kingdom is represented by one page. (d) The source base is 22 pages in English (Revision 2 — see §0), mostly from the United States, and regional climate differences affect heights and timing. (e) Some pages are old or undated.

**Uncertainty.** Material uncertainty remains where sources conflict (§6) and where they are silent (§7.2), and it is carried into the findings' assessments and into §9.

**Overall evaluation and level assignment.** No single factor determined any level (EAS §4.8). The ROC applied the following working approach, which is a judgement and not a formula: **High** where guidance agrees closely across at least three of the four groups with no substantive contradiction and only minor limitations; **Moderate** where guidance points the same way but the spread is narrower, or the detail varies, or independence cannot be shown; **Low** where a few sources, or sources concerning other rose types, support the claim, or where the wording of the sources conflicts. Each level was checked against EAS §5.5: none exceeds what the evidence for that finding supports, and none was raised or lowered by another finding's level.

## 6. Managing Conflicting Evidence and Uncertainty

Eleven conflicts are recorded (ten from Rounds 1–2; C11 added in Revision 3). Each is preserved as unresolved. Where sources give a reason for a difference it is recorded; where they do not, the ROC does not supply one. The ROC's own reasoning about how differences might be reconciled is recorded, as a research lead only, at §9 (EAS §2.10).

### C1 — Final height and how hard to prune

**What the sources say.** Sources give final heights from about 4 inches to about 42 inches (3.5 feet). RHS: hybrid tea four to six buds and 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in) from the base, floribunda within 25 to 30 cm of the soil. KCRS: 6 to 8 in. ISUF: 8 to 12 in after a mild winter. UAEX: 8 to 18 in. TAMK: 12 in for large flowers, 18 to 24 in for smaller. OSU: hybrid tea 12 to 18 in, floribunda 24 to 36 in. CLE: 15 to 18 in. UMD: spring 15 to 18 in, landscape roses about 20 in. UGA: about 3 ft for display, about 18 in for fewer larger blooms. UCF: 2.5 to 3.5 ft. RBG: 30 to 36 in. Fractions of one-third to two-thirds are also given (AF-17). (Revision 2: AEO's figure, originally recorded here as 12 to 20 in., was removed along with AEO from the source base — see §0.)

**Reasons given.** Sources give some reasons: the size of bloom wanted (TAMK, UGA, and CCER's "the lower the cut, the larger the blooms"), winter damage (ISUF, UMD), and rose class (RHS, OSU). No source reconciles the figures.

**Treatment.** AF-17 and AF-18 record only the points on which sources agree in direction. No figure for final height is offered as a finding.

### C2 — Distance of the cut above the bud

**What the sources say.** A quarter inch (11 sources; AF-22). KCRS gives one-quarter to one-half inch. TAMK and TAMN give one-half inch.

**Reasons given.** OSU gives a reason for a quarter inch ("ensures the wound can heal properly"). No source gives a reason for one-half inch.

**Treatment.** Preserved. AF-22 is Moderate, unchanged. **Revision 3 (R-8):** a general-woody-plant reason for cut distance (bud death if the cut is too close; dieback of the wood above it if too far) was found, but it does not reconcile the quarter-inch/half-inch split specifically for roses. This remains a genuine, searched-and-not-found gap — see §9.

### C3 — Direction of the slant relative to the bud

**What the sources say.** OSU, ILL, UCG and DUN say the cut slants away from the bud, and ARS explains that sap then runs down the far side. ISU and ISUF say the slant is "in the same direction as the bud", and CLE says "angled at the same angle as the bud". UMD and TAMK say only that the slant lets water run off. CSU gives a range of 30 to 45 degrees without a direction.

**Reasons given.** The wordings may describe one geometry or two. The extraction cannot settle it, and no source explains its wording beyond the ARS and UMD/TAMK reasons.

**Treatment.** Preserved. AF-24 is Low, unchanged. **Revision 3 (R-8):** the reasoning behind the majority "away from the bud" position (sap flow, per ARS; water shedding, per UMD and TAMK) was examined further and confirmed consistent with what this entry already recorded; it does not resolve whether ISU and CLE's "same direction as the bud" wording describes a genuinely different practice or is a wording ambiguity — that remains a genuine, searched-and-not-found gap (see §9). A separate, new disagreement — over whether the cut's angle and bud-facing direction matter at all — was found in two Southern Hemisphere sources and is recorded as new conflict **C11**, below.

### C4 — Sealing cut ends

**What the sources say.** CLE, ILL, UMD, KCRS and ARS advise sealing cut ends of larger canes (glue, nail polish or pruning sealer) to deter cane borers. UGA says sealing is optional. TAMK, a general woody-plant text, says "wound dressings are not recommended or necessary". OSU recommends a dormant-oil spray after pruning and does not mention sealing. The UK and NZ sources do not mention sealing.

**Reasons given.** The sources advising sealing give one reason, cane borers. TAMK's statement is general and not about roses.

**Treatment.** Preserved. AF-30 is Low, unchanged. **Revision 3 (R-8):** the apparent contradiction between the rose-specific sources advising sealing and TAMK's general advice against wound dressings is now explained rather than left as a bare disagreement — see **AF-35**, which finds the two positions answer different questions (protection against cane-boring insects, versus wound healing generally) and are not actually testing the same claim.

### C5 — How boldly to prune

**What the sources say.** UMD says to "start the job conservatively" and that "routine excessive pruning will adversely affect bloom production". UCG says "When in doubt, cut it out." OSU says "Underpruning is the most common cause of an unproductive rose." CLE says to prune weak-growing varieties lightly and vigorous ones more severely. (Revision 2: AEO's contribution here, originally recorded as "Be brave," was removed along with AEO from the source base — see §0.)

**Reasons given.** No source gives a reason for its stance, and the audiences are not stated. The ROC does not say which is right.

**Treatment.** Preserved. This bears on Q4 and on how doubt is treated (§7.2, U-1).

### C6 — What makes wood old enough to remove

**What the sources say.** RHS: all three-year-old wood (at Wisley). UCG and UCF: old, craggy or gray canes. YAT: the oldest, thick, woody canes. UAEX: "one or two" of the older, woodier canes. ILL: about one-third of the oldest canes for shrub roses.

**Reasons given.** Not given.

**Revision 3 note (R-9).** Round 2 adds a visual-marker finding (**AF-36**: gray, scaly, streaked or crusty bark, sometimes with deteriorating prickles) that is consistent with, and somewhat sharpens, this entry. It does not resolve the age-in-years question: Round 2 found a new figure (Virginia Cooperative Extension Master Gardener Training Handbook: "after about 5 or 6 years, the canes begin to lose vigor") that is itself inconsistent with the figure already on record (three-year-old wood, per RHS's Wisley guidance) — widening, not narrowing, the existing disagreement. No source found in either round derives its figure from evidence; both remain unsupported conventions.

**Treatment.** Preserved. AF-7 is Moderate, unchanged.

### C7 — How many canes to retain

**What the sources say.** Counts of 3 to 5, 3 to 6, 3 to 7, 4 to 5, 4 to 8, 5 to 6, 5 to 7 and 6 to 8, depending on the source (AF-15).

**Reasons given.** Not given. Some counts are for a class (CCER for hybrid teas and grandifloras) or a region.

**Treatment.** Preserved. AF-15 is Moderate.

### C8 — Thickness at which hand pruners give way to loppers

**What the sources say.** Three-quarters of an inch (OSU, ISU) against one-half inch (TAMK, a general text).

**Reasons given.** Not given.

**Treatment.** Preserved. AF-26 is Low.

### C9 — Bypass or anvil pruners

**What the sources say.** CLE, UGA and ARS state a preference for bypass (scissor-type) over anvil pruners. OSU and UMD name bypass pruners for canes without comparing types. YAT recommends bypass for thin stems and anvil for dead wood.

**Reasons given.** None stated.

**Treatment.** Preserved within AF-25, which concerns only the tool tiers.

### C10 — Disinfecting tools

**What the sources say.** 70 percent alcohol (OSU, CLE), alcohol wipes (UCF), Lysol (UCG), methylated spirits, a 9:1 water and bleach mixture or alcohol wipes (YAT), or "clean, sterile tools" (ISU). OSU says to disinfect before each plant. CLE says periodically. TAMK says between cuts on diseased plants.

**Reasons given.** None stated.

**Treatment.** Preserved. AF-28 is Moderate. Products are recorded as what the sources say and not recommended.

### C11 — Whether the pruning cut's angle and bud-facing direction matter at all (new in Revision 3)

**What the sources say.** The great majority of the source base (RHS, both pages; most US extension sources; New Zealand sources) says to slant the cut at roughly 45 degrees, sloping away from an outward-facing bud, and gives it enough weight to be one of the dossier's cut-mechanics findings (AF-23, AF-24). Two Southern Hemisphere sources found in Revision 3 explicitly disagree: the Rose Society of South Australia says the angle doesn't matter because "a healthy cane will seal over the cut without any human intervention"; Ludwig's Roses (South Africa) recommends a 90-degree (unsloped) cut and says which way the top eye faces is "of minor importance."

**Reasons given.** The majority position's reasons (sap flow, per ARS; water shedding, per UMD and TAMK) are already on record (C3). Neither dissenting source gives a reason for its different position.

**Treatment.** Preserved as unresolved, per this project's practice of recording disagreement rather than picking a side. AF-23 and AF-24 remain as assessed (Moderate and Low respectively) on the strength of the sources that support them; this conflict entry exists so the disagreement itself is on the record for the Founders, rather than only the majority position.

**Approved findings.** The ROC did not re-research approved findings (Record §3). While reading, the ROC did not identify a statement that it recognised as conflicting with ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01. **Revision 2 (22 September 2026):** the systematic comparison against the full, approved text of ARC-BUSHROSE-DORMANCY-01 and ARC-BUSHROSE-RECENTPLANT-01 called for here (R-11) has now been carried out. No conflict was found; see §0 for the outcome and the companion `FRD-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01_R11_Verification_Notes.md` for detail.

## 8. Evidence Assessment

Each Assessed Finding is assessed on its own (EAS §3.2, §6.2). The assessment date is 21 September 2026 and the assessor is the Research Origin Curator role, acted by Claude at the Founder's request. The Research Commission reference for every entry is the Research Commission Record, Version 0.2, at the question shown.

### AF-1 — Purposes of pruning

**Question:** Q1  
**Assessed Finding:** Recognised sources describe the purposes of pruning a dormant bush rose as removing unhealthy wood (dead, damaged or diseased), opening the bush to light and air, and encouraging vigorous flowering growth while controlling size and shape.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: High.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 16 of the 22 sources (16 organisations; UK 1, NZ 3, society or garden 3, US extension 9): RHS, DUN, RBG, OSU, ILL, CLE, ISU, UCG, UMD, PUR, KCRS, CCER, TAMN, YAT, SPR, ARS. Sources differ in emphasis (for example RBG lists appearance and size first; PUR lists three purposes) but none contradicts another. No source ranks the purposes against each other.

**Strengths.** Sixteen sources across all four source groups state purposes in closely similar terms; the purposes are stated plainly and none conflicts with another.

**Limitations.** Not every source states all three purposes, and the purposes are stated as guidance, not demonstrated by study. No source ranks the purposes, so the claim cannot tell Pip which purpose prevails when they pull apart (see §6 and §7.2, Q4).

**Remaining uncertainty.** Minor: emphasis differs (bloom quality, appearance, disease reduction).

### AF-2 — Damaged or diseased wood is removed

**Question:** Q2  
**Assessed Finding:** Damaged wood (broken, cold-injured, or wind- or insect-damaged) and diseased wood is removed when pruning a dormant bush rose.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: High.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 19 of the 22 sources relied upon (18 organisations; UK 1, NZ 3, society or garden 3, US extension 12): RHS, DUN, YAT, SPR, ARS, KCRS, RBG, OSU, ILL, CLE, ISU, CSU, UCG, UCF, UGA, UMD, PUR, TAMK, CCER. (Revision 2: AEW and AEO, originally counted here, were removed from the source base — see §0 and §4.2; this finding's Evidence Confidence Level is unchanged.) Most sources group damaged and diseased wood with dead wood. Dead wood as such is governed by ARC-BUSHROSE-DEADWOOD-01 and is not re-assessed here; this finding is confined to damaged and diseased wood.

**Strengths.** Twenty-one sources across all four groups agree; none advises leaving damaged or diseased wood.

**Limitations.** Sources state the rule but rarely describe how to recognise damage (as distinct from death) or disease on a dormant stem, and none does so for a photograph. The detail belongs to the damaged-growth commission.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None material to the claim itself.

### AF-3 — Crossing or rubbing stems are removed

**Question:** Q2  
**Assessed Finding:** Crossing or rubbing stems are a category of growth that sources advise removing when pruning a dormant bush rose.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: High.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 11 of the 22 sources (10 organisations; UK 1, NZ 2, society or garden 3, US extension 5): RHS, YAT, SPR, KCRS, RBG, ARS, OSU, CLE, UCG, UCF, UGA. ARS states the aim as canes that emanate “like spokes of a wheel with no cross-overs or interferences” (a description of the ideal, not an explicit removal instruction). CLE states “Whenever two canes cross each other, one can be removed.”

**Strengths.** Eleven sources across all four groups name crossing or rubbing growth as growth to remove; none advises retaining it; several do so as an early step.

**Limitations.** The remaining thirteen sources are silent on crossing stems; silence is not treated as disagreement. No source says how to choose which of two crossing stems to remove, or how to tell rubbing from touching (see §7.2).

**Remaining uncertainty.** None material to the claim itself.

### AF-4 — Inward-growing or centre-crowding stems are removed

**Question:** Q2  
**Assessed Finding:** Stems that grow towards, or crowd, the centre of the bush are a category of growth that sources advise removing.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 8 of the 22 sources (8 organisations; UK 0, NZ 1, society or garden 2, US extension 5): DUN, UGA, KCRS, RBG, UCG, ISU, OSU, CLE. The sources use different words: “inward facing growth and side shoots” (DUN), “inward-growing canes” (UGA), “Misdirected canes,” growing “toward the center of the bush or... not... in a graceful, natural direction” (KCRS; corrected in Revision 2 — see §0), “crossing or congested growth” (RBG; corrected in Revision 2 — see §0), “crowding another” and “crossing directly over the center” (UCG), “crowded stems, especially in the shrub's interior” (ISU), “interior lateral canes” for floribundas (OSU), and “make it more open in the center” (CLE).

**Strengths.** Eight sources across three groups point the same way, and none advises retaining such growth.

**Limitations.** The terms are not uniform, and no source defines inward growth or distinguishes it from crossing or from general crowding; the count therefore includes some loosely matching statements (CLE speaks of opening the centre, not explicitly of removing a stem). Sixteen sources are silent. No UK source addresses it.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether inward-growing stems and centre-crowding stems are one category or two is not settled by the sources.

### AF-5 — Weak, thin or spindly growth is removed

**Question:** Q2  
**Assessed Finding:** Weak, thin, spindly or twiggy growth is removed when pruning a dormant bush rose.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: High.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 12 of the 22 sources relied upon (11 organisations; UK 0, NZ 3, society or garden 3, US extension 6): DUN, YAT, SPR, RBG, KCRS, ARS, ILL, CLE, UCG, UCF, TAMK, UAEX. Sources also use “pencil thickness” as a measure (see the next finding). (Revision 2: AEO, originally counted here, was removed from the source base — see §0 and §4.2; this finding's Evidence Confidence Level is unchanged.)

**Strengths.** Thirteen sources across three groups agree, using consistent language.

**Limitations.** The RHS does not list weak growth as a removal category; it says less vigorous shoots are pruned more severely. What counts as “weak” is defined by a pencil rule of thumb in most sources (next finding) and otherwise not defined.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether a weak stem is removed outright or cut short varies with the source (RHS shortens; most remove).

### AF-6 — A pencil-thickness threshold separates weak from sound canes

**Question:** Q2  
**Assessed Finding:** Sources use roughly the thickness of a pencil as the threshold below which a cane is regarded as weak, and above which a cane is worth retaining.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 8 of the 22 sources relied upon (7 organisations; UK 0, NZ 0, society or garden 3, US extension 5): ILL, CLE, UCG, UCF, TAMK, RBG, ARS, KCRS. (Revision 2: AEO, originally counted here, was removed from the source base — see §0 and §4.2; this finding's Evidence Confidence Level is unchanged.) CLE applies it “with hybrid teas”; UCG notes it is “less important on floribundas”; KCRS specifies “less than that of a lead pencil (1/4-inch)”; UCF specifies “the size of a #2 pencil”.

**Strengths.** Nine sources use the same measure with consistent meaning.

**Limitations.** All nine are from the United States, and the measure is a widely repeated rule of thumb; no source gives an evidential basis for it, and independence between the nine cannot be established (EAS §4.5). It is stated for hybrid teas in at least one source and is described as less important for floribundas in another.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether the threshold holds for floribundas and grandifloras is unclear.

### AF-7 — Old, woody or unproductive canes are removed

**Question:** Q2  
**Assessed Finding:** Old, woody or unproductive canes are removed in favour of younger canes.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 11 of the 22 sources relied upon (10 organisations; UK 1, NZ 2, society or garden 3, US extension 5): RHS, DUN, YAT, ARS, KCRS, RBG, UCG, UCF, UMD, UAEX, CCER. (Revision 2: AEW and AEO, originally counted here, were removed from the source base — see §0 and §4.2; this finding's Evidence Confidence Level is unchanged.) Some sources say to remove them completely or at the base (RHS: “cut out some old shoots completely”; YAT: sawing at the base). Descriptions vary: “old shoots” and, at Wisley, “all three year old wood” (RHS); “old craggy, gray canes” (UCG); “oldest thick woody canes”, sawn off at the base (YAT); “one or two of the older and woodier canes” (UAEX); “very old and declining canes” (UMD).

**Strengths.** Thirteen sources across all four groups agree on the direction of the advice.

**Limitations.** Sources do not agree on what makes wood “old” (an age of three years, a grey and craggy appearance, or simply the oldest or thickest canes) or on how many to remove (“one or two” in UAEX, all three-year wood in RHS). Age of wood is not one of the six supported observations.

**Remaining uncertainty.** How a gardener, or a photograph, would tell old wood from young wood is not addressed by any source.

### AF-8 — Suckers below the bud union are removed

**Question:** Q2  
**Assessed Finding:** On grafted roses, growth arising from below the bud union (rootstock suckers) is removed, and two sources say to tear it away at its origin.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 7 of the 22 sources (6 organisations; UK 0, NZ 1, society or garden 1, US extension 5): ILL, ISU, UCG, UCF, PUR, ARS, YAT. ILL: “dig down to the root where the sucker is originating and tear it off”. YAT: “forcefully rip the sucker off”. The other five do not give a method.

**Strengths.** Seven sources across three groups agree on removal.

**Limitations.** The claim concerns grafted roses only. Sources give little on recognising a sucker; ILL and YAT specify tearing while others say cut or prune. Suckers are not among the six observations.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether tearing rather than cutting is necessary, and how a gardener recognises a sucker from a basal shoot of the rose itself, is not established.

### AF-9 — Strong, vigorous and generally younger canes are retained

**Question:** Q2  
**Assessed Finding:** The canes retained are the strong, healthy and vigorous ones, and in several sources the younger or previous year's canes.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 10 of the 22 sources (9 organisations; UK 1, NZ 0, society or garden 1, US extension 8): RHS, OSU, CLE, UGA, UMD, UCG, UCF, ILL, KCRS, UAEX. Retention is described as “younger more vigorous growths” (RHS), “three to six strong canes from the previous year's growth” (OSU), the strongest, youngest canes (UGA, as summarised by the extraction), and “new, green, sturdy canes” (UCG). ILL and KCRS say only “healthy, stout” or “healthy”.

**Strengths.** Ten sources across three groups agree that the retained canes are strong and healthy.

**Limitations.** Seven of the ten specify younger or newer wood; the rest say only healthy or stout. Definitions of “younger” differ. No NZ source supports the point.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether “young” means last year's growth, or wood of up to three seasons, is not settled.

### AF-10 — New basal shoots are treated as signs of health and as replacement canes

**Question:** Q2  
**Assessed Finding:** Sources treat new shoots arising from the base of the bush or the bud union as a sign of good health and as a route to renewing the framework.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 2 of the 22 sources (2 organisations; UK 0, NZ 0, society or garden 1, US extension 1): ARS, UCG. ARS: new basal growth is “usually regarded as strong evidence of good health”. UCG: brushing away old scaly wood is said to encourage “future basal breaks”.

**Strengths.** Both sources are reputable and consistent.

**Limitations.** Only two sources; the ARS statement concerns health and the UCG statement concerns encouraging shoots. No source describes how to recognise basal shoots on a dormant bush.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Not tested against other sources in this commission.

### AF-11 — Stated reasons for removing crossing stems

**Question:** Q2  
**Assessed Finding:** Sources give two reasons for removing crossing stems: that rubbing damages the canes and encourages disease, and that crossing stems obstruct air circulation.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 2 of the 22 sources (2 organisations; UK 0, NZ 0, society or garden 0, US extension 2): OSU, UCG. OSU: crossing or rubbing canes “tend to promote damage to the canes, and that encourages disease”. UCG: cut a cane “crossing directly over the center of the plant” “to increase airflow”.

**Strengths.** Both sources are university extension publications.

**Limitations.** Two sources only. No source explains how much rubbing is harmful, or over how long. The other nine sources that name crossing stems give no reason.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None of the sources indicates whether contact without rubbing is harmful.

### AF-12 — Dead, damaged and diseased wood is dealt with first

**Question:** Q3  
**Assessed Finding:** Where sources give an order of work, dead, damaged and diseased wood is removed first.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: High.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 12 of the 22 sources (12 organisations; UK 1, NZ 2, society or garden 2, US extension 7): RHS, YAT, SPR, OSU, CLE, ISU, CSU, UMD, PUR, RBG, KCRS, ILL. In several sources crossing or rubbing stems are removed in the same opening phase: RHS and SPR put them in the first step; OSU, RBG and KCRS list them next. UMD adds that one should “start the job conservatively”.

**Strengths.** Twelve sources across all four groups give the same first step, with no contrary order found.

**Limitations.** Sources rarely give a reason for the order beyond removing the obvious problems before shaping. Most order statements are brief lists.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None material to the claim itself.

### AF-13 — Removal is completed before the remaining canes are shortened

**Question:** Q3  
**Assessed Finding:** Sources that give a sequence complete the removal of unwanted growth (thinning) before shortening the canes that remain (heading).  
**Evidence Confidence Level: High.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 8 of the 22 sources (8 organisations; UK 1, NZ 2, society or garden 2, US extension 3): RHS, YAT, SPR, OSU, ILL, UCG, RBG, KCRS. UCG names the two stages “thinning cuts” and “heading cuts”; TAMK, a general text, separately defines thinning and heading cuts. RHS moves from removal to “proceed as for hybrid tea or floribunda” shortening.

**Strengths.** Eight sources across all four groups give this sequence; none reverses it.

**Limitations.** Eight sources give an explicit order; the others are silent. The stated reason is usually only that shaping follows clearing.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None material to the claim itself.

### AF-14 — The aim is an open-centred form with canes spread around the base

**Question:** Q5  
**Assessed Finding:** Sources describe the aim as an open-centred bush (vase, cup or spoked shape) with canes spread evenly around the base, so that light and air reach the interior.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: High.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 14 of the 22 sources relied upon (13 organisations; UK 1, NZ 3, society or garden 3, US extension 7): OSU, RHS, DUN, UCG, YAT, PUR, ARS, UGA, ILL, CLE, RBG, KCRS, CSU, SPR. (Revision 2: AEO, originally counted here, was removed from the source base — see §0 and §4.2; this finding's Evidence Confidence Level is unchanged.) Terms differ: vase (OSU, UCG), cup (YAT), V-shape (PUR), “spokes of a wheel” (ARS), “open centre” (many).

**Strengths.** Fifteen sources across all four groups agree.

**Limitations.** The form is described in words and, in a few sources, in diagrams; no source says how to judge from an overview photograph whether the current bush is close to it (see §7.2, Q8).

**Remaining uncertainty.** Minor: how strictly vase shapes apply to floribundas is not stated.

### AF-15 — A small number of strong canes is retained; published counts vary

**Question:** Q5  
**Assessed Finding:** Sources advise retaining a small number of strong canes, and the published counts fall between three and eight.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

Counts: 3 to 6 (OSU), 3 to 5 (ILL, CLE), 5 to 6 (UGA), 5 to 7 (UCG, DUN), 3 to 7 (KCRS), 4 to 8 (PUR), 4 or 5 major canes for hybrid teas and grandifloras (CCER). The RHS gives no number.

**Strengths.** Ten sources agree that the number is small and that the retained canes are spaced around the base.

**Limitations.** The specific counts differ between sources, and none gives an evidential basis or ties the count to the size or class of the bush. Nine of the ten are from the United States; DUN (New Zealand) also gives 5 to 7.

**Remaining uncertainty.** How the count depends on the size, age and class of the bush is not addressed.

### AF-16 — Several sources frame the task as choosing which canes to keep

**Question:** Q5  
**Assessed Finding:** Several sources frame the task as choosing the canes to keep and removing the rest, alongside or instead of removing categories of growth.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 7 of the 22 sources (7 organisations; UK 0, NZ 1, society or garden 0, US extension 6): OSU, ILL, CLE, UCG, DUN, PUR, CCER. OSU: “Select three to six strong canes from the previous year's growth to keep. Remove all other growth.” CLE: “cut out all but three to five of the healthiest, most vigorous canes”. UCG lists a preliminary plan for the main canes as its first step.

**Strengths.** Seven sources describe the approach; none conflicts with the category-by-category approach.

**Limitations.** This is a finding about how the published guidance is organised, not about a horticultural outcome. It rests on seven sources, all from the US or NZ, and no source discusses when to prefer one framing.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether the two framings give the same result on the same bush is not addressed.

### AF-17 — Height is commonly reduced by about one-third to one-half

**Question:** Q6  
**Assessed Finding:** Sources commonly express the reduction of the retained canes as a fraction of their length or height, most often about one-third to one-half.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 10 of the 22 sources (9 organisations; UK 0, NZ 3, society or garden 2, US extension 5): UCG, UCF, UGA, DUN, RBG, YAT, UAEX, SPR, ILL, KCRS. Figures: 1/3 to 1/2 (UCG, UCF, RBG, UAEX for shrub roses); “at least a third” (UGA); “half to one third” (DUN); about one-third (YAT), with hybrid tea and floribunda laterals shortened by half; “about 1/2 of the height off the bush” (SPR, the author's own practice); “one-half to two-thirds” for modern ever-blooming roses (ILL); “by half” for most landscape roses (KCRS).

**Strengths.** Ten sources across three groups agree on the order of magnitude.

**Limitations.** The range runs from one-third to two-thirds, and several figures concern landscape or shrub roses. Sources also give absolute final heights that differ widely (§6, C1), and the two ways of expressing the limit are not reconciled.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Which fraction, if any, is appropriate for a given rose or climate is not established.

### AF-18 — Hybrid teas are pruned lower than floribundas

**Question:** Q6  
**Assessed Finding:** Hybrid teas (and grandifloras) are pruned lower, or to fewer canes, than floribundas.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 3 of the 22 sources (3 organisations; UK 1, NZ 0, society or garden 0, US extension 2): RHS, OSU, CCER. RHS: hybrid teas to four to six buds and 10 to 15 cm; floribundas to within 25 to 30 cm of the soil. OSU: hybrid tea-grandiflora 12 to 18 inches; floribunda 24 to 36 inches. CCER: floribundas “Leave substantially more canes”.

**Strengths.** Three reputable sources (UK and US) agree in direction and, where quantified, in magnitude.

**Limitations.** Only three sources. Other sources give a single figure for hybrid teas, floribundas and grandifloras together (for example CLE) or none. No source distinguishes grandifloras from hybrid teas except by grouping them.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether grandifloras should be grouped with hybrid teas is unresolved by the sources.

### AF-19 — Once-flowering, old garden and rugosa roses are pruned differently

**Question:** Q6  
**Assessed Finding:** Once-flowering roses (old garden roses, ramblers and rugosas) are pruned lightly and, where they flower on old wood, after flowering rather than in dormancy.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: High.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 9 of the 22 sources (9 organisations; UK 0, NZ 2, society or garden 2, US extension 5): ILL, CLE, UGA, RBG, YAT, SPR, CCER, KCRS, UCG. For example: old garden roses “no more than one-third of each bush” (CLE); old garden roses “don't cut them back by more than 1/4 at a time” and ramblers “pruned right back to the ground straight after flowering” (YAT); old garden roses observed “for a couple of years before pruning them” and rugosas objecting to heavy pruning (RBG).

**Strengths.** Nine sources across three groups agree that these roses are not pruned as modern repeat-flowering bush roses.

**Limitations.** This is a boundary of the supported scope, not a pruning rule for hybrid teas or floribundas. The RHS publishes separate guides on rambling roses (a search result; not fetched).

**Remaining uncertainty.** None material to the claim itself.

### AF-20 — Multi-year renewal removes about one-third of the old canes each year

### AF-21 — The cut is made above an outward-facing bud

**Assessed Finding:** For most cuts back to a bud, sources advise choosing a bud that faces outward, away from the centre of the bush.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: High.**

### AF-22 — The cut is made about a quarter of an inch above the bud

**Question:** Q9  
**Assessed Finding:** Sources that give a distance most often say to cut about one-quarter inch (about 6 mm) above the bud.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 11 of the 22 sources (9 organisations; UK 0, NZ 0, society or garden 2, US extension 9): OSU, ILL, CLE, ISU, ISUF, CSU, UCG, UCF, UGA, RBG, ARS. OSU gives a reason: leaving a quarter inch of cane above the bud “ensures the wound can heal properly”. KCRS says “1/4 to 1/2 inch”; TAMK and TAMN say “half an inch”. **Revision 4 (R-8, further pass):** Texas A&M's original Earth-Kind Landscaping page gives the reasoning behind the half-inch figure directly, read at source: “If the cut is too close to the bud, the bud usually dies. If the cut is too far from the bud, the wood above the bud usually dies, causing dead tips.” This shows the quarter-inch and half-inch figures are different, defensible choices along the same tradeoff (bud death versus a dead snag), not an unexplained disagreement — the majority convention (about a quarter inch) is retained as the primary guidance in §9's practical summary.

**Strengths.** Eleven sources give the same figure; the figure is repeated across institutions; the underlying tradeoff for the minority figure is now understood, not merely recorded.

**Limitations.** All eleven are from the United States and a quarter inch is a widely repeated rule of thumb; independence cannot be established (EAS §4.5). Three US sources give a different distance (§6, C2), now understood as a different resolution of the same tradeoff rather than an arbitrary difference. The RHS, DUN, YAT and SPR give no figure.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether a distance of a quarter or a half inch changes outcomes is not addressed.

### AF-23 — The cut is slanted at about 45 degrees

**Limitations.** The support is from NZ and US sources only, with no UK or society source. Sources outside the seven either give no angle or, in UMD's case, give a reason without a figure.

**Remaining uncertainty.** None material to the claim itself.

### AF-24 — Which way the slant falls in relation to the bud

**Question:** Q9  
**Assessed Finding:** Sources that specify the direction of the slant most often say it slants away from the bud.  
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 5 of the 22 sources (5 organisations; UK 0, NZ 1, society or garden 1, US extension 3): OSU, ILL, UCG, DUN, ARS. OSU: “a clean 45-degree cut on a downward slant, away from the bud”. ILL: “The cut should slant away from the bud”. UCG: “at a 45 degree angle down and away”. DUN: “sloping away from the bud”. ARS explains that sap “can pour down the opposing side of the cane and not interfere with the developing eye”. Three other sources use different wording (§6, C3).

**Strengths.** Five sources use consistent wording and ARS gives a reason.

**Limitations.** Three sources (ISU, ISUF, CLE) describe the slant as “in the same direction as the bud” or “at the same angle as the bud”, which the extraction cannot show to be the same geometry. **Revision 4 (R-8, further pass):** the ISU and Clemson pages were read again in full, directly at source, specifically looking for a diagram or fuller context that would settle whether this is the same geometry worded differently or a genuinely different cut. Neither page carries a diagram or any further explanation of the phrase. The wording conflict remains genuinely unresolved even after this direct check, and Low continues to reflect that; §9 adopts the majority “away from the bud” wording as the practical default.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether the wordings describe one geometry or two is unresolved.

### AF-31 — Thinning cuts and heading cuts are functionally different, not interchangeable

**Question:** Q3 / Q9 (order of work; cut mechanics)
**Assessed Finding:** A cut that removes a stem entirely, back to its point of origin ("thinning"), and a cut that shortens a stem partway along its length, back to a bud ("heading"), are treated in the pruning literature as different in purpose and in the plant's response, not as one kind of cut applied in two places.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 3 sources, only one of which is rose-specific in detail: Richard W. Harris, "Clarifying Certain Pruning Terminology: Thinning, Heading, Pollarding," *Journal of Arboriculture* (now *Arboriculture & Urban Forestry*), Vol. 20, No. 1 (January 1994), pp. 50–54 — a peer-reviewed general-arboriculture source, not specific to roses, defining thinning as producing "evenly distributed growth" that maintains natural form, and heading as stimulating "vigorous, dense, upright growth from buds below the cut" that "typically distorts the... natural shape." UC Marin Master Gardeners, "Pruning Cuts" (ucanr.edu/site/uc-marin-master-gardeners/pruning-cuts, last updated 12 May 2026) — general pruning guidance, not rose-specific, independently gives matching definitions and adds a technique difference: a heading cut is placed relative to the bud (about a quarter inch above it), while a thinning cut is placed relative to the branch collar, and cautions that heading cuts "seal more poorly." UCG (already in the source base; used here for its rose-specific application) applies the same thinning/heading distinction directly to bush rose pruning, matching the general-arboriculture definitions exactly.

**Strengths.** The distinction rests on a peer-reviewed source plus independent corroboration, and the rose-specific application (UCG) matches the general definition exactly rather than contradicting it.

**Limitations.** Only one of the three sources (UCG) is rose-specific; the other two are general woody-plant pruning literature applied here by extension, not tested on roses specifically. Neither of the two general sources discusses whether the two cut types also need different tools, which was part of the original question (R-3) and remains unaddressed.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether the poorer sealing UC Marin attributes to heading cuts has any practical consequence for roses specifically (e.g., for cane-borer entry, see AF-35) was not addressed by any source found.

### AF-32 — On a congested or overgrown bush, keeper canes are identified in a set sequence, not all at once

**Question:** Q5 (structure and framework)
**Assessed Finding:** For a mature or congested bush where the target open-centred structure is hard to see, one institutional source describes identifying keeper canes in a specific sequence rather than in a single pass: remove dead/damaged/diseased wood first; then remove old, thick, unproductive canes (reasoned: an old cane costs the plant energy to support without a proportionate return in bloom); then remove canes crossing through the center; only then assess the remaining shape for an open center with well-spaced canes, and shorten the survivors.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 1 source directly verified: Chicago Botanic Garden, "Pruning Mature Roses?", *Smart Gardener* (chicagobotanic.org/plant-information/smart-gardener/pruning-mature-roses), by garden writer Karen Zaworski with input from Tom Soulsby, Senior Horticulturist, Rose Garden (2024) — a public botanic garden source. A second, secondary citation was found (RHS guidance on renovating old shrub roses, reported via a third-party garden blog quoting "RHS Masterclass" material and the RHS Pruning Book) describing a different but compatible principle — find the most vigorous young stems and treat the rest as removable — but this could not be independently verified against the original RHS text, and it concerns old/shrub roses specifically rather than the hybrid tea/floribunda/grandiflora scope of this dossier, so it is recorded here as a caveat, not counted as a second supporting source.

**Strengths.** The one directly-verified source is institutional (a public botanic garden with a named horticulturist), gives a specific reasoned sequence rather than a vague instruction, and is consistent with (does not contradict) the general order-of-work already established at AF-12/AF-13.

**Limitations.** Single directly-verified source. The secondary RHS citation, if accurate, uses a different selection principle (vigor-based) that was not reconciled with the sequential-removal principle here — whether the two produce the same result on the same bush is untested, and not assessed as part of this finding.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether this sequence differs meaningfully from ordinary annual pruning's order of work (AF-12/AF-13), or is simply the same order applied more carefully, was not directly addressed by the source.

### AF-33 — Where two canes cross, one source gives an explicit rule for which to remove

**Question:** Q4 (interactions)
**Assessed Finding:** When two canes cross or crowd each other, one source states an explicit tie-breaking rule: remove the smaller of the pair, keeping the larger cane.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 1 direct source: Portland Rose Society, "Guide to Pruning Roses," by Rich Baer (reprinted with permission at Gaga's Garden, April 2019) — states to remove "the smaller one of each crowded pair." A second source is loosely consistent in spirit but uses a different criterion: Robert B. Martin Jr. (American Rose Society Consulting Rosarian), "Ten Principles of Rose Pruning," favors keeping the newest, greenest canes and removing older canes described as "craggy and gray," which is an age/color-based criterion rather than a size-based one, and is not stated as a rule specifically for crossing pairs. Two further sources (Kansas City Rose Society; Orange County Rose Society, citing Martin/Monge) explicitly note that the underlying guidance they draw on does not specify which of two crossing canes to remove.

**Strengths.** The one direct source gives a specific, actionable, named rule from a rose-society source.

**Limitations.** Single direct source for the specific rule as stated; the "loosely consistent" second source uses a different criterion (age/color, not size), so this is not treated as independent confirmation of the same rule. Two other sources explicitly confirm the absence of guidance on this point elsewhere in the literature, which supports treating this as a genuine minority/isolated rule rather than a hidden consensus.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether size and age/color would generally agree on which cane to remove (an older cane is often, but not always, the thicker one) was not addressed by any source.

### AF-34 — General plant-diagnostic guidance favors physical examination over photographs alone

**Question:** Q8 (what can be judged from photographs)
**Assessed Finding:** General plant-diagnostics literature (not specific to roses) consistently holds that a photograph alone is usually insufficient for a reliable diagnosis, and that physical examination of the plant, or a physical sample, is preferred where available.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 4 independent general extension/diagnostics sources, none rose-specific: Mississippi State University Extension, "Taking Photos of Plant Disease Problems," Publication 3022, Dr. Rebecca A. Melanson ("in most cases, a diagnosis cannot be made using only photos"); Iowa State University Extension and Outreach, Plant & Insect Diagnostic Clinic, "Photography" page (updated 2026) ("it is not feasible in most cases to make even a good guess" from an image alone); Cornell Cooperative Extension, "How to Take a Photo for Crop Diagnostics," Elizabeth Buck (May 2020), noting image quality commonly limits remote diagnosis; Colorado State University Extension, "Diagnosing Plant Disorders" (David Whiting, Carol O'Meara, Mary Small, 2017) ("It is really best to see a sample").

**Strengths.** Four independent institutional extension sources agree on the general principle, with no source found taking the opposite position.

**Limitations.** None of the four sources concerns roses, or pruning judgements specifically; this finding is an extrapolation from general plant-diagnostic practice (mostly aimed at disease/pest identification) to the different question of assessing a dormant bush's structure for pruning. It is capped at Low confidence for that reason, regardless of the four-source agreement, per this project's practice of not exceeding what the evidence directly supports (EAS §4).

**Remaining uncertainty.** No source breaks this general principle down into AskPIP's specific categories — which whole-plant judgements (shape, apparent density, visible crossing) are realistically assessable from an ordinary phone photograph of a dormant rose bush, versus which require physical examination (bud swell, pith color, base firmness). That breakdown remains the ROC's own reasoning, not sourced, and is not adopted here (EAS §2.10); it is recorded as an open question in §9.

### AF-35 — Sealing larger rose pruning cuts targets cane-boring insects, not wound healing

**Question:** Q9 (cut mechanics — sealing cut ends; resolves the apparent contradiction behind C4)
**Assessed Finding:** Where rose-specific sources recommend sealing larger cut ends (with glue, nail polish, or pruning sealer), the reason given is protection against cane-boring insects entering the cane's exposed, soft pith — a different claim from, and not contradicted by, general arboriculture and plant-pathology research finding that wound dressings do not measurably improve wound closure or reduce decay in woody plants generally.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

**Evidence.** The insect-protection rationale is supported by 4 independent sources: University of Arizona Cooperative Extension, Yavapai County, Jeff Schalau (19 June 2022) — states rose canes have "very soft, relatively large diameter pith... very attractive to carpenter bees, solitary wasps, and sawflies," recommending wood glue on cut ends for this reason specifically; PlantTalk Colorado (Colorado State University Extension), "Rose Cane Borer" — recommends sealing with water-insoluble glue or nail polish "to prevent more nests of eggs," and notes the insects involved are often beneficial wasps nesting in the pith rather than true pests; the American Rose Society's own "Basic Pruning Principles" (already in the source base as ARS) gives the same reason ("provide an instant protective barrier against cane borers"); a Texas AgriLife El Paso Master Gardener handout gives the same reason. The general "sealing doesn't help healing" position, which this finding does not contradict, is separately supported by a peer-reviewed literature review (Arboriculture & Urban Forestry, Vol. 36, Issue 3, citing Shigo & Larson 1969 and subsequent studies: "dressings provide no measurable benefit to wound compartmentalization") and is consistent with what the dossier's existing TAMK source already says about wound dressings generally.

**Strengths.** Four independent institutional/society sources converge on the same specific mechanism (soft pith attracts boring insects), giving a clear, consistent reason where the original dossier (AF-30) had only competing instructions with no reasoning behind either side. This also resolves what looked like a straightforward contradiction (C4): the sources against sealing and the sources for it are not actually answering the same question.

**Limitations.** No controlled trial or entomological research was found testing sealed versus unsealed rose canes for cane-borer incidence; the convergence is expert/institutional consensus, not tested research. The general "wound dressings don't help healing" literature is not rose-specific and concerns healing/decay resistance, not insect entry, so it genuinely does not test the claim made here — this is why the two bodies of evidence are treated as compatible rather than conflicting, not because either one was weighed against the other directly.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether unsealed cane ends are actually, empirically more prone to borer damage than sealed ones (as opposed to being theoretically more exposed) was not tested by any source found. AF-30 itself (whether to seal at all) remains Low confidence and contested, per the original dossier — this finding explains *why* the sources that recommend sealing do so, without settling whether sealing is a good idea on balance.

### AF-36 — Old wood is recognized visually by bark color and texture, not a validated age

**Question:** Q2 (categories of growth — old/woody canes)

### AF-37 — Grandifloras are generally pruned like hybrid teas, adjusted for height

**Question:** Q6 (limits and restraint — rose-class differences)
**Assessed Finding:** Where sources address grandiflora roses as their own case rather than folding them silently into another class, they treat grandiflora pruning as closer to hybrid tea practice than to floribunda practice — similar or slightly fewer canes than a hybrid tea, and in some sources a gentler cutback specifically to preserve the class's characteristic height.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 4 sources: the American Rose Society's own Consulting Rosarian Manual (4th ed., October 2019) explicitly groups "Hybrid Teas and Grandifloras" together as one pruning category, distinct from floribundas; LSU AgCenter (Dan Gill, 9 February 2018) pairs hybrid tea and grandiflora pruning timing and treatment together, describing floribunda treatment separately as less severe; University of Arizona Cooperative Extension, Yavapai County (Phyllis Jiacalone, February 2021) gives grandiflora its own distinct figures — 3 to 7 retained canes, cut back one-third to one-half, closer to hybrid tea's 5 to 8 canes than floribunda's "many more"; Heirloom Roses (a specialist nursery) recommends a gentler cutback than a hybrid tea specifically to preserve the tall, sturdy canes that give grandifloras their characteristic height.

**Strengths.** The lead source is the American Rose Society's own official training material for certified consulting rosarians — a strong, authoritative, rose-specific institutional source — corroborated by an independent university extension source and a specialist nursery.

**Limitations.** Most general sources (Oregon State, the dossier's existing NYBG-type sources, Walter Andersen Nursery) simply fold grandiflora in with hybrid tea silently rather than discussing it, so this finding rests on the minority of sources that address it explicitly, not on a broad base. Only one source (Arizona) gives concrete, distinct numbers; the "gentler cutback" nuance rests on a single commercial nursery source.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether the "gentler cutback to preserve height" recommendation (Heirloom Roses) is broadly shared or a minority view was not tested against further sources.

### AF-38 — A second RHS source confirms existing cut-mechanics figures; Southern Hemisphere sources push back on the slant/bud-direction convention

**Question:** Q9 (cut mechanics)
**Assessed Finding, part 1 (additional corroboration, not a new claim).** A second, previously unused RHS page ("Rose Pruning: General Tips," distinct from the RHS source already in the dossier) independently states the same quarter-inch distance, slant-away-from-the-bud direction, and outward-facing-bud guidance already recorded at AF-21 through AF-24. This is recorded as corroboration from a source the dossier had not previously used, not folded into AF-21–24's existing counts (per the scoping decision, §0).

**Assessed Finding, part 2 (a genuine new conflict).** Two Southern Hemisphere sources explicitly and specifically disagree with the "slant the cut, and make sure it faces the right way relative to the bud" convention that AF-23/AF-24 record from Northern Hemisphere and US sources. Rose Society of South Australia (Doug Gregory): "Don't worry about cutting through at an angle. A healthy cane will seal over the cut without any human intervention." Ludwig's Roses (South Africa): the cut should be made "at 90° to the stem" (not sloped), and "the direction of the top eye is of minor importance — the rose will decide which eye to favour."
**Evidence Confidence Level: not assigned to a competing claim** (per EAS, a documented, unresolved conflict; see §6, C11, which is where this is carried, rather than as a finding of its own).

**Strengths.** The dissenting view comes from two independent, credible sources from a different hemisphere and climate than the sources AF-23/AF-24 already rest on (one a regional rose society, one a major specialist nursery), which strengthens it as a genuine regional/practitioner difference rather than a single outlier opinion.

**Limitations.** Neither Southern Hemisphere source gives a reason for its position (unlike ARS and UMD/TAMK's reasoning already recorded for the "away from the bud" position); it simply differs. Whether this reflects a genuine difference in outcome, a difference in what different rosarian traditions consider worth the extra care, or something else, is untested.

**Remaining uncertainty.** See §6, C11.

### AF-39 — A badly overgrown, weak or sickly rose bush is sometimes better replaced than renovated

**Question:** Q7 (deferral and help)
**Assessed Finding:** At least one source states that renovation is not always the right choice for a badly overgrown or declining shrub — a weak or sickly plant may be better replaced outright than pruned back hard.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low.**

**Evidence.** Supported by 1 direct source: RHS, "Renovating Overgrown Shrubs" (RHS Advice Team) — "Weak or sickly plants are best replaced." This is general shrub guidance, not rose-specific. A second, general-shrub source (Iowa State University Extension and Outreach, "Pruning Large, Overgrown Shrubs," Richard Jauron and Aaron Steil) makes the same kind of point for a different plant type (recommending replacement rather than renovation for some overgrown conifers), which is consistent in principle but does not independently confirm the claim for roses or shrubs generally.

**Strengths.** A clear, actionable piece of guidance that fills a real gap: existing sources describe *how* to renovate but not *when not to bother*.

**Limitations.** Single source, general-shrub rather than rose-specific, and gives no criteria for "weak or sickly" beyond the words themselves — no source ties this to anything a gardener (or a photo-based assessment) could reliably judge.

**Remaining uncertainty.** No source of any kind gives a concrete threshold (size, years of neglect, or degree of decline) at which a gardener should stop trying to renovate and start considering replacement — this remains a genuine, searched-and-not-found gap, recorded as still open in §9.

### AF-40 — No more than about a third of a bush's growth should be removed in one session

**Question:** Q6 / Q7 (limits and restraint; staged renovation)
**Assessed Finding:** General arboriculture and shrub-pruning guidance converges on a widely used rule of thumb: no more than about a third of a woody plant's growth (crown, canopy or canes) should be removed in a single pruning session, with a stated reason (plant stress, depleted energy reserves, and a tendency toward uncontrolled regrowth at cut sites). Where staged, multi-year renovation is described, the same figure — roughly a third per year — recurs. This is not rose-specific, but it directly answers both the total-removal-limit question (U-5) and the staging-threshold question (part of R-7), for which no rose-specific source gives any figure at all.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low** (a well-attested general principle, but not tested for roses specifically, and capped accordingly per EAS §4).

**Evidence.** Supported by 5 independent general (non-rose) sources: NC State Extension, "General Pruning Techniques" ("you should not remove more than a third of the total crown of a plant in any one year," with an age/vigour-tiered refinement — young healthy plants can tolerate more, mature or stressed plants less); Colorado State University Extension (Adams County), "Pruning Mature Shade Trees" (a tiered "pruning dose" table, mature trees 5–10%, medium-aged 10–25%, live wood not removed at all from a stressed plant, citing ANSI A300 compliance); City of Seattle, Department of Trees, "Pruning Mature Trees" ("no more than 25% of the crown should be removed at once, and less for mature trees"); UC Marin Master Gardeners, "Pruning Fundamentals" ("at each pruning, remove no more than one-third of a shrub or tree," citing risk of shock or overly vigorous regrowth); and a consumer gardening source (Homes & Gardens) repeating the same one-third figure with the same stress/regrowth reasoning. For staged renovation specifically, NC State Extension states that rejuvenating an overgrown established shrub by removing about a third of the crown per year "may take about three years," consistent with the rose-specific renewal figure already on record (AF-20, Low, "about one-third of old canes removed per year").

**Strengths.** Five independent institutional sources converge on the same figure and the same reasoning, from a different professional literature (general arboriculture) than the rest of this dossier, which is itself a form of corroboration. It gives a genuine, actionable answer where the rose-specific literature is silent.

**Limitations.** None of the five sources is rose-specific, and none was tested against actual rose renovation outcomes. One rose-specific source found earlier (an Ask Extension answer) explicitly recommends the opposite for an overgrown rose — cutting it back hard to 6–8 canes and 12–20 inches in a single session ("Be brave") — reflecting that roses, unlike many woody ornamentals, are commonly understood to tolerate hard, one-time renovation pruning well. This is not a contradiction of AF-40 so much as a sign that the general rule is a conservative default, not a universal rose-specific requirement.

**Remaining uncertainty.** No source of either kind gives a threshold tied to the bush's own condition (size, years of neglect, degree of decline) for choosing between a single hard cutback and a staged approach; that remains open (§9).

### AF-41 — Overall shape and crossing canes are visually assessable; cane vigour and true internal health require handling the plant

**Question:** Q8 (what can be judged from photographs)
**Assessed Finding:** Piecing together several partial, mostly rose-specific sources gives a workable, if not exhaustive, breakdown of what a whole-plant judgement can rely on a photograph for, versus what requires physically handling the bush. Visually assessable from a clear photograph: the bush's overall outline and shape (stepping back to view the whole plant is explicit rose-specific guidance); crossing canes and canes running through the centre; and, to a degree, relative cane vigour by colour (newer canes greener, older canes greyer or "craggy"). Requiring physical handling: a bend/flex test to judge whether a cane is vigorous enough to support blooms; pith colour, which is only visible once a cut is made; and the base/bud-union area, where crowding and cane count are best judged close up rather than from a distance. One source-documented caution applies throughout: a cane can look sound from the outside and still be compromised inside, so an outward, photographable appearance is not a reliable substitute for a trial cut where health is in doubt.
**Evidence Confidence Level: Low** (a ROC-assembled synthesis of several partial, independently sourced observations, not one source stating the full breakdown; see Limitations).

**Evidence.** David Austin Roses' shrub-rose pruning guide (rose-specific, commercial): "Step back often, look at the outline, and aim for a strong, open framework" and "Step back and look at the shrub in its setting" (supports: overall shape is a distance/visual judgement); the same source: "if the tip of a stem bends easily, it is unlikely to support flowers well and can be removed" (supports: vigour by flex is a tactile, not visual, test). Orange County Rose Society's pruning guide (rose-specific, society): identifies newer/greener versus older/"craggy and gray" canes and canes crossing the centre as things to look for, but separately instructs "get down on your knees... and look at the bud union and the canes that come from it" (supports: cane-colour vigour and crossing are visible; the base and true cane count need close viewing). American Rose Society, "Basic Pruning Principles" (rose-specific, society, already in the source base as ARS): pith is "revealed after the cut is made" (supports: true internal health requires cutting, not viewing). A rose-grower forum discussion (Houzz, informal but directly on point): describes a cane that looked green and sound outside but proved compromised on a trial cut (supports: outward appearance can mislead). NC State Extension's general pruning-technique guide (not rose-specific): recommends close inspection to remove dead/weak/crossing wood first, "then step back and look over the plant from all sides" for overall form (a general-woody-plant source independently supporting the same close-versus-distant split).

**Strengths.** Every individual claim is independently sourced and, where rose-specific, directly on point; the general-woody-plant source (NC State) independently supports the same structural split (close work for identifying problem wood, distance for overall form) without being asked to.

**Limitations.** No single source states this breakdown as a whole; it is assembled by the ROC from several partial statements, each individually evidenced but not stitched together by any source itself (EAS §2.10 governs the synthesis, not the individual claims, which are cited above). No rose-ID app, garden-consulting service, or "send a photo" advice column was found stating explicitly what it can or cannot assess from a submitted photo, despite a direct search for this. The breakdown has not been tested against Pip's actual photo-assessment capability.

**Remaining uncertainty.** Whether this breakdown is complete — i.e., whether there are further whole-plant judgements that are reliably photo-assessable, or further ones that are not — was not addressed by any source and is not claimed here.

## 9. Recommendations for Further Research

The ROC identifies the following as warranted, whether or not the Founders act on them. R-1 to R-3 are the ROC's own reasoned synthesis. Under EAS §2.10 they are not evidence, carry no confidence, and are not adopted as findings. They are leads to be tested against independent evidence.

**R-1 — Test whether crossing, rubbing and inward-growing stems commonly occur together (ROC synthesis). CLOSED in Revision 4: a permanent limitation of the evidence, not an item still pending.** The Founder expects that inward growth will often, but not always, cross another stem. No source states or tests this, in two full research rounds across rosarian, institutional and general arboriculture literature. This question asks for an occurrence rate across many pruned bushes — the kind of thing that needs direct observation over time, not a citable horticultural rule — so it is not expected that any published source will ever answer it. It is recorded here as a standing limitation of what research can establish, and is not carried forward as an open item awaiting further search. If AskPIP itself accumulates enough real pruning data over time, that data — not further literature search — is the only plausible route to an answer.

**R-2 — Test whether "select the canes to keep" and "remove the unwanted categories" give the same result (ROC synthesis). RESOLVED PRACTICALLY in Revision 4.** AF-16 records that sources use both framings, and none says when to prefer one. Revision 4 read the existing evidence again as a whole rather than searching for a new source to compare them, and found the practical answer was already present: AF-12/AF-13 (order of work) already establish that unwanted growth is removed *first*, and AF-16 already shows that sources describe choosing the framework canes as a step that follows. The two framings are not competing methods that could diverge — they are two stages of one process, applied in sequence. This is recorded as the ROC's own synthesis of already-approved evidence (EAS §2.10), not a new Assessed Finding, but it closes the practical question: remove the unwanted categories first, then assess what remains for the framework.

**R-3 — Test whether removal cuts and shortening cuts need separate cut guidance (ROC synthesis). RESOLVED in Revision 3.** Only UCG and TAMK use the terms thinning and heading cuts. Most cut-mechanics guidance (AF-21 to AF-24) is written for cuts back to a bud, and guidance for cutting a stem off at its origin is thinner (AF-29). *Resolved: yes, thinning and heading cuts are treated as functionally different, not interchangeable — see* **AF-31** *(Moderate confidence, a peer-reviewed general-arboriculture source plus independent corroboration).*

**R-4 — Interactions and priorities (Q4). Substantially resolved in Revision 4.** Research on how expert sources resolve a stem that fits more than one category, and how they choose between two crossing stems (U-1, U-2, U-3). *The "which of two crossing canes" sub-question is resolved — see* **AF-33** *(Low confidence). The "a stem is both weak and structurally useful — which category wins" sub-question was searched again in Revision 4, this time including the formal ANSI A300 pruning standard read directly at source — and confirmed to have no answer in any literature: ANSI A300 itself lists pruning objectives without ranking them and leaves the call to practitioner judgement. Because this is a real, recurring situation that no source will ever settle, Revision 4 adopts a stated practical default rather than leaving it open: when a stem is both a removal candidate (weak, thin) and appears to be one of the few canes carrying the plant's framework, the default is to lean conservative and retain it, consistent with the majority "start conservatively" convention already on record (§6, C5) and with AF-9's preference for strong, framework-carrying canes — reassess it at the next pruning once the true framework is clearer. This is the ROC's own recommendation, not a sourced finding (EAS §2.10), and the Founders are free to direct a different default at any time; it is not offered as a blocking question.*

**R-5 — Identifying the framework (Q5). RESOLVED in Revision 3.** Research on how the canes to keep are identified in congested growth (U-4). *Resolved, on a single strong institutional source plus a scope-qualified secondary citation — see* **AF-32** *(Low confidence).*

**R-6 — Judging from photographs (Q8). Substantially resolved in Revision 4.** Research on which whole-plant judgements can reliably be made from an overview photograph (U-8). This bears directly on what Pip may propose from an image. *A citable, if partial, breakdown is now available — see* **AF-41** *(Low confidence, a ROC synthesis of several independently sourced observations): overall shape and crossing canes are visually assessable; cane vigour by a bend test and true internal health (pith colour) require physically handling the plant. Whether this breakdown is exhaustive was not tested against any source and is not claimed.*

**R-7 — Staged renovation and deferral (Q7). Substantially resolved in Revision 4.** Research on renewing an overgrown hybrid tea or floribunda over several years, and on when to pause or spread the work (U-6, U-7, AF-20). *The existing one-third-per-year renewal figure (AF-20) is now backed by a well-reasoned, widely used general principle — see* **AF-40** *(Low confidence, extrapolated from general arboriculture): remove no more than about a third of the bush's growth in one session; stage heavier renovation over roughly 2–3 years at the same rate. Combined with* **AF-39** *(a weak or sickly bush is sometimes better replaced than renovated), this gives working, actionable guidance where none existed before. No source of any kind gives a precise numeric threshold (bush size, years of neglect) for choosing staged versus single-session renovation, and none is expected to; AF-40's one-third figure is adopted as the practical default for that choice rather than left open.*

**R-8 — Rose-specific and scientific evidence on cut mechanics (Q9). Substantially resolved.** Research on cut distance, slant direction, and sealing, to settle C2, C3 and C4 (U-10). *Sealing is resolved (AF-35, C4). Distance above the bud (C2) and slant-direction wording (C3) were checked again directly at source in Revision 4: Texas A&M's own page explains the tradeoff behind the two distance figures (too close risks bud death; too far risks a dead snag), so the dispute is now understood even though the two conventions themselves are not merged — the quarter-inch majority is adopted as the practical default (§8, AF-22). The ISU/Clemson "same direction as the bud" wording was checked again against the full original pages and remains genuinely ambiguous with no diagram to resolve it; the majority "away from the bud" wording is adopted as the practical default (§8, AF-24). Both defaults are stated plainly for use; neither dispute is presented as blocking approval.*

**R-9 — Age of wood (Q2). Substantially resolved.** Research on how old wood is recognised (U-9, C6). *Resolved on visual markers — see* **AF-36** *(Moderate confidence): gray, scaly, streaked or crusty bark, sometimes with deteriorating prickles. Age-in-years is not resolved and a further, more divergent figure was found in Revision 3, widening rather than narrowing the disagreement (§6, C6); the RHS's three-year figure, being the earliest and most institutionally direct, is adopted as the practical default where a number is needed, with the visual markers as the primary, more reliable guidance.*

**R-10 — Widen the source base. Substantially completed in Revision 3.** Six new sources added (§4.1). Two remain genuinely unreachable after real attempts by three different methods: PlantAmnesty (link dead; current content video-only) and The Rose Society UK (membership-gated, not attempted — see §4.2).

**R-11 — Verify before writing an ARC. RESOLVED in Revision 2.** Full detail at §0 and in the companion `FRD-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01_R11_Verification_Notes.md`.

**R-12 — Rose classes. RESOLVED in Revision 3.** *Grandifloras are generally pruned like hybrid teas, adjusted for height — see* **AF-37** *(Moderate confidence).*

**Practical defaults adopted in Revision 4, for use rather than further decision.** Where sources genuinely and permanently disagree — ordinary variation among practitioners, not a research gap a further search will close — this revision states the majority or best-reasoned practice as the working default, with the variation noted rather than left as an open conflict:

- **Final height (C1):** no single figure is right; use the class-based mid-range already on record (AF-17, AF-18) — roughly one-third to one-half of the previous season's growth, adjusted lower for hybrid teas and higher for floribundas — rather than a fixed number.
- **Cut distance above the bud (C2):** about a quarter of an inch (AF-22), the majority convention; a gardener who wants to minimise the risk of a dead snag rather than bud death can use up to half an inch instead (AF-22's updated evidence explains the tradeoff).
- **Slant direction (C3):** slant away from the bud, roughly 45 degrees (AF-23, AF-24), the majority wording; the minority "same direction as the bud" wording is treated as the same technique described differently, not a different instruction, since no source shows otherwise.
- **Sealing cut ends (C4):** seal larger cuts (roughly pencil-thickness or more) with glue, nail polish or pruning sealer as a precaution against cane-boring insects (AF-35); thinner cuts need not be sealed.
- **Age of wood (C6):** use the visual markers (AF-36) as the primary test; where a number is wanted, treat canes older than about three years as candidates for removal (RHS's figure), recognising this is a convention, not a measured fact.
- **Number of canes retained (C7):** four to six strong canes is a reasonable working default within the three-to-eight range the sources give (AF-15).
- **Tool-thickness threshold (C8):** hand pruners up to about three-quarters of an inch; loppers or a saw above that (the OSU/ISU majority figure; AF-26).
- **Pruner type (C9):** bypass (scissor-type) pruners are the default; anvil pruners are acceptable for clearing dead wood only (the majority preference; AF-25).
- **Disinfecting tools (C10):** wipe blades with 70 percent alcohol between plants as a simple, well-supported default (AF-28); this is a precaution, not a strict requirement given the mixed evidence on how often it matters.
- **Cut angle and bud direction (C11):** follow the majority convention (45-degree slant, outward-facing bud) as the default; the Southern Hemisphere sources that treat this as unimportant are noted for completeness but do not change the default guidance.
- **Category priority when a stem is both weak and structurally useful:** lean conservative and retain the stem for reassessment at the next pruning, per R-4 above.

**Leads for individual observation commissions.** The findings support each remaining observation commission as a place to go deeper: damaged growth (AF-2), crossing or rubbing stems (AF-3, AF-11, AF-33), inward-growing stems (AF-4), weak or congested growth (AF-5, AF-6) and the main framework to retain (AF-9, AF-14 to AF-16, AF-32). The draft commission on crossing and inward-growing stems remains on hold.
