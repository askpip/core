# Pip's Answers — Which Months to Do Each Rose Job in Australia

> **Approved and published, 8 October 2026.** A Founder approved these answers in chat ("I approve this."). They are published in PKR-CGD-000022 to PKR-CGD-000027 at Version 1.1, and the research is filed as `ARC-BUSHROSE-TIMING-AU-01`. This document is kept as the record of what was approved.

This is everything Pip would say about which months to prune, feed, deadhead, plant, move and spray a rose in Australia. **You are asked for one thing: approval of these answers.** Approving them also approves the research behind them and lets them be published. Nothing else needs reading.

## 1. What gardeners will see

The six questions are already in the app, published for New Zealand on 7 October. Today a gardener with a rose in Australia who opens them is told Pip has nothing for that place yet. On approval they see the statements below.

Every statement is shown only for a rose in Australia. Where a statement is for one state or city, that is marked, and only a rose there sees it. Each statement is shown with the label in brackets. A list of the sources sits under each answer for a gardener who wants it.

### Which months do I prune my rose?

- In most areas, the main winter pruning is done in July. In some places it can start in June. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[High confidence]*
- In a cold or frosty area, wait until August to prune, so that frost does not damage the new shoots. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[High confidence]*
- In New South Wales, one rose society says to prune in July near the coast, where frosts are rare or never come, and that you can start in late June. Inland, or where frosts are regular, it says to prune in August. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in New South Wales only.**
- In Victoria, one rose society says the best time to prune is June or July. In a cold area, it says to wait until August, so that frost does not knock back the new shoots. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Victoria only.**
- In South Australia, prune in July. One nursery says pruning can start in late June. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners in South Australia only.**
- For a frost area in South Australia, one rose society says to wait until August to prune, and one nursery says July. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[Sources disagree]* **Gardeners in South Australia only.**
- In Western Australia, one rose society says to prune in winter, in June and July. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Western Australia only.**
- In Brisbane, one public rose garden says the best time to prune is June or July. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near Brisbane only.**
- I couldn't find months for rose jobs written for Tasmania. A local rose society is the place to ask. *[No source found]* **Gardeners in Tasmania only.**
- I couldn't find months for rose jobs written for the Northern Territory. A local garden society or botanic garden is the place to ask. *[No source found]* **Gardeners in the Northern Territory only.**
- I couldn't find months for rose jobs written for Queensland, apart from pruning in Brisbane. A local rose society is the place to ask. *[No source found]* **Gardeners in Queensland only.**
- I couldn't find months for rose jobs written for the dry inland of Australia, such as Mildura. A local rose society is the place to ask. *[No source found]* **Gardeners in New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, South Australia or Western Australia only.**

### When do I start and stop feeding?

- Start feeding after the winter prune, as new growth appears. One nursery waits until the rose has plenty of leaves and its first blooms. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Give the spring feed in the second half of August or in September. Months are only a guide. *[High confidence]*
- Give the last feed in late summer or early autumn, in February or March. One nursery says not to feed in late autumn. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]*
- In New South Wales, one rose society says to give the first feed after the winter prune, ideally in the second half of August, or in September in a cold area. It says to give the last feed in mid to late February. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in New South Wales only.**
- In Victoria, one rose society says to feed roses at the end of August. For a newly planted bare-root rose (a dormant plant sold in winter, with no leaves or flowers), it says to wait until late November or early December, and then to feed at half the recommended amount. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Victoria only.**
- In South Australia, feed in September and again in February. One rose society says early in September, and in March if you missed February. On sandy soil, it says to feed four times: in September, November, February and April. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners in South Australia only.**
- In Western Australia, one rose society says to feed as new growth appears in early spring, in August or September, and to keep feeding into May. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Western Australia only.**

### When do I stop deadheading?

- I couldn't find a month to stop deadheading (cutting off the finished blooms) in Australia. A local rose society is the place to ask. *[No source found]*
- To bring on flowers in autumn, give the rose a light trim in summer or early autumn. This is done between January and March, depending on where you live. Months are only a guide. *[High confidence]*
- In New South Wales, one rose society says to trim roses for flowers in autumn: in January in a cool area, early in February in a mild area, and in March in a warm area. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in New South Wales only.**
- In Victoria, one rose society says to give a summer trim in February if you are timing the flowers for a show or special event. It also says to deadhead in April. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Victoria only.**
- In South Australia, a light trim in late summer or early autumn brings on the autumn flowers. One nursery says mid February, and one rose society says one to two weeks after the February feed. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners in South Australia only.**
- In Western Australia, one rose society says to give a light prune, taking off about one-third, in March or April, and to keep deadheading into May. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Western Australia only.**

### Which months do I plant or move a rose?

- In Australia, plant a bare-root rose (a dormant plant sold in winter, with no leaves or flowers) in June or July. One source says planting can start in May. Months are only a guide. *[High confidence]*
- Move an established rose in winter, while it is dormant. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Don't plant a rose in very hot weather. *[Precaution · Moderate confidence]*
- In New South Wales, one rose society says to prepare the soil in late April or May, for planting in June or July. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in New South Wales only.**
- In Sydney, one garden centre says a rose in a pot can be planted at any time of year, though not in very hot weather. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near Sydney only.**
- In Victoria, one rose society says to order new roses in May and start planting them in June. It also says to move an established rose in June. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Victoria only.**
- In South Australia, plant a bare-root rose in June or July. Planting can go on into August. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners in South Australia only.**
- In Western Australia, one rose society says to plant new roses in May, and to plant or repot as needed in June and July. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Western Australia only.**

### When do I clear up and spray in winter?

- After the winter prune, pick off any leaves left on the rose, clear up the prunings and fallen leaves, and get rid of them. One source says to put them in the rubbish bin, not the compost. *[High confidence]*
- If you use a clean-up spray in winter (a spray to clear pests and diseases left on the plant), put it on after pruning. Lime sulphur and a spraying oil are two of the types used. Follow the label on the spray. *[High confidence]*
- If you use lime sulphur as a winter spray, put it on while the rose is dormant, before new growth appears. One source says lime sulphur burns new growth. Follow the label on the spray. *[Precaution · Low confidence]*
- In New South Wales, one rose society says to put the prunings and fallen leaves in the green waste bin as soon as pruning is finished, and then to spray. It says lime sulphur is effective. Follow the label on the spray. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in New South Wales only.**
- In Victoria, one rose society says to take all the leaves off the bushes and rake up around the beds in July, and then to spray the bare roses and, lightly, the ground around them. Follow the label on the spray. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Victoria only.**
- In South Australia, clear up the prunings and fallen leaves after pruning, in July or August, and then spray. Lime sulphur, copper and a spraying oil are the sprays used. Follow the label on the spray. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners in South Australia only.**
- In Western Australia, one rose society says to spray after pruning, in June or July, to clean up pests and disease. Follow the label on the spray. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Western Australia only.**
- I couldn't find Australian advice on covering roses or mounding soil over the base for winter. A local rose society is the place to ask. *[No source found]*

### How do I allow for frost and my own garden?

- In an area that gets frosts, wait until the last frost has passed before you prune. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Large areas of northern Australia are generally free of frost, the national weather bureau says. Days cold enough for frost fall mainly between late autumn and early spring. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Frost is local: how severe it is can vary a lot over a very small area, the national weather bureau says. Frost can form in a valley while the land around it stays free of it. *[Moderate confidence]*
- In a humid climate, roses are more likely to get fungal diseases, and one source says roses are harder to manage in the hot tropics. Another says to choose a rose that is not prone to these diseases if your climate is humid. *[Moderate confidence]*
- In South Australia, one rose society says frosts are common in country areas, and that it is colder and wetter in the Adelaide Hills, the South-East, and Southern Yorke Peninsula and Eyre Peninsula. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in South Australia only.**

## 2. What you should know

- **Each statement is shown only in the place its source speaks of.** 18 are for all of Australia and 27 are for one state or city: New South Wales, Victoria, South Australia, Western Australia, Queensland, Tasmania, the Northern Territory, Sydney or Brisbane. One, the gap for the dry inland, is shown in the five mainland states. A state rose society's months are shown only in its own state. So a gardener sees the Australia-wide statements and then their own state's.
- **Two towns are added to the app,** Sydney and Brisbane, because one source each speaks of those cities. Nothing else in the app changes.
- **Pip says nothing local for Canberra yet.** The two pages from Canberra's horticultural society would not open on any try on 7 October, so nothing from them is confirmed. A Canberra gardener sees the Australia-wide statements. I will try the pages again; adding them would be a small follow-up.
- **Cold and frost are said in words,** as you decided: "in a cold or frosty area, wait until August". No climate zones are used, because no rose source times a job by them.
- **Most state statements rest on one rose society, and say so.** 19 statements carry a Low label. Most of South Australia's are Moderate, because the state society and one nursery agree.
- **One statement is marked "Sources disagree":** when a frost area in South Australia prunes (the society says August, one nursery July). Elsewhere Pip leads with the better-supported view and says who differs.
- **Where Pip couldn't find an answer, it says so and points to a local rose society.** There are six: a month to stop deadheading; covering or mounding roses for winter; and months written for Tasmania, for the Northern Territory, for Queensland apart from pruning in Brisbane, and for the dry inland.
- **Two precautions:** don't plant in very hot weather, and lime sulphur goes on before new growth appears.
- **19 findings are left out.** Eight are about the research and not the plant (climate zones, how sources divide the country, and that none names bud swell). Five come from television segments on one grower's own garden, which the rules do not count as a reliable source on their own. One is Canberra's. The other five would not change what a gardener does, or gave nothing a gardener could act on.
- **The check corrected the research before you saw it.** The quotations were sound, but many findings folded several states' months into one claim for "Australia". 27 findings were reworded or had their level changed, four of them lowered a level. 26 were added, mostly a narrower finding for each state, and 4 disagreements the research had not recorded.
- **Two answers already published overlap with these.** "When can I plant or move a rose?" gives the same Australian planting months in older wording that counts its sources, and shows Moderate where this shows High. The winter spray answer shows Moderate where this shows High for spraying after pruning. Bringing the two older answers into line is a small follow-up, if you agree.
- **Terms.** "Bare-root" is explained only as far as a source explains it ("a dormant plant sold in winter, with no leaves or flowers"). "Dormant" and "established" are not explained, as in the New Zealand answers.
- **Tested in a local copy of the app** for seventeen plants, in every state and territory and on a state border, where the app asks which state. Each saw its own place's statements and no others. Not checked: the signed-in screens and a real phone.
