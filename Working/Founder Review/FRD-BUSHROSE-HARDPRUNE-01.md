## 2. Research Commission Record (Summary)

**Background.** No approved ARC answers whether hard pruning can kill or harm a rose. `ARC-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01` AF-40 (Low) and the published advisory `PKR-CGD-000003` carry a general woody-plant rule of removing no more than about a third of a bush's growth in one session, labelled "not rose-specific". AF-20 (Low) records staged renewal of about a third of old canes a year, and the ARC's §6 default stages heavier renovation over two to three years.

**Commissioned questions.**

- **Q1** How tolerant of hard pruning do reputable sources say an established bush rose is? Can pruning kill it?
- **Q2** What do sources mean by "hard", "moderate" and "light" pruning?
- **Q3** What real risks do sources name (old or weak plants, cutting below the graft, removing all canes, wrong timing, repeated severe pruning, stress, disease entry, grafted versus own-root)?
- **Q4** What do sources say about renovating an old or neglected bush, in one go or over two to three years, and how does this compare with the approved one-third advisory?
- **Q5** What recovery signs do sources describe, and what do they advise if a rose seems to have been pruned too hard?

**Scope.** Established Hybrid Tea, Floribunda and (provisionally) Grandiflora bush roses. Newly planted roses are outside scope. Priority New Zealand, then Australia, United Kingdom, United States. Every online source is recorded with its web address and access date (ROC Operations Manual §5.5).

**Method.**

- Web searches were run for each question, including searches aimed at New Zealand and Australian rose societies and nurseries, the RHS and the Royal National Rose Society, extension services and botanic gardens.
- Pages were read through an automated extraction step that returns text drawn from the page in answer to a stated question; each extraction was asked for exact sentences. For NZRS-FAQ, SARS, RHS-MB, RHS-REN, CBG, CLE, ILL, OSU, RSNSW, GU-P, TRE-P, PSU, BEALES, LO, NYBG, UMD and RNRS the quotations relied on were re-checked against a second extraction asking for the sentence character for character.
- Every quotation used is under 25 words and is recorded, by finding, in the Source Register.
- Pages that could not be opened, or that were opened but are outside the commission's rose classes, are recorded in §4.3 and support no finding.
- Findings are separated by claim so each receives its own Evidence Confidence Level (EAS §3.2).
- Approved findings in `ARC-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01`, `ARC-BUSHROSE-WEAKCONGESTED-01`, `ARC-BUSHROSE-DORMANCY-01` and `ARC-BUSHROSE-RECENTPLANT-01`, and the published `PKR-CGD-000003`, were checked for agreement only (§7, final subsection); they were not re-researched.

**Anticipated challenges.** "Can I kill my rose?" is answered mostly by practitioner assurance rather than measurement. "Hard" has no fixed meaning. Renovation guidance is often written for shrub, climbing or old garden roses rather than Hybrid Teas and Floribundas.

## 5. Evidence Evaluation

The evaluation applies the EAS factors (Chapter 4) to each finding individually in §8. The general position across the body of evidence is as follows.

**Relevance.** All sources in §4.1 address rose pruning directly except RHS-REN (general shrub renovation) and DA-SR (English shrub roses). CLE-D and PNW are rose disease guides relevant to Q3.

**Source reliability.** The RHS, the Royal National Rose Society, rose societies in New Zealand, Australia and the US, two botanic gardens and nine US extension sources are recognised authorities. Nurseries are practised growers, given less weight as independent authority. GORG is a general-interest website without stated expertise or sources.

**Consistency.** Sources agree strongly that pruning does not kill an established rose, that winter pruning commonly removes half to two-thirds of growth or height, and that pruning before frosts have passed risks damage. They disagree on what "hard" means, on whether weak growth is pruned harder or lighter, on renovating in one go or in stages, and on the season for large cuts.

**Independence.** GU-P and GU-P1 count once; the RHS pages count as one organisation; RNRS and BEALES may report the same trial programme.

**Limitations.** All page content was read through an extraction step. The central reassurance (AF-1) is practitioner judgement, not measurement. Several renovation sources address old garden, shrub or climbing roses and were excluded (§4.3), leaving few renovation statements specific to Hybrid Teas and Floribundas.

## 6. Managing Conflicting Evidence and Uncertainty

**C-1 — Can hard pruning kill a rose (AF-1, AF-2, AF-20)?** NZRS-FAQ: "Absolutely not!"; SARS: "You will not kill a rose by pruning it."; also GU-P, TRE-P, BEALES, DA-SR, OCRS. GORG: cutting back "too far could stress it out or even kill it." CBG, PRS and SARS state conditions (otherwise healthy; not below the ground line; roots intact). The weight of sources is on one side; GORG is a single, unsourced commercial page. Preserved as unresolved, with the conditions recorded as AF-2.

**C-2 — Is weak growth pruned harder or lighter (AF-11)?** CLE: "Prune weak-growing varieties lightly"; ILL, RSNSW and VSRG-PM say the same of a weak or slow-growing plant. RHS-MB: "Prune back less vigorous shoots more severely" (Floribunda) and shortens less vigorous Hybrid Tea shoots to "two to four buds 5-10cm"; LO: "Weak stems are cut back further." The first group speaks of the whole plant or variety; RHS-MB and LO speak of individual shoots or stems. No source reconciles the two. Unresolved.

**C-3 — How much to remove in one session, and the approved one-third advisory (AF-5, AF-10; `ARC-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01` AF-40; `PKR-CGD-000003`).** AF-40 (Low) and `PKR-CGD-000003` carry a general woody-plant rule of no more than about a third of a plant's growth in one session. Rose-specific sources commonly remove more at winter pruning: RSNSW "half to two thirds of all growth"; GAM "half to two-thirds of last season's growth"; ILL "one-half to two-thirds of the plant's height"; TRE-P "Remove 2/3 of plant height"; BEALES "take 2 thirds off of the plant"; YAT-AU "up to two-thirds"; RNZIH "at least half". LO calls cutting stems back by a third "light" pruning and does not recommend it; NYBG calls a one-third cut "moderate"; YAT-AU uses a third for a "light prune in summer". The figures are not all measured the same way (share of growth, of height, or of cane length). This conflicts in figure with the approved advisory, and is consistent with AF-40's own recorded limitation that a rose-specific source recommended hard renovation instead. Unresolved; Founder decision point 3.

**C-4 — Renovating an old or neglected bush: in one go or staged (AF-21, AF-22; `ARC-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01` AF-20 and §6 default).** In one go: CBG cuts the remaining canes "to one to two feet from the ground" after removing dead and old canes; RNRS rejuvenated three varieties "by hard-pruning" and they "responded very well"; LO recommends hard pruning "to rejuvenate old or neglected shrubs". Staged: UCF "Don't remove all of those old canes at once, only a third at a time." Both: CBG also offers "ongoing maintenance—a little at a time"; RHS-REN (general shrubs) renovates robust shrubs in one year and stages over "two or three years" where the response is unknown. UCF agrees with AF-20 and the §6 staged default; CBG, RNRS and LO describe the one-go alternative. Unresolved.

**C-5 — When to make large cuts (AF-17, AF-18).** GU-P (New Zealand): "make any large cuts in the autumn when silver leaf spores are not around." ISU-F and NMSU (cold-winter US climates) advise against autumn pruning because new growth will not harden before winter. The reasons and climates differ. Unresolved.

**C-6 — What "hard" means (AF-5, AF-6).** LO and NYBG: canes cut to three to five buds from the base. ILL calls removing one-half to two-thirds of height "very hard". YAT-AU calls cutting up to two-thirds "hard" winter pruning. A single word covers a wide range of severity. Unresolved.

**Uncertainty preserved.** No source measures harm from hard pruning on Hybrid Teas or Floribundas; no rose-specific source gives a condition for choosing staged renovation; no rose-specific source gives recovery signs or a waiting time (§4.3, §9).

## 7. Principal Findings

Each finding is one claim.

### Tolerance (Q1)

**AF-1 (established).** Sources across all four regions state that pruning, even hard or badly done, does not kill an established rose. NZRS-FAQ: "Absolutely not! Roses are very tough and very forgiving so don't be afraid." SARS: "You will not kill a rose by pruning it." GU-P: "It is impossible to kill a rose just by pruning it incorrectly and you probably deserve a medal if you do." TRE-P: "you will not kill the rose by pruning". BEALES: "no matter how bad a job you make of pruning your roses, you are not going to kill them!" DA-SR: "You will not kill a rose by pruning it imperfectly." OCRS: "a rose bush is one tough cookie." Also CBG and PRS with conditions (AF-2). Expert opinion, not measurement. GORG dissents (AF-20, C-1).

**AF-2 (supported interpretation).** Several of those sources attach conditions. CBG: "An otherwise healthy rose won't die because you pruned it". PRS: "no matter how you prune unless you cut the rose below the ground line, it will survive and bloom." SARS: a rose can "dieback to the stump and provided the roots are somewhat in tact, it will shoot out when the conditions are favourable." RHS-REN (general shrubs): "Weak or sickly plants are best replaced."

**AF-3 (expert opinion, limited).** RNRS reports a pruning trial using "conventional, minimal, and by means of a hedge trimmer" methods, with "More flowers produced where minimal and hedge-trimmer techniques were used." BEALES reports a trial at the Gardens of the Rose, St Albans, where "roses rough pruned in this way can still produce an abundance of blooms". Neither page gives years, measures or rose classes beyond three named varieties in RNRS's rejuvenation phase.

**AF-4 (supported interpretation).** Sources describe harm from over-pruning as temporary. DA-SR: a shrub reduced more than intended "is rarely anything more than a short-term setback". GU-P1: "Ok, it may sulk for a little bit but will get over it." CBG: "While the first year will be a recovery year for growth, a 'hard' pruning typically stimulates plenty of flowers in successive years."

### What "hard", "moderate" and "light" mean (Q2)

**AF-5 (supported interpretation).** The terms are defined differently. LO: hard, "Stems are cut back to three or four buds from the base, leaving short, sturdy stems about five inches long"; moderate, "Recommended stems are cut back to half their length"; light, "Stems are cut back by one third." NYBG: hard is cutting "back to 3 to 5 buds per stem from the crown"; heading back "is a moderate pruning where the plant is cut back by ⅓." ILL: Hybrid Teas "are pruned very hard", meaning "removing about one-half to two-thirds of the plant's height." YAT-AU: a summer "light prune" cuts "back a third of plant height". SARS frames it differently: "It's not a matter of how much you cut off, it's a matter of what you leave that counts." (C-6.)

**AF-6 (supported interpretation).** Where "hard" is defined by what remains, it means canes cut back to a few buds near the base: LO (three or four buds, about five inches), NYBG (three to five buds from the crown). RHS-MB, without using the label, shortens the strongest Hybrid Tea shoots "to four to six buds 10-15cm (4-6in) from the base".

**AF-7 (expert opinion, limited).** Hard pruning is described as giving fewer but larger flowers. TAMU-EP: "Prune hard (far back) for fewer but larger blooms; less hard for more but smaller blooms". NMSU: "For exhibition roses, cut canes back to 6 to 10 inches".

**AF-8 (supported interpretation).** Some sources reserve the hardest pruning for particular purposes. LO: hard pruning is "Recommended only for newly planted roses or to rejuvenate old or neglected shrubs." PRS: "Hard pruning is sometimes recommended for newly planted roses and is often used by exhibitors". RHS-GT: "prune all newly planted roses hard to encourage vigorous shoots". (Newly planted roses are outside this commission; see §7 final subsection.)

**AF-9 (supported interpretation).** Light pruning or under-pruning is described as giving tall, spindly, less productive bushes. LO: light pruning is "Not recommended as it produces tall spindly plants with early but inferior blooms." PRS: "Light pruning is not generally recommended because it often results in overly tall, spindly bushes in our climate area." OSU: "Underpruning is the most common cause of an unproductive rose."

### How much is removed at routine winter pruning (Q2, Q4)

**AF-10 (established).** Rose-specific sources commonly remove about half to two-thirds of a Hybrid Tea's or Floribunda's growth or height at winter pruning. RSNSW: "it is best to remove half to two thirds of all growth". GAM: "remove about half to two-thirds of last season's growth". ILL: "one-half to two-thirds of the plant's height." TRE-P: "Remove 2/3 of plant height". BEALES: "take 2 thirds off of the plant". YAT-AU: "Prune up to two-thirds of the plant height". RNZIH: "Established roses can be cut back by at least half." NYBG: "Take off ⅓ to ⅔ the length of the canes". MATT: "at least a third or up to half". This conflicts in figure with the approved one-third advisory (C-3).

### Risks (Q3)

**AF-11 (supported interpretation).** A weak or slow-growing rose is pruned less hard than a vigorous one. CLE: "Prune weak-growing varieties lightly and vigorous varieties more severely." ILL: "If the plant is weak or small, you may not want to cut off as much material." RSNSW: "If the rose bush is not growing as vigorously as the other roses in your garden it is best not to prune as hard." VSRG-PM: "Little growth, light prune; massive growth, heavy prune." RHS-MB and LO say weak shoots are cut back further (C-2).

**AF-12 (expert opinion, single source).** ILL gives the reason: "Each time you remove this much wood you are removing a lot of the food-making ability of the plant."

**AF-13 (expert opinion, single source).** UMD, on Hybrid Teas: "Routine excessive pruning will adversely affect bloom production." No source located says repeated hard pruning kills a rose.

**AF-14 (supported interpretation).** Cutting below the graft or below the ground line is the cut sources identify as able to lose the rose. PRS: the rose survives "unless you cut the rose below the ground line". RHS-REN (general shrubs): "Take care not to cut back below the scion in grafted plants." PSU, on winter damage: "If the graft union is compromised", the part above it dies. SARS adds that regrowth after dieback to the stump depends on the roots being "somewhat in tact".

**AF-15 (expert opinion, single source).** PSU: "If the canes of an own-root rose die back, new ones will often emerge" from the roots. The statement concerns winter dieback, not pruning.

**AF-16 (established).** Pruning too early, before frosts have passed, risks frost damage to new growth and dieback. TUI: "you risk the plant sending out new growth which won't have had time to harden off". OSU: "Earlier pruning (before the last hard frost or forecasted cold weather) may cause die-back that necessitates repruning." PSU: "Don't prune roses too early, as late winter dieback can occur." NMSU: early pruning "tends to induce early cane growth that is subject to spring freezes". RSV: wait in cold areas "so that the frosts don't knock back the new shoots." TRE-P: "hold off until the most severe frosts have passed in frost prone areas". None says this kills the rose.

**AF-17 (expert opinion, limited).** Pruning in late autumn or early winter is linked with winter injury in cold climates. ISU-F: "Pruning stimulates new growth. That new growth will not have an opportunity to properly harden off before winter". NMSU: "Bushes pruned in late fall or early winter may be injured by cold". GU-P (New Zealand) favours autumn for large cuts for a different reason (C-5).

**AF-18 (supported interpretation).** Pruning wounds and stubs are entry points for disease. CLE-D: canker fungi "enter healthy canes through wounds caused by winter injury, improper pruning, wind, hail damage, or flower cutting." PNW: "Common canker is favored by improper pruning, especially leaving long stubs above the last node." GU-P: do not leave stubs "as this will encourage die back", and silver leaf spores "are more prevalent in the air on damp days."

**AF-19 (expert opinion, single source).** NZRS-W: "Note that white and yellow roses often resent hard pruning." No other source addresses flower colour.

**AF-20 (dissenting opinion, single source).** GORG: "cutting a rose (or any plant) back too far could stress it out or even kill it." The page advises not cutting back more than half at once. It gives no source and is contradicted by AF-1 (C-1).

### Renovating an old or neglected bush (Q4)

**AF-21 (supported interpretation).** Old or neglected roses can be rejuvenated by hard pruning. OSU: "neglected roses can easily be rejuvenated by pruning." RNRS: three varieties rejuvenated by hard pruning "responded very well and we conclude that the rejuvenation can be carried out satisfactorily after a period of years." CBG: "A lot of old roses tolerate aggressive pruning well." LO: hard pruning to "rejuvenate old or neglected shrubs". RHS-MB (Floribunda): "Occasionally some older stems can be pruned hard back to a few inches from soil level."

**AF-22 (supported interpretation).** Sources describe both renovation in one go and staged renovation, and none gives a rose-specific rule for choosing. One go: CBG, after removing dead and thick old canes, cuts "the remaining canes to one to two feet from the ground"; RNRS and LO as in AF-21. Staged: UCF "Don't remove all of those old canes at once, only a third at a time." CBG also offers "ongoing maintenance—a little at a time". RHS-REN (general shrubs): "Robust shrubs can be completely renovated in one year, but where the response is unknown, carry out a staged renovation instead", spread "over two or three years" (C-4).

### Recovery and what to do (Q5)

**AF-23 (expert opinion, limited).** Aftercare named after hard renovation. RHS-REN (general shrubs): "Follow up this hard pruning with a feed and mulch in spring", and the regrowth "may need subsequent thinning out to select the strongest, best placed shoots." GORG (commercial) waters compost in and feeds in spring. No rose society or extension source located gives rose-specific aftercare for an over-pruned bush.

**AF-24 (unresolved question).** No rose-specific source located gives signs, or a waiting time, for judging whether a hard-pruned rose is recovering. The closest statements are SARS (a rose cut to the stump "will shoot out when the conditions are favourable") and CBG (the first year is "a recovery year for growth").

**AF-25 (expert opinion, single source).** NZRS-FAQ: "If you need some advice, contact your local rose society or head along to a pruning demonstration."

## 9. Recommendations for Further Research

Each recommendation could not be resolved within the commission's scope and authority. Each states what a Founder decision would need to settle and the ROC's recommended practical default (ROC Operations Manual §7.7).

**R-1. Measured evidence on whether hard pruning harms Hybrid Teas and Floribundas.**
No trial measuring survival or long-term vigour after hard pruning was located; RNRS reports a trial without data (AF-3). Answering it needs access to trial reports or specialist consultation, a different evidentiary standard.
*Founder decision needed:* whether to seek RNRS trial reports or consult the New Zealand Rose Society.
*Recommended default:* Pip states AF-1 as the sources' consistent view, with the AF-2 conditions alongside it.

**R-2. When to renovate in one go and when to stage it, for a bush rose.**
No rose-specific source gives a condition (AF-22). RHS-REN gives one only for shrubs in general.
*Founder decision needed:* whether to commission expert consultation.
*Recommended default:* keep the approved staged default (`ARC-BUSHROSE-PRUNINGFRAMEWORK-01` §6), and record that sources also describe renovation in one go (Founder Decision Point 4).

**R-3. Recovery signs and a waiting time after hard pruning.**
No rose-specific source located (AF-24). Answering it needs specialist advice.
*Founder decision needed:* whether to commission it.
*Recommended default:* Pip gives no time frame or recovery signs, and points to a local rose society if no new growth appears in the growing season (AF-25).

**R-4. How the rose-specific "half to two-thirds" relates to the general "about a third".**
The figures measure different things (AF-10, C-3). Settling whether they conflict in practice depends on Founder judgement about how `PKR-CGD-000003` is worded, not on further searching.
*Founder decision needed:* Founder Decision Point 3.
*Recommended default:* see Founder Decision Point 3.

**R-5. The ROC's own reasoned synthesis, recorded as such and assigned Not Assigned (EAS §2.10).**
These are questions arising from the findings that the evidence does not itself state. They are not findings and are not proposed for adoption.

- R-5a. Whether the RNRS trial (AF-3) and the St Albans trial reported by BEALES are the same trial programme.
- R-5b. Whether "prune a weak plant lightly" (CLE, ILL, RSNSW, VSRG-PM) and "cut weak shoots further" (RHS-MB, LO) describe different things — the whole plant against a single shoot — rather than a contradiction (C-2).
- R-5c. Whether "no more than a third of a bush's growth" (AF-40) and "remove half to two-thirds of the height" (AF-10) measure the same quantity, given that much of the height removed at winter pruning is the previous season's growth (C-3).
- R-5d. Whether a very old grafted bush, with mostly grey wood at the union, regrows less reliably after hard renovation than a younger one, given AF-2 and AF-21.
- R-5e. Whether the newly planted hard-pruning advice (AF-8: RHS-GT, LO, PRS) conflicts with `ARC-BUSHROSE-RECENTPLANT-01` AF-1, or concerns a different kind of cut at planting.

*Founder decision needed:* whether any of these is worth commissioning.
*Recommended default:* none is acted on until independent evidence is found.
