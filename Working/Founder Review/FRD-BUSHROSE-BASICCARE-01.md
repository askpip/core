## 2. Research Commission Record (Summary)

Shaphan (Founder) commissioned this research on 22 September 2026, stating that basic bush rose care should be included in the Ask Pip MVP journey, because a rose that isn't yet ready for pruning still has a gardener who wants to do something for it in the meantime. A live check of the system at the time (querying the `pkr` table directly) confirmed zero published PIP Knowledge Records exist, so this commission starts from no existing approved knowledge on the subject.

The full draft Research Commission Record sets out five commissioned questions, reproduced here for reference:

- **Q1.** What is a bush rose? How is it classified relative to climbing/rambling, standard/tree, species and shrub roses, and is "bush rose" a term sources use consistently?
- **Q2.** What does ordinary care across a year involve, apart from pruning — watering, feeding, mulching, deadheading, general pest/disease watchfulness — and where do sources agree or differ (e.g., by climate/region)?
- **Q3.** What care is appropriate for a rose that is not yet ready for pruning (too recently planted, not yet dormant, or otherwise deferred)?
- **Q4.** What does winter protection involve, separately from pruning itself?
- **Q5.** What are simple, general signs of poor health worth a gardener's attention — explicitly *not* diagnosis?

**Excluded from this commission:** pruning mechanics (covered by the separate, already-progressed `PRUNINGFRAMEWORK` commission), diagnosis of specific diseases or pests, product or chemical brand recommendations, planting a new rose, and climate-specific schedules presented as universal.

This commission record remains in draft, not yet formally confirmed by the Founders as a standalone approved commission. It is presented here alongside the completed research so that commission and findings can be reviewed together; if the Founders wish to amend the commission's scope, this dossier's findings can be revised to match.

## 3. Research Plan

**A note on sequencing, in the interest of transparency (ROC Charter: "Transparency over certainty"):** the Operations Manual sequences the Research Plan before Evidence Collection begins. In practice here, the plan below was formalised alongside evidence collection rather than strictly ahead of it, because the immediately preceding step was reading the governing documents themselves for the first time. The plan reflects the actual questions and approach used; it is recorded honestly as having been written concurrently, not retroactively dressed up as prior planning.

**Information requirements**, derived directly from the five commissioned questions above (Q1–Q5).

**Anticipated source types:** national and regional horticultural authorities and societies (e.g. the Royal Horticultural Society); university cooperative extension services, which publish research-based, non-commercial home-gardening guidance and are a recognised source type for this kind of question; secondarily, established gardening trade/enthusiast publications where they cite named horticultural practitioners, used only to corroborate or fill a gap left by institutional sources, never as a sole source for a finding.

**Approach:** search broadly per question, favouring sources with clear institutional backing and a stated basis (research-based extension guidance, or a named professional's stated reasoning) over anonymous commercial gardening-blog content; deliberately seek sources from more than one country/region and more than one institution per question, since a single source repeating itself under different headings does not count as independent confirmation (Evidence Assessment Standard §4, Independence); read each source in enough depth to capture specific figures, dates and stated reasoning, not just headline claims; note disagreement as it is found rather than resolving it by picking a preferred source.

**Known challenges anticipated:** climate and regional variation is expected to produce genuinely different specific figures (dates, quantities) across sources that are not in conflict on the underlying principle — this needed to be told apart from real disagreement; "bush rose" was anticipated as a possibly inconsistent term across sources, since horticultural classification language varies by country; simple non-diagnostic "signs of poor health" content is harder to find in isolation, since most published material on this subject is diagnostic (aimed at identifying a specific disease or pest) rather than general-awareness in nature, which required more selective sourcing for Q5 specifically.

## 5. Evidence Evaluation

Evaluated against the Evidence Assessment Standard's factors (relevance, source reliability, consistency, independence, limitations, uncertainty):

**Relevance.** All eleven sources address the commissioned questions directly; none required inference to connect to Q1–Q5.

**Source reliability.** Nine of eleven sources are university cooperative extension services or a national horticultural charity (RHS) — bodies with a recognised research basis and no commercial interest in the guidance given. The remaining source (Fine Gardening) is a trade/enthusiast publication; it was retained only because it names and quotes a specific, identifiable professional rosarian (Paul Zimmerman) and its central claim (don't prune a newly planted rose; let foliage establish) is independently corroborated by two institutional sources (Missouri Extension, Cornell CCE Rockland). It was not used as a sole source for any finding.

**Consistency.** Strong agreement on underlying principles across nearly all findings (see §7 and §8). Where sources differ, the differences are almost entirely in specific figures (exact dates, exact inches, exact amounts) rather than in the underlying guidance — this distinction is treated carefully in §6 below rather than being flattened into either "agreement" or "conflict."

**Independence.** The nine extension sources are operated by nine separate institutions in different US states (plus RHS in the UK), each publishing its own guidance rather than syndicating a shared original — genuine independent corroboration, not the same material repeated under different banners (Evidence Assessment Standard §4, Independence).

**Limitations.** Coverage skews heavily toward the UK and the US; no source from a different climate zone or gardening tradition (e.g. Southern Hemisphere, continental Europe, Asia) was consulted. Q5 (non-diagnostic signs of poor health) rests on meaningfully thinner evidence than Q1–Q4, because most published material on rose health is diagnostic by nature (see §3). One source (UGA Cobb County) includes specific branded-product recommendations (e.g. named proprietary fertiliser and fungicide products); these are excluded from every finding below, consistent with the commission's stated exclusion of product/chemical recommendations — only that source's general timing/structure was drawn on.

**Uncertainty.** Recorded per finding in §8 via the assigned Evidence Confidence Level, rather than as a single blended uncertainty statement for the whole commission (Evidence Assessment Standard §3.2, §5.3).

## 6. Managing Conflicting Evidence and Uncertainty

Three areas of apparent disagreement were investigated. In each case, the disagreement was found to be a difference in specific, climate-dependent figures rather than a genuine conflict about the underlying guidance — consistent with what the Research Plan (§3) anticipated.

**Feeding cutoff date.** Illinois Extension states no fertiliser should be applied after 15 August. Penn State Extension describes stopping monthly fertilisation in August. UGA Extension (Cobb County, Georgia — a materially warmer, later-frost climate) schedules a fertiliser application as late as September. Investigation: these figures track each region's own first-frost timing, not a disagreement about the reasoning (stop feeding early enough that new growth can harden off before winter, wherever winter arrives for that garden). Recorded as regional variation, not conflict. No single date is treated as a finding in its own right (see F4).

**Deadheading cutoff date.** Illinois Extension gives 1 October as an explicit stop date; UGA Cobb gives October generally; Iowa State describes stopping "in fall" without a specific date. Same underlying reasoning in all three (stopping deadheading lets the plant set hips and signals it toward dormancy). Recorded as regional variation, not conflict (see F5).

**Mulch depth.** At first glance, sources appear to disagree sharply — some describe 2–3 inches, others 10–12 inches. Investigation showed this is not a disagreement at all: every source describing 2–3 inches is discussing ordinary growing-season mulch for moisture/weed control, while every source describing 10–12 inches is discussing the separate practice of winter protection mounding. Conflating the two would misrepresent the evidence; they are kept as two separate findings (F3 and F9) rather than merged or treated as contradictory.

No area of genuine, unresolved conflict (as opposed to explainable regional variation) was found in the evidence gathered for this commission.

## 7. Principal Findings (Assessed Findings)

Each finding below is a single, discrete, evidence-stated claim, kept separate from findings of differing evidential strength even where they relate to the same topic (Operations Manual §8.3; Evidence Assessment Standard §3.2).

**F1.** UK horticultural authority (RHS) uses "bush rose" to mean modern shrub-form garden roses, principally Hybrid Tea and Floribunda types, as distinct from climbing/rambling roses, standard (tree) roses, and species/shrub roses.

**F1a — usage observation, not a separate factual claim.** None of the nine US extension sources consulted used "bush rose" as a category label; they referred to the same growth habit using "Hybrid Tea," "Floribunda," "Grandiflora" or simply "rose bush" descriptively. This suggests "bush rose" as a formal classification term may be more UK/Commonwealth-specific than universal, rather than being a term all English-language horticultural sources use consistently.

**F2.** Established bush roses are generally watered deeply and infrequently rather than shallowly and often, in the general range of 1–2 inches (or local rainfall equivalent) of water per week during the growing season when rainfall is insufficient, with closer attention needed in roughly the first two years after planting.

**F3.** During the growing season, a 2–3 inch layer of organic mulch (bark, well-rotted manure, wood chips, pine needles) applied around the base and kept a few inches clear of the stem/crown is commonly recommended to conserve moisture and suppress weeds.

**F4.** Feeding commonly follows a pattern of starting in spring as new growth begins, with further application(s) through summer, and stopping by mid-to-late summer so new growth can harden before winter. The exact cutoff date varies by source/region (see §6) and is not itself treated as a single fixed fact.

**F5.** Deadheading (removing spent blooms) during the growing season is commonly recommended to encourage further flowering, and is commonly stopped in early-to-mid autumn to allow the plant to set hips and move toward dormancy. The exact cutoff date varies by source/region (see §6) and is not itself treated as a single fixed fact.

**F6.** Commonly cited rose problems to watch for include blackspot, powdery mildew, aphids and spider mites; sources generally advise watering at the base, or in the morning if overhead watering is used, to reduce the foliage wetness associated with fungal disease.

**F7.** A rose planted within roughly the past year is generally not pruned the way an established rose is; sources recommend leaving as much foliage and stem growth as possible in that first season — limiting cuts to removing spent blooms rather than cutting stems for arrangements — because a newly planted rose relies on its foliage to photosynthesise and its energy to establish its root system.

**F9.** For bush roses in climates with hard winter frosts, common winter protection guidance is to wait until the plant has had several hard/killing frosts and entered dormancy, then mound soil, compost, or mulch to a height of approximately 10–12 inches around the base/crown (covering the graft union on grafted plants), removing the mound again in spring once hard-frost risk has passed.

**F10.** Additional or alternative winter protection methods described include building a collar (tarpaper, wire mesh, or similar) around the plant and filling it with insulating material (straw, pine bark, peat moss), and wrapping canes with burlap or evergreen boughs — described particularly for climbing roses or more exposed/colder sites.

**F11.** Watering thoroughly after the first hard frost but before the ground freezes, so the plant enters winter well-hydrated, and clearing fallen leaf debris from around the plant before winter to reduce overwintering fungal spores, were both specifically described as part of winter preparation.

**F12.** Gardeners are commonly advised to regularly observe a rose's leaves, buds and stems through the growing season for general signs such as yellowing, spotting or other discoloration, leaf drop, stunted or reduced growth, distorted or curled leaves, and reduced or absent flowering — with noticing these signs early, before identifying a specific cause, described as the useful first step.

**F13.** At least one source states explicitly that ordinary growing conditions and care — rather than pests or diseases — are the most common cause of a rose's decline, particularly in newer plantings, and specifically names inconsistent watering, poor drainage/soggy soil, and weather extremes as worth a gardener's first consideration when a rose looks unwell.

## 9. Recommendations for Further Research

The following are ROC's own observations and reasoning, not directly stated by any source gathered. Per Evidence Assessment Standard §2.10 and Operations Manual §8.4A, none of these are Assessed Findings, none carry a confidence level, and none should be treated as approved or approvable knowledge on the strength of this dossier. Each is eligible to become its own Assessed Finding only once independent evidence is found for it.

- **Connecting Q3 to already-approved findings.** This commission's evidence on ordinary establishment-period care (F7, and the general pattern in F2–F6) appears complementary to the already-approved RECENTPLANT and DORMANCY findings from the pruning-framework research, in that both describe a newly planted or not-yet-dormant rose as needing ordinary maintenance rather than pruning. This dossier has not cross-checked its findings against the specific source basis of those approved findings, and no explicit connection between the two bodies of evidence has been verified. If the Founders wish, a focused follow-up could confirm the two are compatible (or identify where they should be reconciled) before any integrated guidance is built.
- **Geographic diversity.** All sources gathered are from the UK or the continental US. A further research pass seeking a source from a different climate tradition (e.g. Southern Hemisphere, continental Europe) would strengthen F2–F6 and F9–F11 and might surface guidance relevant to Ask Pip users gardening outside those regions.
- **Q5 depth.** Non-diagnostic "signs of poor health" content is thin in the wider literature, most of which is diagnostic by design. A further, more targeted search — including asking a horticultural extension service directly, if that avenue is available to the project — could strengthen F12 and F13 beyond their current Moderate and Low–Moderate ratings.
- **F1's UK/US terminology gap.** Worth a specific, separate small research pass: is "bush rose" ever used as a formal classification term in US or other non-UK horticultural literature, or is it consistently absent, as this dossier's (limited) evidence suggests? This bears directly on the words Ask Pip uses with gardeners outside the UK.
- **F11's single-source status.** A second independent source for pre-winter watering/debris-removal guidance would let this move out of Low confidence.
