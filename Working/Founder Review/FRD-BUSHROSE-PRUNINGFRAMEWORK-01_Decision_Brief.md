## 2. The short version

**The research.** We asked how recognised sources say an established, dormant bush rose should be pruned as a whole, including how each cut is made. We read 24 pages from 20 organisations and recorded what they say, how well they agree, and what they leave out.

**The result, in numbers.** The research produced 30 claims (the FRD calls them Assessed Findings, AF-1 to AF-30). Each has its own strength rating: **11 High, 13 Moderate and 6 Low**. There is no single rating for the whole piece of research, and none should be worked out from these. Ten disagreements between sources were found and left unresolved (C1 to C10). Ten further matters produced no finding (U-1 to U-10).

**What the sources agree on (the High findings).** Pruning aims to remove unhealthy wood, open the bush to light and air, and encourage flowering growth. Damaged and diseased wood, crossing or rubbing stems, and weak or spindly growth come out. Dead, damaged and diseased wood is dealt with first, and unwanted growth is removed before the remaining canes are shortened. The aim is an open-centred bush with canes spread round the base. Once-flowering roses are handled differently. For the cut itself, choose an outward-facing bud, choose the tool by the thickness of the stem, and use sharp, clean tools.

**Where sources point the same way but with less spread or more variation (Moderate).** This includes the finer figures: how far above the bud, what angle, how many canes to keep, how much to shorten, what age of wood is "old", how thin is "weak". It also includes some statements that are not about figures, such as inward-growing stems, rootstock suckers and disinfecting tools. Many American pages appear to repeat the same rules of thumb.

**Where the ground is thin (Low).** Only a few sources, or the wording conflicts: the reasons for removing crossing stems, new basal shoots, renewing over several years, which way the cut slants, where pruners give way to loppers, and sealing cut ends.

**What the sources leave open.** Ten matters produced no finding, because the sources are silent, conflict, or could not be retrieved. Among them: how to treat a stem that fits more than one category, how to choose between two crossing stems, how to find the framework in a tangle, when to pause or ask for help, and what can be judged from a photograph. Each is recorded as an absence of evidence, not as a finding.

**Images.** None of the 24 sources states an open licence for its images, and nothing has been requested. No image is available for reuse on the strength of what the pages say (section 8).

**One thing to keep in mind.** The findings record what recognised sources say and how firmly. They do not tell Pip what to say to a gardener, and none of them lets Pip authorise a cut from a photograph.

## 3. What was researched

**The commission.** Nine questions about pruning an established, dormant bush rose as a whole, asked by the AskPIP Founder Authority on 21 September 2026: why we prune (Q1); what categories of growth sources use (Q2); the order of work (Q3); how a stem in more than one category is handled (Q4); the shape to aim for and how to find the stems that carry it (Q5); limits on how much to take off, and differences by rose class (Q6); when sources advise pausing or getting help (Q7); what can be judged from photographs (Q8); and how the cut is made (Q9).

**In scope.** The established bush rose already defined for Ask Pip (hybrid tea, floribunda and, provisionally, grandiflora), pruned in the dormant season, considered as a whole plant, plus the mechanics of the cut.

**Out of scope.** Recognising each individual observation in detail (those are separate commissions), re-researching dead wood, dormancy or recent planting (already approved), other rose types, pruning outside the dormant season, feeding, pests, planting, and any authorising of a cut from an image.

**The sources.** 24 pages: one from the United Kingdom (the RHS), three from New Zealand (one public garden and two commercial), three from American specialist societies or public gardens, and seventeen from American university extension services and similar pages. They come from 20 organisations. Some pages come from the same publisher and are not counted as independent of each other. Appendix A lists every source and gives the short code used throughout.

**How they were read.** Every page was read the same way, against all nine questions, in three search passes. Where a page did not address a question, that was recorded as silence, not as disagreement.

**Two limits on everything below.** First, all the guidance is practitioner advice. No controlled study of these practices was found. Second, the pages were read through a tool that returns a machine-generated summary of each page, not the page itself. Quoted wording is as the tool reported it, and every quotation needs checking against the live page before any Approved Research Compilation (ARC) is written from it (section 11).

## 4. How to read the strength ratings

Each claim carries one of three ratings. They describe how much the evidence supports the statement that recognised sources say this, and nothing more.

| Rating | Plain meaning | How many claims |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Sources agree closely across at least three of four groups (UK, New Zealand, societies and gardens, US extension), with no real contradiction and only minor limits. | 11 |
| **Moderate** | Sources point the same way, but the spread is narrower, or the detail varies, or we cannot show that the sources are independent of each other. | 13 |
| **Low** | Only a few sources, or sources about other kinds of rose, or the wording of the sources conflicts. | 6 |

**Groups and organisations.** Each claim's backing is shown as sources, organisations and groups. Two pages from one publisher count as two sources but one organisation. The four groups are: the United Kingdom (RHS, one page); New Zealand (DUN, YAT, SPR); specialist societies and public gardens (ARS, KCRS, RBG); and US university extension and similar pages (seventeen). A claim backed by many pages from one group is generally rated lower than one backed by fewer pages from several groups, which is why a claim with eleven sources can be Moderate while one with eight can be High.

Three cautions.

- **A rating is a judgement, not a formula.** The FRD explains how it was reached for each claim (FRD section 8).
- **Repeated is not the same as confirmed.** Many American pages repeat the same rules of thumb (a pencil's thickness, a quarter inch above the bud, three to five canes, a 45 degree cut). We could not show whether one page copied another, so we treated a rule found only in American pages as repeated, not independently confirmed. That is why several of these are Moderate.
- **A rating says nothing about whether a rule is right for Pip.** It says how firmly the sources support the statement. Approving a claim at Moderate means you accept that it is a fair statement, at that strength, and no more.

### 5.2 What comes out (Q2)

The Ask Pip MVP already supports six observations: dead versus living wood; damaged growth; crossing or rubbing stems; inward-growing stems; weak or congested growth; and the main framework to retain. |---|---|---|---|---|

| AF-3 | Stems that cross or rub against each other are cut out. | **High** | 11 sources; 10 organisations; 4 of 4 groups | Sources say to remove them, but none says how to choose which of two crossing stems to take out (gap U-2). |

| AF-5 | Weak, thin, spindly or twiggy growth is cut out. | **High** | 13 sources; 12 organisations; 3 of 4 groups | — |
| AF-6 | Sources use 'about as thick as a pencil' as the dividing line: canes thinner than that are treated as weak, canes thicker are worth keeping. | **Moderate** | 9 sources; 8 organisations; 2 of 4 groups | All nine sources are American, and the rule may be one rule of thumb repeated, not independently confirmed. | AF-7 | Old, woody or unproductive canes are removed in favour of younger canes. | **Moderate** | 13 sources; 12 organisations; 4 of 4 groups | Sources differ on how old is 'old' (C6), and none explains how to recognise old wood (gap U-9). Whether older and younger wood should become a supported observation in its own right is one of the decisions in section 10 (Decision 5).

### 5.6 How the cut is made (Q9)

This is the cut itself. All ten claims answer Question 9. Most of the detail is practitioner advice with rules of thumb repeated across pages, and there is no scientific evidence on the finer points. TAMK is a general woody-plant text with a short rose section, and it is among the sources for AF-21, AF-23, AF-25, AF-26, AF-27, AF-28, AF-29. The RHS and the two commercial New Zealand pages say little on cut mechanics, and the United Kingdom is represented by one page.

| ID | What sources say | Strength | Backing | Worth knowing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AF-21 | For most cuts back to a bud, choose a bud that faces outward, away from the centre of the bush. | **High** | 18 sources; 16 organisations; 3 of 4 groups | Applies to cuts back to a bud. Cutting a stem off at its origin is a different cut (AF-29). No UK source supports it (the RHS gives no bud-direction advice), and two sources allow exceptions. |
| AF-22 | Sources that give a distance most often say to cut about one-quarter inch (about 6 mm) above the bud. | **Moderate** | 11 sources; 9 organisations; 2 of 4 groups | All eleven sources are American, and a quarter inch is a widely repeated rule of thumb. Three American sources give a different distance (C2). |
| AF-23 | Sources that give an angle describe a slanting cut of roughly 45 degrees, not a flat one. | **Moderate** | 7 sources; 7 organisations; 2 of 4 groups | Support is from New Zealand and US sources only. One source gives a range of 30 to 45 degrees. |
| AF-24 | Sources that say which way the slant falls most often say it slopes away from the bud. | **Low** | 5 sources; 5 organisations; 3 of 4 groups | Rated Low because the wording conflicts: some sources say 'away from the bud', others 'in the same direction as the bud'. They may describe one shape or two, and the research could not settle it (C3). |
| AF-25 | The tool is chosen by the thickness of the stem: bypass pruners for thin canes, loppers for thicker canes, and a pruning saw for the thickest or oldest. | **High** | 9 sources; 9 organisations; 3 of 4 groups | Sources differ over bypass and anvil pruners (C9). |
| AF-26 | Sources that give a figure say hand pruners are suitable up to about three-quarters of an inch. One general woody-plant text says one-half inch. | **Low** | 3 sources (OSU, ISU, TAMK); 3 organisations; 1 of 4 groups | Only three sources (C8). |
| AF-27 | Tools should be sharp and clean, so that the cut is clean and the cane is not crushed. | **High** | 12 sources; 11 organisations; 3 of 4 groups | — |
| AF-28 | Several sources advise disinfecting tools between plants, or after cutting diseased wood, so as not to spread disease. | **Moderate** | 7 sources; 6 organisations; 2 of 4 groups | Sources name different disinfectants. The FRD records them as what sources say and does not recommend any (C10). |
| AF-29 | A stem that is being removed is cut off at its point of origin (the base, the graft or its parent stem) and no stub is left. | **Moderate** | 9 sources; 9 organisations; 3 of 4 groups | For categories other than dead wood, only four sources address it, and one of them is a general woody-plant text (TAMK). No source says how it applies to crossing, inward or weak stems specifically. |
| AF-30 | Some sources advise sealing the cut ends of larger canes with glue, nail polish or pruning sealer to deter cane borers. One source says sealing is optional, and one general woody-plant text advises against wound dressings. | **Low** | 6 sources; 6 organisations; 2 of 4 groups | This is contested (C4). Rated Low. |

**Two kinds of cut.** Most cut guidance is written for a cut back to a bud (AF-21 to AF-24). Guidance for cutting a stem off at its origin is thinner (AF-29). The FRD suggests that research could test whether the two kinds of cut need separate guidance (R-3). That is the ROC's own reasoning, not a finding.

**Where cut mechanics sit in Pip's knowledge.** The FRD asks whether the cut findings should be approved as part of this same ARC, and whether you want to decide separately how they are held and linked from each observation (Decision 3).

## 9. What approval would and would not do

| Approving a claim would | Approving a claim would not |
|---|---|
| Allow that claim, with its rating and its recorded limits, to be written into an Approved Research Compilation (ARC) in the Mother Information Library. Only what you expressly approve goes in (ROC Manual section 11.9). | Tell Pip what to say to a gardener. Pip's records and behaviour are built from an ARC in later, separate steps. |
| Preserve any conditions or changes you attach, in the approved record. | Remove the uncertainty. Approval does not take documented limits or disagreements out of the record (ROC Manual section 11.4). |
| Leave the underlying research and evidence in the commission record, whatever you decide. | Approve any image, or authorise a cut from a photograph. Pip never authorises a cut from a photograph alone. |

If you flag, defer or decline a claim, it stays in the commission record. It is not deleted and it does not go into the Mother Information Library (ROC Manual section 11.10). If you ask for changes or more research, the ROC revises and resubmits, and keeps the earlier version.

| **Heading cut** | A cut that shortens a stem that stays, usually back to a bud. |

| **Rootstock sucker** | A shoot growing from the rootstock, below the bud union. |
| **Once-flowering rose** | A rose that flowers once a year, often on last year's wood, for example old garden roses, ramblers and rugosas. |
| **Hybrid tea, floribunda, grandiflora** | The three bush-rose classes within Ask Pip's supported scope (grandiflora provisionally). |
| **Cane borer** | An insect pest that tunnels into cut cane ends. |
