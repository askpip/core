## 1. What gardeners will see

The six questions are already in the app, with answers for New Zealand, Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States. Today a gardener with a rose in Canada who opens them is told Pip has nothing for that place yet. On approval they see the statements below.

### Which months do I prune my rose?

- In Canada, one rose society says the main pruning is usually done in April. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[Low confidence]*
- Let the forsythia (a shrub with yellow flowers) tell you when: prune your rose when the forsythia is in flower where you live. *[High confidence]*
- Look at the buds as well: prune as they begin to swell, before any leaves open. *[Moderate confidence]*
- If your rose has winter protection, take it off first, then prune. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Wait until the danger of a hard frost has passed, or the newly pruned tips may be killed. *[Moderate confidence]* *(precaution)*
- In Toronto, one Master Gardener group says the main pruning is usually in April or May, just before new growth begins and as the buds begin to swell. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near Toronto only.**
- On the coast of British Columbia, the main pruning is done in late winter, between late February and mid-March, as the buds begin to swell. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners on the coast of British Columbia only** (the Lower Mainland, the Fraser Valley and Victoria).
- In the Okanagan, one garden company says not to prune in fall, but to wait until early spring. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near the Okanagan only.**
- I couldn't find a source that gives a month for the main pruning of Hybrid Tea or Floribunda roses in Quebec, Alberta, Saskatchewan or Manitoba. A local rose society or horticultural society is the place to ask. *[No source found]* **Gardeners in Quebec, Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba only.**
- I couldn't find rose months from a source in Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island or Newfoundland and Labrador. A local rose society or horticultural society is the place to ask. *[No source found]* **Gardeners in Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland and Labrador only.**

### When do I start and stop feeding?

- Give the first feed in spring, once you have tidied the bed and pruned, as new growth begins. *[High confidence]*
- Give the last feed by mid-July, so that the stems can ripen before winter. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]*
- In Toronto, one Master Gardener group says not to feed your roses after the end of July. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near Toronto only.**
- In Barrie and Orillia, one rose society says to feed established roses once you have tidied the bed, following the directions on the container, again before the peak of bloom in June, and a last time in mid-July. Months are only a guide. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near Barrie or Orillia only.**
- On the coast of British Columbia, sources disagree about the last feed. The national rose society says to give the last feed by mid-July. A garden company says to stop feeding by late summer, and a rose nursery says to give no feed from September onward. Months are only a guide. *[Sources disagree]* **Gardeners on the coast of British Columbia only** (the Lower Mainland, the Fraser Valley and Victoria).

### When do I stop deadheading?

- Towards the end of the season, stop deadheading (removing the faded flowers) and let the last flowers go to seed. The hips (the rose's fruit) can stay on the stems over winter. *[Moderate confidence]*
- One rose grower says to stop deadheading a month before the first frost is expected. *[Low confidence]*
- Don't prune your rose in late summer or fall; leave the main pruning until spring. The one exception is shortening very tall canes, so that winter winds don't whip them about. *[Moderate confidence]*

### Which months do I plant or move a rose?

- Plant a bare-root rose in spring: in April, or as soon as the ground has thawed and you can dig the soil. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Outside the Prairies (Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba), a rose can also be planted in fall: sources give September, or late fall. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]*
- On the Prairies (Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba), one rose society says planting in fall is not recommended. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba only.**
- A rose in a pot can be planted at any time during the growing season. *[Moderate confidence]*
- In Kingston, one Master Gardener group says a rose in a pot is best planted in spring or early summer, and that bare-root grafted roses can be ordered for planting in late fall. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near Kingston only.**
- I couldn't find a Canadian source on when to move an established rose. A local rose society or horticultural society is the place to ask. *[No source found]*

### When do I clear up and spray in winter?

- In the colder parts of Canada, Hybrid Tea and Floribunda roses need protecting over winter. *[High confidence]*
- In fall, as you get your roses ready for winter, remove all dead leaves and clippings from the bed. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Put the winter protection (such as a mound of soil or a cover) on after hard frosts, once the rose is dormant (resting, no longer growing) and the ground is starting to freeze. Don't put it on too early. *[High confidence]*
- Take the winter protection off in spring as the soil thaws. Sources disagree on how soon: some say as soon as thawing allows, while the national rose society waits until late spring, when a hard freeze is no longer expected. *[Sources disagree]*
- Take the protection off a little at a time as the ground thaws, not all at once. *[Low confidence]* *(precaution)*
- On the coast of British Columbia, winter protection is not usually needed. If extreme cold is forecast, protect newly planted roses, and move roses in pots into a garage or other unheated space. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners on the coast of British Columbia only** (the Lower Mainland, the Fraser Valley and Victoria).
- In Ontario, one source says to put winter protection on in mid- to late November, once the ground is frozen, and not too early. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Ontario only.**
- In Georgina, one garden centre says to start getting roses ready for winter in early to mid-November or after the first few hard frosts, and to wait for several hard frosts before covering them, because covering too soon can trap moisture and invite rot or mildew. It says to uncover them gradually, around late April or early May, once the snow has melted and daytime temperatures consistently stay above freezing. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near Georgina only.**
- In Quebec, one garden company says to wait for the cooler weather of November before putting on foam cones, though nets, wooden frames and snow fences can go up earlier. It says to take the protection off as soon as the snow and ice allow. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Quebec only.**
- In Alberta, one guide says to cut the canes back to about 70 cm and remove the remaining leaves, to wait until the rose is fully dormant (resting, no longer growing), and then to build the mound once the ground is at least partly frozen. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Alberta only.**
- In Alberta, one garden company says to water your roses for the winter on 15 October, and to take the winter cover off around the May long weekend. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in Alberta only.**
- In the Okanagan, one garden company says to mound soil or compost around the base of grafted roses in late fall. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near the Okanagan only.**
- In Barrie and Orillia, one rose society says a dormant-oil spray early in spring, and again before the winter protection goes on, will keep disease down. If you use one, follow the label and the local rules. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in or near Barrie or Orillia only.**

## 2. What you should know

- **Each statement is shown only in the place its source speaks of.** 19 are for all of Canada. 18 are for one place: Ontario, Quebec and Alberta; the Prairies together (Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba); the Atlantic provinces together, for the one gap there; and nine towns and areas (Toronto, Kingston, Barrie and Orillia, Georgina, the Okanagan, and the coast of British Columbia as the Lower Mainland, the Fraser Valley and Victoria). A gardener anywhere else sees the statements for all of Canada.
- **The app learns nine towns and areas,** so that their local advice is shown only there. A gardener elsewhere on the BC coast (Nanaimo, for example) sees the Canada-wide statements, not the coastal ones. Nothing else in the app changes.
- **No hardiness zones.** The research grouped places into climate bands and used the Canadian Rose Society's zone bands. No band is used, and Pip never asks for a zone. The society's advice that it gives only under a zone band is kept on record.
- **Your decision on single companies is applied.** One garden company or nursery that disagrees with a recognised body is not shown: later last feeds in Ontario (early and mid-August) and Alberta (mid-August), the Okanagan's late-summer last feed, one Quebec company's covering "just before the first frosts", and one Alberta nursery's fall cut to about 6 inches. The findings stay on record.
- **One judgement for you.** On the BC coast, a garden company and a rose nursery both feed later than the national rose society's mid-July. That is two sources, not one, so it is shown there as "Sources disagree", leading with the society. If you would rather it went, say so on the form.
- **One spray.** A local rose society (Barrie and Orillia) gives a dormant-oil spray in early spring and before winter protection; Pip shows it there and sends the gardener to the label and local rules. A Quebec garden company's dormant oil is not given, as with the UK.
- **Gaps are said.** No month for the main pruning in Quebec or the Prairies, no rose months at all for the Atlantic provinces, and no Canadian source on when to move an established rose. Each points the gardener to a local rose or horticultural society.
- **Most local statements rest on one source and say so.** 16 of the 37 statements carry a Low label. Several local ones rest on a garden company, as your standing decision allows; each says "one garden company says" and that months are only a guide.
- **16 findings are left out:** 10 about zones and how sources organise timing, and 6 on record only (two given only under a zone band, one magazine article on its own, one page that disagrees with itself, and two from single companies).
- **The check corrected the research before you saw it.** Every quotation was on its page. The main fault was place: findings spoke for climate bands and zone bands, and some gathered pages from different provinces into one claim. 22 findings were reworded or had their level changed, limitations were added to 16, and 3 findings were added from sentences confirmed on the pages.
- **Tested against the app's own place code** for 24 test plants, 22 of them in Canada and two elsewhere (Australia and the UK): each saw only its own place's statements. Near a provincial or US border the app asks which province rather than guess, as it should. The app builds with the new places. Not checked: the screens themselves in a browser, the signed-in screens and a real phone. The harness used for the earlier countries was not available in this session.
