## 1. What changes for gardeners

Each line shows the sentence as it is now, then as it will be. Labels are in brackets. Sentences not listed do not change.

### Is the rose still dormant? (the dormancy gate)

- **Question.** Now: "Look closely at the dormant buds along your rose's canes. Has active new growth already begun — buds swelling, breaking open, or new leaves emerging anywhere on the plant — or does the plant still appear dormant?" **Becomes:** "Look closely at the buds along your rose's canes. Are they still tight and closed, or just beginning to swell? Or have leaves opened and new shoots started to grow?" *Why: the old question counted swelling buds as growth already begun, while its own answers and the research put swelling buds under "ready to prune".*
- **Mild-winter note.** Becomes: "In a mild winter, a rose may keep some of its leaves, so judge by the buds, not by whether the leaves have dropped. Warm spells can also start buds growing early."
- **"Not sure" reply.** Becomes: "That's alright — have another look at the buds, not the leaves: are they still tight and closed, or have any started to swell or open?" *The old reply added "the safest choice is to treat the rose as not quite ready, and check back in a week or two", which no source says.*

### Is the rose newly planted? (the recently-planted gate) — as you directed

- **Question.** Now: "…has it been growing in this spot for about three years or more?" **Becomes:** "Was this rose planted this growing season, so that this is its first season in this spot?" *[Moderate confidence]* Answers: "Yes, it was planted this growing season" / "No, it has been growing here longer than that" / "I'm not sure".
- **If yes:** "Most sources leave a newly planted rose unpruned, apart from dead wood, until it has established; two prune at planting or in the first winter. So I'll only look at dead wood today."
- **If not sure:** the three signs (new growth, more than about three canes, a firm base) are no longer asked. "I can't tell whether this rose has established yet, so I'll only look at dead wood today."

*Why: no bush-rose page gives three years; the figure is one extension service's for shrub roses. Three rose sources say a newly planted rose is left unpruned, apart from dead wood, until it has established. The three signs rested on a Low finding that would allow more pruning, which the rules do not allow.*

### Dead wood

- "Let's look for dead wood. Dead canes are usually brown, black or shrivelled rather than green, and have no live buds." *(no page gives grey bark)*
- "Bark colour: green suggests living; brown, black or shrivelled suggests dead. One source says living wood on older canes, or on roses with bronze stems, can look brown." *[Moderate confidence]*
- "Buds: plump, healthy buds suggest living wood; no live buds suggests dead." *[Moderate confidence]*
- "Sunken patches or lesions on a cane may be canker, a fungal disease that kills more of the cane as it spreads, rather than ordinary dead wood." *[Low confidence] (precaution)*
- "Cut into the cane and look at the pith (the centre): white is living, brown is dead." *[High confidence]* *(was "the surest check… white or pale green… brown, grey or black": seven pages agree on white and brown; the other colours are on one or two pages)*
- **Removed:** "A living cane bends; a dead one is brittle and snaps." *(one page, about woody plants in general; a Low finding the rules do not let Pip use)*
- Photo line: "A photo can't show the pith inside the cane — check that on the plant."
- "Not sure" line: "Look again at the cane on the plant itself — its buds and its bark colour."
- **When you cut:** "Cut a dead cane back into living wood, where the pith is white, not just to where the dead part first shows. One source differs: it says to cut at the point of discoloration." *[High confidence]*
- "If the pith is still brown, keep cutting lower in small steps. One source says to remove the whole cane if no live buds remain on it." *[Moderate confidence]* *(was "keep going toward the bud union, or remove the whole cane")*

### Crossing or rubbing stems

- "A stem that crosses another, or rubs against it, is a candidate." *(no label; was "Crossing and rubbing are looked for together", Moderate)*
- "Where two stems cross or rub, remove one of them as part of ordinary pruning." *[High confidence]*
- "Cut the stem you remove back to the bud union (the swollen join near the base) or to the larger cane it grows from, rather than just shortening it. One source also allows cutting it back to the next outward-facing bud." *[Moderate confidence]*
- **New, from the inward-growing check:** "When you shorten a cane, cut just above a bud that faces outward, so the new shoot grows away from the centre of the bush." *[Moderate confidence]*
- "Why: stems rubbing together damage each other's canes, which lets disease in." *[Moderate confidence]*
- **Removed:** "If you need to choose which one, the few sources that give a rule say remove the weaker, thinner one…" *(one page on point; a Low finding)*

### Inward-growing stems — the check is removed, as you directed

The separate step goes. Its sign ("a stem growing toward the centre") and its cut rested on Low findings, which the rules do not let Pip use. What the research does support at Moderate, cutting to an outward-facing bud, is now part of the crossing-stems step above. One rose society advises against clearing the centre at all.

### Suckers

- "Let's check for suckers. A sucker is a shoot from the rootstock your rose was grafted onto, below the bud union — the swollen join near the base. It isn't your rose's own new cane."
- "Where the shoot starts is the deciding check: below the bud union is a sucker; at the union or just above it is usually your rose's own new cane, which you keep." *[High confidence]* Shown under it: "One nursery says the rootstock can also send up shoots from its stem above the union."
- "That is most likely your rose's own new cane. Keep it: new canes from the base are valuable. I'll look at it again when we check the main framework."
- "That's alright. Suckers and a rose's own new shoots are easy to mix up. Look again at where the shoot starts, on the plant, not in a photo." *(was "even for experienced gardeners", which no page says)*
- "Can you see the bud union — the swollen join near the base where your rose was grafted?"
- "If you know your rose grows on its own roots (it wasn't grafted), shoots from the base are the same rose, so they are not suckers in the usual sense."
- "In the growing season only, these can help you look, but never decide on their own: sucker leaves are often paler or smaller, or have a different number of leaflets (though counting seven leaflets isn't always a reliable test); sucker flowers often differ from your rose's; suckers are often unusually vigorous."
- "Don't leave a confirmed sucker. It weakens your rose, so remove it." *[High confidence]*
- **Removed:** "If suckers keep coming back after removal, get experienced local help." *(no source says it)*
- **Removing it:** "Uncover the point where the sucker starts, clearing soil if needed, and remove it right there. Don't just cut it off at soil level, because it can grow back." *[High confidence]*
- "Most sources say to grip the sucker as close to where it starts as you can and pull or tear it away, because tearing takes the dormant buds at its base with it and so makes regrowth less likely. Some sources disagree and cut it cleanly instead." *[Sources disagree]* *(was told as settled; about eight pages pull or tear, four or five cut cleanly)*
- "One source says to wear gloves and support the stem with one hand while you pull the sucker off. Another says to refill the soil afterwards and firm it gently around the base." *[Low confidence] (precaution)*
- "Take care around the base when weeding or digging. One source says damaging the roots may cause suckers." *[Low confidence] (precaution)*

## 2. What you should know

- **Your direction is applied:** "whatever the research most strongly suggests". It settled three points: the recently-planted question, the removal of its three signs, and the inward-growing check.
- **Gardeners will notice two changes in the journey.** A rose whose planting date is unknown now gets the dead-wood-only check, and there is one check fewer (inward-growing stems). The app's code changes in one place for the first.
- **Most faults were in the words, not the advice.** Sentences said more than the pages: "the surest check", "never", "the safest choice", "even for experienced gardeners", grey bark. Two disagreements were hidden: how to remove a sucker, and how far back to cut a crossing stem.
- **The research behind these answers is corrected** (section 3). Most corrections drop a page that does not support a finding, such as a tree-pruning guide, a general shrub page, or a page that never mentions crossing stems. The finding still stands on its other sources.
- **Five sources could not be checked.** Two academic papers block automated reading, and three sources have no web address. No sentence rests on them alone.
- **Not yet checked:** the gates' removal-only part (in batch 3), and the damaged-growth, weak-growth and framework checks (batch 2).
