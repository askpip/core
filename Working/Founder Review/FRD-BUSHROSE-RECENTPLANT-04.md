## Additional Evidence Collected — Source Log

### Q3 — Can a gardener tell grafted from own-root by looking?

15. **Katherine Rowe** (published 29 May 2025, gardening content site Epic Gardening), "5 Signs Your Rose is Reverting to the Rootstock," https://www.epicgardening.com/rose-reverting-to-rootstock/ — "Look for a graft union at the crown. The bud or graft union looks like a knot or swollen point at the base of the plant, just above the roots... A healthy bud union should feel firm." States own-root roses "grow from cuttings with their own original roots" and lack this feature. Also notes: "Your rose likely won't come with a label indicating whether it's grafted or own root," making this visual check the practical method.
16. **David Austin Roses** (recognised commercial rose breeder), "The Anatomy of a Bare Root Rose," https://eu.davidaustinroses.com/blogs/inspiration/the-anatomy-of-a-bare-root-rose-1 — independently corroborates the bud union's appearance: "a knobbly swelling known as the bud union," where "two plants have been joined: the hardy rootstock below and the chosen rose variety above."
17. **Ask Extension / Oregon State University Extension** (Stephen Oldfield, 25 March 2021) — an institutional source confirming bud union placement is climate-dependent: recommended "one inch above soil level" in milder regions (Eugene, Oregon) versus "two inches below the soil" in colder regions (Central Oregon), specifically for winter cold protection. Does not address whether the union stays visible over time once buried, but confirms that whether it is visible at all depends on regional planting practice, not just propagation type.

### Q4 — Is first-year light pruning corroborated?

18. **Homeguides/SFGate** (unattributed), "How to Prune Roses in the First Year," https://homeguides.sfgate.com/prune-roses-first-year-56616.html — "First year roses... don't require heavy pruning, but a light pruning helps keep them healthy and begins to train them to the desired shape." Also: "First-year roses only need mild shaping and not heavy pruning or you may weaken the plant before it's fully established." Recommends removing dead canes and cutting damaged portions to healthy wood as part of that light treatment.
19. **University of Maryland Extension**, "Guide to Pruning Roses," https://extension.umd.edu/resource/guide-pruning-roses — checked specifically for this question; does not address first- or second-year pruning distinctly from established roses at all. Silent, not contradicting.

## Evidence Evaluation

**Q3 (AF-3):** Sources 15 and 16 are independent and agree closely on the bud union's appearance and location, giving reasonable confidence that a visible knot/swelling at the base *is* a grafted rose when present — this is also basic, essentially undisputed rose horticultural anatomy, not a contested claim. But Source 17, an institutional extension source, establishes that visibility is not just a function of propagation type — it depends on regional planting depth, chosen specifically for cold protection in colder climates. This means the *absence* of a visible knot is genuinely ambiguous: it could mean an own-root rose, or a grafted rose whose union was deliberately buried, or one that has become buried over time through mulching or soil buildup. Sources 15/16 are commercial/content-site tier, similar to sources already used elsewhere in this dossier series; Source 17 is extension-tier, the most reliable available for the climate-dependency point specifically.

**Q4 (AF-4):** Sources 18 and 12 (Marin Rose Society, already in -03) are independent and agree: light/mild shaping is acceptable after a rose's first full growing season, heavy pruning is not. Neither source, nor any other checked in this series, defines "light" or "mild" against this MVP's specific six supported observations — there's no way to know from the evidence whether, say, removing a crossing stem counts as "light shaping" or the kind of heavier intervention these sources are cautioning against. Reliability is Low on both counts: neither source is institutional, and University of Maryland Extension (checked specifically) is silent rather than corroborating.

---

## Principal Findings

**AF-3:** A visible knot or swelling at a rose's base reliably indicates a grafted rose when seen. Its absence does not reliably indicate an own-root rose — it may simply mean the union is planted below the visible soil line, a deliberate and climate-dependent practice. This is an asymmetric signal: useful as a positive check, not usable as a negative one.

**AF-4:** Light, mild shaping pruning is generally considered acceptable on a rose that has completed at least one full growing season, distinct from the heavier pruning that should wait longer (AF-1, -03). What counts as "light" is not defined by any source found against this MVP's specific observation set, and is flagged as a design question, not resolved here.

---

## Evidence Assessment

**AF-3 — Evidence Confidence Level: Moderate for the positive signal (visible knot present → grafted); the negative case (no visible knot) does not support an Evidence Confidence Level at all — it is not evidence of own-root, it is an absence of information, per EAS §2.9's requirement that confidence attach to what the evidence actually supports, not to what it fails to rule out.**

**AF-4 — Evidence Confidence Level: Low.** Two independent, non-institutional sources agree on the general principle; no source specifies what "light" means for this MVP's actual observation set, and the one extension source checked was silent rather than corroborating.

## Implications for the Proposed Gate Design (-03)

Two refinements to what -03 proposed, both presented as design implications for Founder decision, not as evidenced findings themselves:

**On propagation type (revisits -03 Decision Point 4):** AF-3's asymmetry actually strengthens the case for keeping V1 propagation-agnostic, for a different reason than -03 gave. It's not just that own-root roses establish only "a little longer" (Hunt County Master Gardeners, -03 Source 14) — it's that a large share of gardeners checking for a bud union and finding none would be in the ambiguous case (buried union, not necessarily own-root), so a gate branch built on this signal would misclassify an unknown number of grafted roses as own-root. Building this into gate *logic* is not recommended on current evidence. **Collecting the graft/own-root question in the plant profile questionnaire anyway is still worthwhile** — Architecture §6.1 already calls for "known rose information and source" as a profile field, this is exactly that kind of information, and it costs nothing to record even if the "recent planting" gate doesn't yet act on it.

**On a three-tier restraint period (revisits -03's two-tier design):** AF-4 suggests the honest picture may be three stages, not two — newly planted this season (dead wood/deadheading only, AF-1/-03), one season to roughly three years (dead wood plus light shaping, AF-4), and three-plus years (the full six-observation set, AF-1/-03) — rather than -03's simpler "dead-wood-only until three years" design. Building this third tier would require deciding which of the MVP's six observations count as "light shaping" and which don't, which no source answers. This is flagged as a live design question, not resolved here.

## Recommendations for Further Research

- If the three-tier design is wanted, KIT/Founder judgement (or further research specifically targeting which pruning actions are "light" versus "heavy" for a young rose) would be needed to assign each of the six MVP observations to a tier — this dossier does not attempt that assignment.
- The bud-union visibility question could be sharpened further by researching how commonly buried unions become re-exposed or stay hidden over multiple years, which would affect how much weight "no visible knot" should carry even as a soft signal — not pursued here, judged low-value relative to the effort given AF-3's already-clear asymmetric-signal conclusion.
