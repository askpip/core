## The findings

Summarised here; full wording is in FRD §7–8. You're not required to rule on each one — use the comment box if something specific needs to change.

**Definitions and relationship (Q1).** Crossing and rubbing are usually treated as one linked pair — contact damages cane tissue, inviting disease. One source excepts tree-form (standard) roses, outside scope.

**Recognising them, and what a photo can tell you (Q2, Q3).** Thin evidence — nothing describes what an actual rubbing wound looks like. No rose source addresses photographic limits at all; the ROC's own synthesis, drawing on general pruning practice and one academic study, is that a single photo has real limits here.

**What to do about it (Q5, Q6).** Strongly supported — the single best-attested finding across the whole research effort: once confirmed, cut it, as routine work, back to its point of origin. Where any source gives a rule for choosing between two crossing canes, it favours the weaker one. No source says which condition wins when a stem is both inward and crossing, but it doesn't matter in practice — both point to the same action.

**Lookalikes and safety (Q4, Q7).** Close to complete gaps, confirmed by real searching, not a failure to search. Given a plain default each rather than left open.
