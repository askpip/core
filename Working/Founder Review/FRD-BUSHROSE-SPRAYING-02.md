## 2. Research Commission Record (Summary)

**Background.** `ARC-BUSHROSE-BASICCARE-01` F6 (High) records the common problems to watch for (blackspot, powdery mildew, aphids, spider mites) and watering at the base; F11 (Moderate) records clearing fallen leaf debris before winter. Neither addresses whether, when or how often to spray. Pip gives no spraying guidance.

**Commissioned questions.**

- **Q1** Do credible sources say an established bush rose needs to be sprayed at all, and what do they say about disease-resistant varieties and growing without spraying?
- **Q2** What do sources say about preventive compared with reactive spraying?
- **Q3** For blackspot, powdery mildew, rust and aphids, what do sources say about managing them, with and without sprays?
- **Q4** What do sources say about winter or dormant clean-up sprays after pruning, their purpose and timing?
- **Q5** What general timing and frequency do sources give through the growing season?
- **Q6** What non-chemical and lower-toxicity options do sources describe?
- **Q7** What safety statements do sources make (label, protective equipment, pollinators, regulation by country)?

**Scope.** Established Hybrid Tea and Floribunda bush roses (Grandiflora provisionally). New Zealand, Australian, United Kingdom and United States sources. Types of spray and active ingredients are recorded as the sources name them. Brand names, mixing rates and dosages are not compiled as findings; the product label and local regulations govern them (Research Commission Record §5, as amended 5 October 2026). Every online source is recorded with its web address and access date (ROC Operations Manual §5.5).

**Method.**

- Web searches were run for each question, including searches restricted to RHS and university extension domains, and searches directed at New Zealand and Australian rose societies, botanic gardens and regulators.
- Pages were read through an automated extraction step that returns text drawn from the page in answer to a stated question; each extraction was asked for exact sentences. Quotations are as returned by that step. For RHS-BS, RHS-CHEM, UCIPM-DIS, UCIPM-INS, UMD-BS, WISC-BS, ARS-FUNG, NZRS-FAQ, ABG-R, GU-DIS, RSNSW, SPR, ROSS-SP, TRE-BS, EPA-NZ, CONS-NZ, GA-PRUNE and SIBB, the quotations relied on were re-checked word for word against a second extraction of the same page.
- Every quotation used is under 25 words and is recorded, by finding, in the Source Register.
- On 5 October 2026, 30 of the 35 pages in §4.1 were opened again and read for the types of spray they name, what each is for and when it is applied. RHS-CHEM, ZLESAK, SIBB, EPA-NZ and CONS-NZ were not reopened. Quotations from that reading are as returned by one extraction, except CLEM-DIS, which was re-checked against a second extraction. The NZRS-W and TUI quotations match those recorded on 1 October word for word.
- Types of spray and active ingredients are recorded as the sources name them. Brand names and mixing rates are not compiled (Research Commission Record §5); where a sentence names a brand, only the part without it is quoted.
- Pages that could not be opened are recorded in §4.3 and support no finding.
- Approved findings in `ARC-BUSHROSE-BASICCARE-01` (F6, F11, F12) were checked for agreement only (§7, final subsection); they were not re-researched.

**Anticipated challenges.** Much rose-specific spraying guidance is published by nurseries and garden-product companies. Institutional sources differ in their overall stance on pesticides. Regulatory pages are general rather than rose-specific.

## 5. Evidence Evaluation

The evaluation applies the EAS factors (Chapter 4) to each finding individually in §8. The general position across the body of evidence is as follows.

**Relevance.** All sources in §4.1 address roses directly except RHS-CHEM, EPA-NZ and CONS-NZ, which address garden pesticide use in general (relevant to Q7).

**Source reliability.** The RHS, six US university extension services, the New Zealand Rose Society, the Rose Society of NSW, Auckland Botanic Gardens, the New Zealand EPA and two peer-reviewed papers are recognised authorities. Broadcaster pieces report presenters' practice. Commercial sources are given less weight as independent authority.

**Consistency.** Sources agree strongly on sanitation, watering at the base, air movement, choosing resistant varieties, following the label, protecting pollinators, and gentle first measures for aphids. They disagree on whether to spray fungicides at all, on preventive versus reactive spraying, on how long resistance lasts, and on overhead watering for powdery mildew. Winter clean-up spraying is described by New Zealand and Australian sources and not by the UK and US institutional pages consulted. On the types of spray, New Zealand and Australian sources name lime sulphur, copper and oil for winter; US extension services name oils, sulfur, potassium bicarbonate, copper and synthetic fungicides for the growing season; the RHS names none.

**Independence.** Multi-page organisations and the Auckland Botanic Gardens group are counted once each (§4.2).

**Limitations.** All page content was read through an extraction step. Two Australian sources (APVMA, Rose Society of Queensland) could not be opened. Most winter-spray detail comes from broadcasters and companies. The quotations added on 5 October, other than CLEM-DIS, rest on one extraction of each page.

## 6. Managing Conflicting Evidence and Uncertainty

**C-1 — Whether to use fungicides at all (AF-4, AF-5, AF-6).** RHS-BS, RHS-PM and RHS-RUST: "The RHS recommends that you don't use fungicides", because they "may reduce biodiversity, impact soil health and have wider adverse environmental effects" (RHS-BS). RHS-CHEM: treatment with chemicals "is rarely necessary". Against this, ARS-FUNG: "Keeping susceptible roses healthy requires some application of fungicides, especially when conditions support disease development"; UMD-BS: "Most people will need to use labeled fungicide sprays every 7-14 days"; RSNSW recommends regular spraying. The positions may reflect differing climates, varieties and goals (a garden display versus show-quality foliage), but no source reconciles them. Unresolved.

**C-2 — Preventive (regular) spraying versus reactive or minimal spraying (AF-7, AF-9, AF-13).** SPR describes both: spray "regularly (every two weeks or so)", which it calls "the most effective way to keep roses looking good all season long", though "more time consuming and costly"; or spray "when they are attacked by a particular pest or disease". RSNSW and UMD-BS describe regular spraying through the season. ROSS-SP: spraying "should only occur minimally and when the problems are really bad". WISC-BS ties preventive spraying to roses with "a history of severe black spot" and a wet season. UCIPM-DIS says keeping roses rust-free may need frequent applications "which may not be justifiable". Unresolved.

**C-3 — How long disease resistance lasts (AF-2, AF-3).** RHS-BS: resistance in new cultivars "usually does not last". RHS-PM: it "may not persist for the lifetime of the plant". UMD-BS: resistance "can be regionally variable". Against this, NZRS-FAQ, ABG-R and SIBB describe roses grown well without spraying through variety choice, SIBB over two decades in Auckland. The RHS statements concern resistance claims for new cultivars generally; the Auckland account concerns cultivars selected by trial in one place. Unresolved.

**C-4 — Overhead watering (AF-17, AF-18).** Black spot sources say keep leaves dry: WISC-BS "DO NOT overhead water"; GA-BS "never water the foliage". For powdery mildew, RHS-PM says overhead watering in mid-morning in dry weather "can reduce powdery mildew", and UCIPM-DIS that midday sprinkling "may limit the disease". RHS-PM itself adds that leaves remaining wet for long periods "may favour other diseases". The difference reflects the two diseases' different needs. Preserved as two findings.

**C-5 — Whether a winter clean-up spray is part of rose care (AF-25, AF-29, AF-45).** NZRS-W, GA-LOOK, GA-PRUNE, GA-RP, ROSS-SP, SPR, TRE-BS and TUI describe one. UCIPM-DIS mentions no dormant spray (checked directly) and recommends dormant-season sanitation; CLEM-DIS says copper fungicides "can be used on dormant bushes", under botrytis blight, and describes no spray after pruning (checked directly); the RHS recommends against fungicides generally; UMD-BS and WISC-BS describe in-season sprays only. No source compares outcomes. Unresolved.

**C-6 — Order and timing of winter sprays (AF-28, AF-41).** NZRS-W: clean-up sprays "in June", with "a post prune spray of copper and oil", and lime sulphur and copper not "within 2 weeks of each other". ROSS-SP: two products after July pruning. SPR: lime sulphur a few days after pruning, oil two weeks later, copper "At first sign of new spring growth". TUI: copper and oil "through winter and into early spring". EUTREMA: lime sulphur in dormancy, after leaf fall. SPR adds that lime sulphur and oil "must never be mixed or spayed together" (AF-41). Unresolved.

**C-7 — Repeat interval (AF-9).** TRE-BS: weekly for an organic spray. UMD-BS, WISC-BS and ARS-FUNG: seven to 14 days. RSNSW: "approximately every 15 days". SPR: "every two weeks or so". SIBB records that Auckland Botanic Gardens formerly sprayed "at two- to three-week intervals". RNZIH told a gardener spraying every 7–9 days "you might be doing a bit too much". ARS-FUNG: "The product label will provide direction on application frequency." Unresolved; the sources agree the label governs.

**C-8 — Home-made sprays (AF-44).** GA-BS gives a baking soda mix and says: "This makes an excellent and inexpensive fungicide." CLEM-DIS lists "baking soda mixed with horticultural oil" for powdery mildew. Against this, RHS-PM: "Homemade products are not recommended as they are unregulated and usually untested." RNZIH, of milk and baking-soda sprays: "really just a safer alternative rather than a panacea". Unresolved.

**Uncertainty preserved.** Whether winter clean-up sprays make a measurable difference, which products are currently approved for home use in each of Pip's four markets, and how much spraying need differs between New Zealand regions are not answered by the evidence (§4.3, §9).

## 7. Principal Findings

Each finding is one claim. Brand names and mixing rates given by sources are not reproduced.

### Whether a rose needs spraying (Q1)

**AF-1 (established).** Roses can be grown well with little or no spraying, including in New Zealand. NZRS-FAQ, asked "Can I grow roses without spraying them?", answers: "Yes you can. Grow varieties that are very disease tolerant." ABG-R: "We only grow those that perform well and stay healthy without spraying." SIBB: "Miticides, insecticides and fungicides are not used." NZRS-ABG: "No spraying is done at the gardens". UCIPM-DIS: "many gardeners can grow plants with little to no use of fungicides, especially in California's dry interior valleys." UCIPM-INS: enthusiasts "produce high quality blooms with little or no use of insecticides." ZLESAK: the Earth-Kind trials were "conducted without fertilizers or pesticides and greatly reduced irrigation".

**AF-2 (established).** Choosing disease-resistant or disease-tolerant varieties is a principal way to reduce disease. NZRS-FAQ: "Grow varieties that are very disease tolerant." UMD-PROB: "Seek and select disease-resistant varieties of roses (very important!)." WISC-BS: select varieties "that have a high level of black spot resistance." UCIPM-BS: "Choose disease-resistant varieties when planting." RHS-PM: "There are considerable differences in susceptibility between rose cultivars". RNZIH: "some of the older varieties are very susceptible to rust and/or black spot." GU-DIS: newer varieties have "great tolerance to the common diseases". TRE-BS points to varieties "recommended by the rose society in your area." Also UMD-BS, UCIPM-DIS, CLEM-DIS (names resistant Hybrid Tea and Floribunda cultivars), RHS-RUST, ZLESAK.

**AF-3 (supported interpretation).** Claimed resistance may not last, and may vary by region. RHS-BS: new resistant cultivars give "a few years' respite", but "this usually does not last." RHS-PM: claimed resistance "may not persist for the lifetime of the plant, or be effective in all localities". UMD-BS: "resistance can be regionally variable." ZLESAK: "Race-specific resistance was generally more prevalent in newer rose cultivars" (C-3).

**AF-4 (supported interpretation).** Many popular Hybrid Tea and Floribunda roses are susceptible, and some sources say susceptible roses need regular fungicide to stay healthy. RHS-BS: "Popular garden cultivars of hybrid teas, floribundas, climbers and patio types are often susceptible." ARS-FUNG: "Keeping susceptible roses healthy requires some application of fungicides, especially when conditions support disease development". UMD-BS: "Most people will need to use labeled fungicide sprays every 7-14 days". RSNSW: regular spraying "will help to prevent the build-up of pests and diseases" (C-1).

**AF-5 (established).** The RHS recommends that gardeners don't use fungicides on roses. RHS-BS, RHS-PM and RHS-RUST each state: "The RHS recommends that you don't use fungicides". RHS-BS gives the reason that fungicides, "including organic types", "may reduce biodiversity, impact soil health and have wider adverse environmental effects." This is one organisation's position (C-1).

**AF-6 (established).** Several sources treat pesticides as a last resort, after non-chemical measures. RHS-CHEM: "Treatment with chemicals is rarely necessary – non-chemical controls are usually effective and sufficient", and chemicals "should be used only as a last resort, in a minimal, highly targeted way". UMD-PROB: "Use a pesticide only as a last resort and apply with care." CLEM-INS: "Whenever possible, initially try physical control measures." UCIPM-INS: "Use of broadly toxic insecticides is not warranted in most gardens and landscapes." ROSS-SP: "Spraying of roses should only occur minimally and when the problems are really bad."

### Preventive or reactive, and when (Q2, Q5)

**AF-7 (established).** Fungicides for rose leaf diseases work by protecting leaves before infection, not by curing it. UCIPM-DIS: "generally you must apply them to prevent rather than eradicate infections, so timing is critical". ARS-FUNG: most fungicides are applied "before disease occurs or at the first appearance of symptoms". PSU-RD: "Apply a fungicide to protect new foliage." UMD-BS: "It is best to spray before a rain event to protect the leaves". WISC-BS: "consider applying preventative fungicide treatments". SPR: spray regularly "to prevent disease from taking hold". RSNSW: spraying "will help slow down the disease and/or help prevent it occurring."

**AF-8 (supported interpretation).** Black spot is the most common rose fungal disease and depends on leaves staying wet. RSNSW: "It is the most commonly occurring fungal disease on roses." UCIPM-BS: "leaves must remain wet for more than about 7 hours." GU-DIS: "Mild temperatures and wet leaf surfaces encourage black spot infection".

**AF-9 (supported interpretation).** Where regular spraying is described, the stated repeat interval is about one to two weeks, and the label sets it. UMD-BS: "every 7-14 days". WISC-BS: "every seven to 14 days". ARS-FUNG: contact fungicides last "seven to 14 days". RSNSW: "approximately every 15 days". SPR: "every two weeks or so". TRE-BS: an organic spray "will need to be repeated once a week". ARS-FUNG: "The product label will provide direction on application frequency." (C-7)

**AF-10 (supported interpretation).** Where regular spraying is described, it runs from the first new leaves after winter through the growing season. RSNSW: "from the time they start to produce healthy foliage after the winter prune and up until the end of the growing season." UMD-BS: "as the first leaves emerge in the spring through the fall". WISC-BS: "from bud break until wet weather subsides." SIBB records Auckland Botanic Gardens' former programme "from September to May".

**AF-11 (supported interpretation).** Spraying need and frequency rise with wet weather, a history of disease, or a heavy outbreak. WISC-BS: preventive spraying where "your rose has a history of severe black spot, and the weather for the growing season is predicted to be wet". ARS-FUNG: "especially when conditions support disease development". RSNSW: "If the rose bushes get a big infestation of diseases or pests, they may need to be sprayed more frequently."

**AF-12 (established).** Sources that spray advise alternating or rotating fungicides to slow resistance. UMD-BS: "alternate types of fungicides." WISC-BS: "DO NOT use the same active ingredient for all treatments." ARS-FUNG: rotation is "to delay the development of the resistance to chemical treatments". GU-DIS: "use different sprays if you use them to manage resistance building up".

**AF-13 (expert opinion, limited).** Trying to keep roses completely disease-free can mean frequent spraying that may not be justified. UCIPM-DIS: keeping plants rust-free "may require frequent applications, which may not be justifiable". RNZIH, to a gardener spraying every 7–9 days: "you might be doing a bit too much". SPR calls regular preventive spraying "more time consuming and costly".

### The common targets, with and without sprays (Q3, Q6)

**AF-14 (established).** Removing fallen leaves and infected stems, especially before and during winter, reduces disease the next season. RHS-BS: "Collect and destroy fallen leaves in the autumn, or bury under a layer of mulch." RHS-RUST: to "reduce the number of overwintering resting spores". UCIPM-DIS: "Remove fallen leaves and other infested material, and prune out infected stems during the dormant season." UMD-BS: this "will dramatically slow initial spring infections." CLEM-DIS: "In the fall or winter, remove all old leaves on the ground along with any mulch where infected leaves have fallen." NZRS-W: fallen leaves "can harbour spores that spread disease later on." GU-DIS: "hence the need to pick up all fallen leaves during winter". Also ABG-R, WISC-BS. This agrees with and extends `ARC-BUSHROSE-BASICCARE-01` F11.

**AF-15 (established).** Infected leaves and shoots are removed during the season as soon as they are seen. RHS-PM: badly affected shoots "are best pruned out and disposed of as soon as the symptoms are seen". RHS-RUST: "Prune out spring infections as soon as they are detected". RHS-BS: "Prune out all stem lesions in spring before leaves appear." CLEM-DIS: "Remove and destroy diseased leaves and canes during the growing season." GA-BS: "pick off any black spot affected leaves". TUI: "Pick off infected leaves as you see them, not always practical but it does help prevent the spread." RNZIH: "Removing diseased leaves and dead flowers is definitely a good idea".

**AF-16 (supported interpretation).** Diseased leaves and prunings are disposed of, not composted. GA-RP: "don't put them in the compost, put them in your council bin". TUI: disposing "by burning or throwing in the rubbish and not composting". GA-BS: "put them in a plastic bag and tie the top tightly". NZRS-W: "Rake up all fallen leaves and burn them". (RHS-BS alternatively allows burying fallen leaves under mulch.)

**AF-17 (established).** Watering at the base and keeping leaves dry reduces leaf disease. WISC-BS: "DO NOT overhead water (e.g., with a sprinkler)." CLEM-DIS: "It is best not to water plants with overhead irrigation". GA-BS: "Always water around the roots of the rose and give it a good soaking." GU-DIS: "where possible, avoid overhead irrigation." PSU-RD: "Water in a manner that keeps foliage surfaces dry." UCIPM-BS: irrigate "in the morning instead of the evening or night." UMD-BS: "Restrict irrigation during cloudy, humid weather." This agrees with `ARC-BUSHROSE-BASICCARE-01` F6.

**AF-18 (supported interpretation).** Powdery mildew is an exception: water on the leaves can reduce it. RHS-PM: "Overhead watering (in mid-morning) in dry weather can reduce powdery mildew". UCIPM-DIS: midday sprinkling "may limit the disease by disrupting the daily spore-release cycle". CLEM-DIS: "A film of water inhibits infection". RHS-PM adds that long-wet leaves "may favour other diseases" (C-4).

**AF-19 (established).** Sun, spacing and air movement reduce leaf disease. WISC-BS: "Plant roses in a high light environment, and space shrubs far apart." UMD-BS: "Good air circulation will reduce the incidence of black spot by promoting faster drying of leaf surfaces." CLEM-DIS: "Space new plants far enough apart to allow for good air circulation." UCIPM-DIS: "Provide good air circulation around plants." GU-DIS: "make sure there is some air movement around your plants." GA-BS: "open up the rose bush to create more air movement through the middle". GA-RP: pruning gives "space for good air circulation which will minimise fungal diseases next year". NZRS-FAQ: "a spot that gets full sun."

**AF-20 (supported interpretation).** Well-watered, well-fed roses are described as better at resisting disease. NZRS-FAQ: "Water and fertilise them well and you can grow good roses with minimal disease problems." GA-BS: "the better fed they are, the more likely they are to resist disease". ABG-R: "apply fertiliser in spring to help keep plants healthy". TRE-BS: "A happy rose is a healthy rose." None gives a mechanism or measure.

**AF-21 (established).** Lower-toxicity fungicide types (oils including neem oil, sulfur and potassium bicarbonate) are named for black spot and powdery mildew. UCIPM-DIS: "Oils, including neem oil, or other fungicides such as potassium bicarbonate and sulfur, have been shown to be effective." UCIPM-BS lists neem oil, potassium bicarbonate and sulfur among "preventive fungicides". CLEM-DIS, for powdery mildew, lists "sulfur, neem oil (clarified hydrophobic extract), or baking soda mixed with horticultural oil". WISC-BS: "Neem oil is another organic product (in addition to copper sulfate) that has been shown to provide some black spot control." RHS-BS's recommendation against fungicides covers "organic types" too (AF-5).

**AF-22 (established).** Aphids: tolerate low numbers, rely on natural enemies, squash or hose them off. RHS-APH: "Where possible tolerate populations of aphids", encourage predators, and "Use finger and thumb to squash aphid colonies where practical". UCIPM-INS: "Low to moderate levels of aphids do little damage to plants", and knocking them off with water "early in the day is all that is needed to supplement natural control." CLEM-INS: "Aphids can be hosed off with a strong stream of water directed above and below the leaves." UMD-PROB: "Aphids have many natural enemies (predators) like ladybugs and lacewings". ROSS-SP: "just a jet from a hose".

**AF-23 (supported interpretation).** Insecticidal soaps and horticultural oils are described as lower-impact aphid sprays. UCIPM-INS: they "can also be used to suppress aphids with only moderate impact on natural enemies." CLEM-INS: "Insecticidal soap must be sprayed onto the aphids to be effective." ROSS-SP: soapy sprays and oils "can all reduce the numbers enough to let natural predators control remaining insects".

**AF-24 (established).** Broad-spectrum insecticides harm natural enemies and can lead to repeat pest outbreaks. CLEM-INS: "these insecticides kill the natural enemies of rose aphids." UMD-PROB: choose measures that "minimize impacts on natural enemies, or else secondary (recurring) pest outbreaks can be problematic." UCIPM-INS: soil-applied systemic insecticides "are not usually necessary and may harm pollinators and natural enemies."

**AF-39 (supported interpretation).** Copper is among the fungicides sources name for black spot in the growing season. CLEM-DIS, for black spot severe enough to warrant control: "chlorothalonil, mancozeb, myclobutanil, propiconazole, or copper fungicides". WISC-BS: "Fungicides containing chlorothalonil, copper sulfate, and myclobutanil can be highly effective in controlling black spot." GA-BS: "Or use Bordeaux mixture or one of the other copper based fungicides." UCIPM-DIS and UCIPM-BS don't list copper for black spot. A gardener writing to RNZIH had used copper oxychloride and still reported "severe rust" in wet weather.

**AF-40 (established).** US university extension services name synthetic fungicides for black spot, most often chlorothalonil. UCIPM-BS: "available preventive fungicides include neem oil, potassium bicarbonate, sulfur, and the synthetic fungicides triforine and chlorothalonil". CLEM-DIS and WISC-BS name chlorothalonil and myclobutanil (quoted at AF-39); CLEM-DIS also names mancozeb and propiconazole. One Australian nursery, ROSS-SP, names triforine and mancozeb. Which of these may be sold and used differs by country (AF-34).

**AF-42 (expert opinion, single source).** One US extension service says oils are not applied to a plant short of water, or within two weeks of a sulfur spray. UCIPM-DIS: "Do not apply oils to water-stressed plants or within two weeks of a sulfur spray."

**AF-44 (supported interpretation).** Sources disagree about home-made sprays such as a baking soda mix. GA-BS gives a baking soda mix and says: "This makes an excellent and inexpensive fungicide." CLEM-DIS lists "baking soda mixed with horticultural oil" for powdery mildew. RHS-PM: "Homemade products are not recommended as they are unregulated and usually untested." RNZIH, of milk and baking-soda sprays: "really just a safer alternative rather than a panacea" (C-8).

### Winter clean-up sprays after pruning (Q4)

**AF-25 (supported interpretation).** New Zealand and Australian rose sources describe a winter clean-up spray after pruning. NZRS-W: "it is highly recommended that clean up sprays be applied in June", with "a post prune spray of copper and oil". GA-RP: "You might also like to spray with lime sulphur to clean up any fungal spores." GA-PRUNE: "After pruning, spray for protection." GA-LOOK: "Winter is the best time to combat fungal pests and other diseases." ROSS-SP: "After pruning in July it is good practise to spray the plants with two products to help control problems in spring." TRE-BS, SPR and TUI describe the same. The wording ranges from "highly recommended" (NZRS-W) to "You might also like to" (GA-RP).

**AF-26 (supported interpretation).** That winter spray is applied after pruning and after prunings and leaves are cleared, to the plant and the ground. GA-LOOK: "collect and dispose of dead or dying leaves and pruned branches. Next, spray the plants - and the ground around them". TRE-BS: "When winter pruning has been done and all waste removed, spray with Lime sulphur on the plant and ground around." SPR: "Prune roses first".

**AF-27 (expert opinion, limited).** The stated purpose of the winter spray is to clean up fungal spores and overwintering mites and insects. GA-RP: "to clean up any fungal spores". GA-PRUNE: lime sulphur "will kill mites and other insects" and is "also a fungicide that will kill black spot." No source measures the effect.

**AF-28 (expert opinion, limited).** Sources differ on the order and spacing of winter sprays; one rose society says lime sulphur and copper are kept two weeks apart. NZRS-W: "you must not use lime sulphur and copper based sprays within 2 weeks of each other." SPR spaces its winter sprays and applies copper "At first sign of new spring growth". EUTREMA: lime sulphur "during the dormant season when the rose plants have shed their leaves". (C-6)

**AF-29 (unresolved question).** The UK and US institutional pages consulted don't describe a winter spray after pruning. UCIPM-DIS was checked directly and mentions none; its dormant-season advice is sanitation: "Remove fallen leaves and other infested material, and prune out infected stems during the dormant season." CLEM-DIS was checked directly: it doesn't mention lime sulfur or a spray after pruning, though it says copper can be used on dormant bushes (AF-45). The RHS pages recommend against fungicides (AF-5), and UMD-BS and WISC-BS describe in-season spraying only. This is an absence across the pages opened, not a statement that winter sprays don't work (C-5).

**AF-36 (supported interpretation).** Copper, often with a spraying oil, is named as a winter spray after pruning by New Zealand and Australian rose sources. NZRS-W: "Don't forget a post prune spray of copper and oil to complete the job." TUI: "Protectant sprays such as a copper based spray and plant spraying oil are effective as clean up sprays through winter and into early spring". SPR: "At first sign of new spring growth spray one application of copper oxychloride". ROSS-SP: "Copper Oxy Chloride, Lime Sulphur or Boudreaux sprays can be used in winter after pruning". No source measures the effect.

**AF-37 (supported interpretation).** Lime sulphur is named as a winter spray after pruning by New Zealand and Australian rose sources. NZRS-W gives "Lime Sulphur at winter strength". SPR: "Spray 1 application of Lime Sulphur a fews days after pruning". TRE-BS: "spray with Lime sulphur on the plant and ground around." GA-RP: "You might also like to spray with lime sulphur to clean up any fungal spores." GA-PRUNE: "I'm using lime sulphur, which will kill mites and other insects." GA-LOOK: "with an organic lime-sulphur mix". ROSS-SP names it with copper (quoted at AF-36). EUTREMA, a UK company, describes the same use in the dormant season (quoted at AF-28).

**AF-38 (supported interpretation).** A spraying oil is used in winter, with or after the other winter sprays. NZRS-W and TUI name oil with copper (quoted at AF-36). SPR applies a spraying oil two weeks after the lime sulphur. ROSS-SP: a second spray of oil "is used to coat insect eggs (mainly aphid) and kill them." Only ROSS-SP states what the oil is for.

**AF-41 (expert opinion, single source).** One New Zealand rose nursery says lime sulphur and oil must never be mixed or sprayed together. SPR: "Lime Sulphur and oil must never be mixed or spayed together." (as written on the page)

**AF-43 (expert opinion, single source).** One New Zealand rose nursery says copper washes off in rain and may need reapplying. SPR: "Copper washes off easily and may need to be reapplied after rain."

**AF-45 (expert opinion, single source).** One US extension service says copper fungicides can be used on dormant bushes. CLEM-DIS, under botrytis blight: "Copper fungicides can be used on dormant bushes." It describes no spray after pruning (AF-29).

### Safety (Q7)

**AF-30 (established).** Product choice, rates and frequency follow the product label; in the UK, following the label is a legal requirement. RHS-CHEM: "Adhering to the label instructions is a legal requirement." RSNSW: "It is important to carefully read the instructions on the packet of the products you are going to use". WISC-BS: "Be sure to read and follow all label instructions". CLEM-DIS: "Apply all chemicals according to the directions on the label". ARS-FUNG: the label "will provide direction on application frequency." ROSS-SP: "reading instructions is always the first step prior to any use." TRE-BS: "read the instructions on the back of the packaging". CONS-NZ: "make sure you follow the instructions on the label". EPA-NZ: "Label information is printed on all insecticides".

**AF-31 (expert opinion, limited).** Protective equipment and sprayer cleaning are each stated by few sources. RHS-CHEM: "Wearing rubber gloves when handling and applying pesticides is a sensible precaution." ROSS-SP: "Safety equipment should be used even in low toxicity chemicals", and cleaning the sprayer after use is "vital to avoid cross contamination".

**AF-32 (established).** Don't spray open flowers or when bees are foraging; one New Zealand source says spray only in the evening. RHS-CHEM: "Open blooms must not be sprayed." UMD-PROB: "Fungicides may harm pollinators or other organisms, so use them with caution and do not spray open flowers." EPA-NZ: "Avoid spraying budding or flowering plants", and no spraying "when bees are foraging". CONS-NZ: "Spray only in the evening after bees have returned to their hives". UCIPM-INS: soil-applied systemic insecticides "may harm pollinators".

**AF-33 (expert opinion, limited).** Two nurseries say not to spray in hot conditions. TRE-BS: "Never in the heat of the day." ROSS-SP: winter sprays "when the temperature is less than 25 degrees".

**AF-34 (supported interpretation).** Which products may be sold and used is set by each country's regulator, and approvals change. RHS-CHEM: "It is illegal to sell unapproved products for use as pesticides", "Some chemicals previously approved and sold to home gardeners have been withdrawn", and the HSE database shows whether a product "is authorised for use in the UK." EPA-NZ states New Zealand rules, including that "neonicotinoids must not be used on flowering crops". No Australian or US regulator page was read (§4.3).

**AF-35 (expert opinion, single source).** One New Zealand source says to ask a local rose society or garden centre before spraying. GU-DIS: "If you do need to spray to control things, consult with your local rose society or garden centre/hardware store for recommendations." TRE-BS similarly points to varieties "recommended by the rose society in your area" (AF-2).

## 9. Recommendations for Further Research

Each recommendation could not be resolved within the commission's scope and authority, and states what a Founder decision would need to settle before it could be pursued (ROC Operations Manual §7.7). Until one is pursued, the Pip Knowledge Rules decide what Pip says, as shown.

**R-1. Which spray types are currently approved for home-garden use on roses in New Zealand, Australia, the UK and the US.**
Approvals are a regulatory matter that changes over time and differs by country (AF-34). The Australian regulator's page could not be opened, no source stated the current UK status of lime sulphur or copper products for home gardeners, and the RHS document listing the fungicides available to UK home gardeners was not opened (§4.3). Settling it needs a check of each regulator's register, which is outside a horticultural evidence commission.
*To pursue it:* a Founder decision to commission a regulatory check.
*Until then:* Pip names types as the sources do, never a brand or an amount. It tells the gardener to use only a product labelled for roses where they live, and to follow its label (rule 7).

**R-2. Whether a winter clean-up spray after pruning makes a measurable difference.**
Eight New Zealand and Australian sources describe it (AF-25), but no trial or institutional source was found that tests it, and the UK and US institutional pages consulted don't describe a spray after pruning (AF-29). Answering it needs trial evidence or specialist consultation.
*To pursue it:* a Founder decision to seek specialist advice, for example from the New Zealand Rose Society.
*Until then:* Pip says that New Zealand and Australian rose sources describe it, and that no source was found that tests it (rules 1, 4 and 5).

**R-3. Which disease-tolerant varieties suit New Zealand conditions.**
The New Zealand Rose Society and Auckland Botanic Gardens select varieties by local performance (AF-1, AF-2), but variety lists change and resistance varies by region (AF-3). Compiling a current list needs direct enquiry.
*To pursue it:* a Founder decision to make that enquiry.
*Until then:* Pip says no source was found, names no variety, and points the gardener to a local rose society (rule 5).

**R-4. How spraying need differs between New Zealand regions.**
Sources tie disease to wet, humid weather (AF-8, AF-11), and UC IPM ties low-spray growing to dry climates. No New Zealand source compares regions.
*To pursue it:* a Founder decision to commission it.
*Until then:* Pip says no source compares regions, and that sources tie leaf disease to wet, humid weather (rule 5; AF-8, AF-11).

**R-5. The ROC's own reasoned synthesis, recorded as such and assigned Not Assigned (EAS §2.10).**
These are questions arising from the findings that the evidence does not itself state. They are not findings and are not proposed for adoption.

- R-5a. Whether a beginner who follows only the non-chemical measures (AF-14 to AF-20) gets most of the benefit that a spraying programme gives, on a typical Hybrid Tea or Floribunda.
- R-5b. Whether a gardener can tell from their own rose's history whether it is a susceptible variety (AF-4), and so whether spraying is worth considering.
- R-5c. Whether winter clean-up of fallen leaves (AF-14) does much of the work sources attribute to the winter spray (AF-27).
- R-5d. Whether beginners can follow a repeat-spraying programme safely and consistently, given label, pollinator and heat precautions (AF-30 to AF-33).

*To pursue it:* a Founder decision to commission any of them.
*Until then:* none is used (rule 8).

**R-6. Sprays for pests outside the commissioned questions.**
CLEM-INS and UCIPM-INS name sprays for spider mites, rose scale, thrips, caterpillars, beetles and other pests. The commissioned questions cover black spot, powdery mildew, rust and aphids only, so these were not compiled.
*To pursue it:* a Founder decision to commission those pests.
*Until then:* Pip says nothing about spraying for them from this record.

## 10. Founder Decision Points

### (a) What the rules give

For each commissioned question, this is what Pip would say under the Pip Knowledge Rules. Each statement comes from the findings named and adds nothing to them. Pip presents each as what sources say, with its Evidence Confidence Level, and names the sources.

**Q1. Does a rose need spraying at all?**

- Roses can be grown well with little or no spraying, including in New Zealand (AF-1, High).
- Choosing a disease-tolerant variety is a main way to reduce disease (AF-2, High). Claimed resistance may not last, and may vary by region (AF-3, Moderate).
- Many popular Hybrid Teas and Floribundas are susceptible (AF-4, Moderate).
- Several sources treat pesticides as a last resort (AF-6, High).
- Sources disagree (C-1). The better-supported view is the one above. Against it, an American Rose Society article, the University of Maryland Extension and the Rose Society of NSW say susceptible roses need regular fungicide (AF-4, Moderate). The RHS recommends that gardeners don't use fungicides on roses at all (AF-5, High, as one organisation's position). Pip leads with the better-supported view and says who disagrees (rule 2).
- No source was found on which tolerant varieties suit New Zealand. Pip says so and points the gardener to a local rose society (rule 5; R-3).

**Q2. Spray regularly to prevent, or only when a problem appears?**

- Fungicides protect leaves before infection. They don't cure it (AF-7, High).
- Sources disagree (C-2), and neither view is clearly better supported. Pip gives both and leads with neither (rule 2).

**Q3 and Q6. The common problems, with and without sprays**

- Without sprays: clear fallen leaves and infected stems, especially before and over winter (AF-14, Very High); remove infected leaves and shoots when seen (AF-15, High); put them in the rubbish, not the compost (AF-16, Moderate); water at the base and keep leaves dry (AF-17, High), with powdery mildew the exception (AF-18, Moderate); sun, spacing and air movement (AF-19, High); a well-watered, well-fed rose (AF-20, Moderate). Black spot is the most common fungal disease and needs wet leaves (AF-8, Moderate).
- The sprays sources name for leaf disease:
  - oils including neem oil, sulfur and potassium bicarbonate, named by three US universities (AF-21, High);
  - copper, named for black spot by two US universities and ABC Gardening Australia (AF-39, Moderate);
  - synthetic fungicides, most often chlorothalonil, named by three US universities (AF-40, High). Pip says these are US sources, and that the research did not establish which are approved for home gardeners elsewhere (R-1).
- Home-made sprays: sources disagree, and neither view is clearly better supported. Clemson and ABC Gardening Australia suggest a baking soda mix; the RHS says home-made products are not recommended because they are unregulated and usually untested. Pip gives both views (AF-44, Moderate; rule 2). Pip gives no recipe.
- The RHS recommends not using fungicides, organic types included (AF-5).
- Aphids: tolerate low numbers, squash or hose them off, rely on natural enemies (AF-22, High). Insecticidal soaps and horticultural oils are lower-impact sprays (AF-23, Moderate). Broad-spectrum insecticides harm natural enemies and can bring pests back (AF-24, High).

**Q4. Winter sprays after pruning**

- Shown to gardeners in New Zealand and Australia only (rule 4):
  - rose sources there describe a winter clean-up spray after pruning (AF-25, Moderate), put on after prunings and fallen leaves are cleared, onto the plant and the ground (AF-26, Moderate);
  - they name lime sulphur (AF-37, Moderate), copper, often with a spraying oil (AF-36, Moderate), and a spraying oil (AF-38, Moderate);
  - no source was found that tests whether a winter spray makes a difference. Pip says so (rule 5; R-2).
- Gardeners in the UK and the US: the institutional pages consulted don't describe a spray after pruning. Pip says so and gives the winter clear-up (AF-29; rule 5). For gardeners in the US, Clemson says copper fungicides can be used on dormant bushes; Pip says only one source was found (AF-45; rule 4).

**Q5. When and how often, if a gardener chooses to spray**

- Where sources describe regular spraying, it runs from the first new leaves after winter through the growing season (AF-10, Moderate), with more in wet weather, where there is a history of disease, or in a heavy outbreak (AF-11, Moderate).
- Sources give repeat intervals from weekly to about every two weeks (C-7). Pip gives the range and says the product label sets the interval (AF-9, Moderate; rules 3 and 7).
- Sources that spray advise alternating products, to slow resistance (AF-12, High).

**Q7. Safety**

- Follow the product label for which product, how much and how often. In the UK this is a legal requirement (AF-30, Very High).
- Don't spray open flowers or when bees are foraging (AF-32, High).
- Which products may be sold and used is set by each country's regulator, and approvals change (AF-34, Moderate).
- Precautions, each included under rule 7: wear gloves and safety equipment and clean the sprayer (AF-31); don't spray in the heat (AF-33); don't use lime sulphur and copper within two weeks of each other (AF-28); don't mix lime sulphur and oil or spray them together (AF-41); don't put oil on a plant short of water, or within two weeks of a sulfur spray (AF-42).

**Kept on record only:** AF-13, AF-27, AF-35 and AF-43. **Never used:** R-5.

**Not said by Pip:** brand names and mixing amounts, including the amounts in a home-made mix (Research Commission Record §5).

**Standing decisions applied (Pip Knowledge Rules §7):** Pip tells gardeners where its information comes from. Pip works out where a gardener is from the device's location where the gardener allows it, and otherwise from the place given for that plant in its journal entry; that decides who is shown the New Zealand, Australian and US material above.
