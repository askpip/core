## The findings

Summarised here; full wording is in FRD §7–8. You're not required to rule on each one — use the comment box if something specific needs to change.

**What it is, and what causes it (Q1).** Damaged growth is mostly treated as its own category, separate from dead wood. Several causes are named: frost, mechanical breakage, canker, pests, sunscald. Weak/thin growth keeps showing up as a separate category too, confirming it's right to research it separately later.

**Recognising it (Q2).** Canker has the strongest, most consistent evidence in this dossier — five independent sources agree on the same visible signs. Frost damage shows visually but needs a cut-and-check to confirm. Hidden splits have no non-destructive test. Pest damage shows as wilting/entry holes, with a useful catch that one commonly-cited source is actually about a harmless wasp, not a real pest.

**Photos and lookalikes (Q3, Q4).** A photo can show colour and shape but not what's happening inside the cane. Harmless galls are a documented lookalike for damage. Canker's early colour genuinely overlaps with healthy new growth — real enough that an expert once declined to diagnose a case from photos alone.

**What to do about it (Q5, Q6).** Near-unanimous: cut to healthy tissue, checked by pith colour. How far back depends on severity, not cause — this is the one place this dossier's rule differs from the project's prior "always remove fully" pattern. Frost damage often needs a wait-and-reassess approach, sometimes with a second pruning pass later.

**Safety (Q7).** Tool disinfection for canker/disease-suspect wood, and burning/trashing (not composting) disease prunings, are both well supported. Handling broken/tensioned canes has no rose-specific guidance — only a general, clearly-labelled caution borrowed from a much bigger-scale context.

## Where the sources differ, and the defaults adopted

Three points of variation are on record (Brief §6): whether "damaged" is always kept distinct from "dead" in wording (mostly, some sources are just terser); what "wait for spring" means for frost damage (two subtly different timings); and real-world inconsistency in whether photos alone can diagnose canker (not a rule to resolve, just a reason to always state uncertainty).
