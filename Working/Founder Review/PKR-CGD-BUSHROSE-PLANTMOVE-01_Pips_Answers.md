## 1. What gardeners will see

"When can I plant or move a rose?" is in the list of questions and today says Pip is still learning. The other five questions open from its answer, under "More about planting and moving".

Each statement is shown with the label in brackets. A list of the sources sits under each answer for a gardener who wants it. Statements marked for a country are shown only to gardeners whose rose is there.

### When can I plant or move a rose?

- Plant a bare-root rose (one sold without soil on its roots) while it is dormant, with no leaves, between late autumn and early spring. One source says to avoid the middle of winter when the ground is frozen. *[High confidence]*
- In New Zealand, winter is the best time to plant a rose, and roses can be planted on through spring. One garden centre says new-season roses reach the shops from June. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners in New Zealand only.**
- In Australia, plant bare-root roses in winter, while they are dormant. Two rose societies say June or July, and one source says from May. Months are only a guide. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners in Australia only.**
- In the UK, plant bare-root roses in late autumn, or from late winter to early spring before growth starts. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners in the United Kingdom only.**
- In the US, where winters are very cold, spring is the preferred time to plant bare-root roses. One source puts the line at winters that fall below -10 °F, and says that where it stays warmer than that, autumn or spring both work. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners in the United States only.**
- A rose in a pot can be planted over a longer season than a bare-root rose. UK and New Zealand sources say all year, and two US sources say through the growing season. *[High confidence]*
  - *Shown under it in smaller type:* One source says not to plant into frozen or very dry ground. Another says to keep it well watered if you plant in spring or summer. One New Zealand source says autumn or winter is still the best time.
- Move an established rose while it is dormant, which means it is not actively growing. Most sources say winter. Some say late autumn or early spring. *[High confidence]*
- In New Zealand, one garden centre says the best time to move a rose is after pruning in July, while it is dormant, and once the risk of frost has passed. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in New Zealand only.**
- A rose can be moved in the growing season if you have to. It will need extra watering afterwards. *[Moderate confidence]*

### Where should I plant a rose?

- Choose a sunny spot. Sources give a minimum of four to six hours of direct sun a day. *[High confidence]*
- Roses need soil that drains well. Where the soil is heavy or drains poorly, some sources suggest a raised bed or mound, and others suggest digging in gypsum or compost. *[High confidence]*
- Keep roses away from the roots of nearby trees and shrubs, which compete with them. *[Moderate confidence]*
- How far apart to plant depends on the type of rose and how big it will grow, so check the label. Where sources give a distance, it is about 50 cm to 1 m (20 inches to 3 feet). One gives 75 to 90 cm (2½ to 3 feet) for hybrid tea roses. *[Moderate confidence]*
- A new rose planted where a rose grew before may struggle to establish and may grow poorly. One source suggests choosing a spot where roses haven't grown. *[Moderate confidence]*

### How do I plant a rose?

- Plant a bare-root rose (one sold without soil on its roots) soon after you get it, and don't let its roots dry out. *[Moderate confidence]*
- If you can't plant a bare-root rose straight away, keep it in its packaging somewhere cool and safe from frost. One source allows up to two weeks, with the roots kept moist, and another up to three weeks. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Soak the roots of a bare-root rose in water before planting. *[High confidence]*
- Soak a bare-root rose's roots for anywhere from one hour to 24 hours before planting. Sources differ within that range. Two say not to soak for longer than 24 hours, and one of them says it may damage the roots. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Trim off any damaged or broken roots before planting. One source says not to cut off roots that are too large for the hole, but to dig a bigger hole. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Dig the planting hole wider than the roots. Several sources say about twice as wide. *[Moderate confidence]*
- For a bare-root rose, make a mound of soil in the bottom of the hole and spread the roots over it. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Plant a potted rose at the same depth it was growing in its pot. Advice on where the bud union (the knobbly join near the base where the rose was grafted) should sit differs, so check that too. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Water the rose in well as soon as it is planted. *[High confidence]*
- Experts disagree on whether to prune a bare-root rose at planting. Some cut the stems back when they plant. One source says a packaged rose has already been pruned and rarely needs more, and another prunes in the first winter after planting. *[Sources disagree]*

### How deep should I plant the bud union?

- Experts disagree on how deep to plant the bud union (the knobbly join near the base where the rose was grafted). Advice ranges from up to 5 cm (2 inches) above the soil, to level with it, to as much as 10 cm (4 inches) below. Sources that give a reason for burying it are speaking of places with cold winters, and say it protects the rose from the cold. Two sources give reasons against burying it: a reported risk of dieback, and, in mild climates, disease at the union. *[Sources disagree]*
- In New Zealand and Australia, plant the bud union level with the soil or up to 5 cm above it. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners in New Zealand and Australia only.**
- In the UK, sources differ on the bud union. One says to plant it at soil level, not below, because burying it is reported to raise the risk of dieback. A UK rose nursery sets it about 5 cm below the soil when replanting a rose. *[Sources disagree]* **Gardeners in the United Kingdom only.**
- In the US, extension sources in places with cold winters plant the bud union below the soil, from slightly below to 4 inches (about 10 cm) down. In one mild area, one source plants it an inch above. Ask your local extension service what is done where you live. *[Moderate confidence]* **Gardeners in the United States only.**
- I couldn't find advice on how deep to plant the bud union in New Zealand's colder districts. A local rose society is the place to ask. *[No source found]* **Gardeners in New Zealand only.**
- The advice I found on how deep to plant the bud union comes from New Zealand, Australia, the UK and the US. If your rose is somewhere else, a local rose society is the place to ask. *[No source found]*

### Can I plant a rose where another rose grew?

- A new rose planted where a rose grew before may struggle to establish and may grow poorly. This is called replant disease. *[Moderate confidence]*
- If you plant in the same spot, swap the soil in the planting hole for fresh soil from another part of the garden. Sources differ on how much to take out: from two buckets of soil, along with the old rose's roots, to a hole 45 to 60 cm (18 to 24 inches) across and 30 to 45 cm (12 to 18 inches) deep. One source gives planting somewhere else as another option. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Experts disagree about replant disease. Several advise replacing the soil. One rose specialist calls that the old method and instead adds compost or well-rotted manure and treats the new rose with mycorrhizal fungi. That source reports that opinion is divided on whether the problem is real. *[Sources disagree]*
- I couldn't find a comparison of which remedy for replant disease works best in a home garden. A local rose society is the place to ask. *[No source found]*

### How do I move an established rose?

- Move an established rose while it is dormant, which means it is not actively growing. Most sources say winter. Some say late autumn or early spring. *[High confidence]*
- In New Zealand, one garden centre says the best time to move a rose is after pruning in July, while it is dormant, and once the risk of frost has passed. Months are only a guide, because frosts vary from year to year. *[Low confidence]* **Gardeners in New Zealand only.**
- Cut the top growth back before a winter move. Sources differ on how much: from cutting it back by a third, to cutting it down to 25 to 30 cm (10 to 12 inches), to cutting it right back to 3 to 5 buds above the base. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Dig wide around the rose and keep as many of the roots as you can. Sources give different distances, from about 25 cm (10 inches) out from the base to as much as 60 cm (24 inches), and one says the larger the better. *[Moderate confidence]*
- Sources differ on the soil around the roots. For a winter move, one shakes the soil off and replants the rose like a bare-root one, and one says to keep as large a root ball as you can. For a move in the growing season, one says to keep as much soil on the roots as possible. *[Sources disagree]*
- One source says it is very important to keep the roots moist until the rose is replanted. Another waters the soil well the day before the move. *[Precaution · Low confidence]*
- Water the rose well once it is replanted, and keep it watered until it recovers. Sources differ on how long: from every day for a week or two, to watering in dry spells through the first year. *[High confidence]*
- A rose can be moved in the growing season if you have to. It will need extra watering afterwards. Sources disagree on whether to cut it back first. *[Moderate confidence]*
- One source says a rose that has been settled for many years becomes tricky to move. Another lists roses among plants that dislike having their roots disturbed. *[Precaution · Low confidence]*
- I couldn't find how often a moved rose survives, or an age beyond which a rose is too old to move. A local rose society is the place to ask. *[No source found]*
