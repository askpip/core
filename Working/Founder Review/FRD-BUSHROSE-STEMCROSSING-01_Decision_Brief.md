# Decision Brief — Crossing or Rubbing Stems on an Established, Dormant Bush Rose

This is the plain-language companion to `FRD-BUSHROSE-STEMCROSSING-01`. It's one of two dossiers split out of what was originally a single combined document — see §0 below for why.

## 1. What you are being asked to do

Read this Brief, then the Review Form asks for one thing: **approve the eight findings below as `ARC-BUSHROSE-STEMCROSSING-01`, approve with amendments, or decline.** Everything the research could close, it closed; everything it couldn't (a real, permanent gap rather than a research shortfall) has a stated ROC-recommended default instead of being left for you to rule on.

## 0. Why this is one of two dossiers, not one

This dossier and its companion, covering inward-growing stems (`FRD-BUSHROSE-INWARDGROWTH-01`), were researched together under a single commission, because the working expectation going in was that an inward-growing stem would often turn out to be one that's also crossing another. That expectation was tested directly rather than assumed — and it didn't hold up: no source anywhere states how often the two co-occur, and only real observation over time could ever settle it. Since the premise for joining them didn't hold, and since the app's plant-care design always treated these as two separate things a gardener would spot, the research is presented as two separate dossiers. No new research was needed — this is a reorganisation of findings already gathered, not a redo.

## 2. The short version

The core question — what to do once a crossing or rubbing stem is confirmed — came back with the strongest evidence found anywhere in this research: cut it, as routine dormant-season work, full removal back to its point of origin. Recognition and photo-assessment questions came back thinner, and two questions (what a rubbing wound actually looks like; safety specific to reaching into tangled growth) turned out to be close to complete gaps in the literature, confirmed by real searching rather than a failure to look. Every gap gets a stated default in §9 of the FRD, so none of them blocks a decision.

## 3. What was researched

Q1: how sources define crossing and rubbing stems, and how they relate to inward growth. Q2: visible indicators. Q3: what a photograph can and can't establish. Q4: what a beginner might confuse them for. Q5: what to do once confirmed, and which of two crossing stems to remove. Q6: how a stem with more than one condition is handled, and when to wait. Q7: safety specific to this task.

## 4. How to read the strength ratings

Same three levels used throughout this project: **High** (many independent sources, no serious contradiction), **Moderate** (fewer sources, or some contradiction), **Low** (a handful of sources, thin evidence, or extrapolated from outside rose-specific literature).

## 5. What the research found

**Definitions and relationship (Q1).** Most sources treat crossing and rubbing as a linked pair — often the same instruction, same reasoning: contact damages cane tissue, which invites disease (AF-1, AF-2, both Moderate). One source treats crossing canes as acceptable specifically on tree-form (standard) roses, outside this dossier's scope (AF-3, Low).

**Visible indicators and photographic limits (Q2, Q3).** No source anywhere describes what an actual rubbing wound looks like — a genuine gap. No rose source addresses photographic limits at all; drawing on general professional pruning practice and one academic study, the ROC's own synthesis is that a single photo has real limits for this kind of structural judgment (AF-4, Low, clearly labelled as extrapolated, not rose-specific — this same finding also appears in the companion dossier, since it applies equally to both observations).

**Pruning choices (Q5).** Near-unanimous, and the strongest single finding across either dossier: once confirmed, cut it, as routine work, back to its point of origin (AF-5, High; AF-6, Moderate on the cut type). Where any source gives a rule for choosing between two crossing canes, it favours removing the weaker and keeping the stronger (AF-7, Low, but it corroborates a similar rule already on record in the pruning-framework dossier).

**Multiple conditions and deferral (Q6).** No source says which condition wins when a stem is both inward-growing and crossing — but this doesn't matter in practice, since every source that addresses either one recommends the same action. Deferral guidance that exists is about general timing, not this observation specifically (AF-8, Moderate).

**Lookalikes (Q4) and task-specific safety (Q7).** Both close to complete gaps, confirmed by real searching. Given a plain default each rather than left open.

## 6. Where the sources differ

**C-1 — Should the centre always be opened up?** Almost everyone says yes, for air and light. One source (Rose Society of South Australia, in one of its own two published guides) argues the opposite — that a healthy plant fills its own gaps and interior canes give useful shade. Recorded as a genuine disagreement. **Default:** follow the majority open-centre guidance.

**C-2 — Which of two crossing canes to remove?** Thin evidence either way (two sources with any rule at all, thirteen with none). **Default:** favour the weaker/thinner cane.

**C-3 — Standard roses.** Not a live conflict for bush roses — a documented scope boundary only, since standard (tree-form) roses are already out of scope.

## 7. What's genuinely left open, and what's been given a default instead

Nothing is left open without a stated default. The six questions the research couldn't answer (what a rubbing wound looks like; lookalikes; priority between conditions on one stem; whether professional help is ever specifically warranted; whether a second photo would help; task-specific safety) each get a plain, clearly-labelled ROC recommendation in FRD §9, rather than being handed to you as decisions. You're free to override any of them.
