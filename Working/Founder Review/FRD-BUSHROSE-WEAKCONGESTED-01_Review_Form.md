## The findings

Summarised here; full wording is in FRD §7–8. You're not required to rule on each one — use the comment box if something specific needs to change.

**What they are, and how they relate (Q1).** Weak growth is about one cane; congestion is about the whole bush. Kept distinct throughout. Congestion may actually cause weak growth (poor light at the base) — useful context for why these are one bundled observation.

**Recognising them (Q2).** "Pencil thickness" is the standard shorthand for weak, though the two sources that quantify it disagree by nearly double. Congestion's target cane count genuinely varies by region — one to four (South Africa) up to four to eight (US), with three to five most common.

**Photos and lookalikes (Q3, Q4).** No direct source on photo-based thickness/density judgment, so the ROC drew on general guidance: a size reference in the photo helps. No source addresses mistaking a healthy new cane for a weak old one — the best available clue is thorn/bark colour by age, not thickness.

**What to do about it (Q5).** Clean and consistent: both weak canes and excess congested canes are removed entirely at the base, never trimmed — matching the rule for crossing/rubbing and inward-growing stems, not damaged growth's more nuanced rule.

**Multiple conditions and young plants (Q6).** No explicit rule for a weak-and-congested cane, but sources consistently deal with weak growth before finalising the congestion decision. Young roses get leniency, though sources disagree on how long (one season to three years).

**Safety (Q7).** Eye protection and gauntlet gloves are specifically recommended for reaching into dense growth. Over-thinning in hot climates risks sunscald to the graft — leave some interior growth rather than clearing it all at once.

## Where the sources differ, and the defaults adopted

Three points of variation are on record (Brief §6): the exact weak-cane threshold (pencil thickness agreed in concept, disputed in millimetres); the target cane count for congestion (a real range by region); and how cautious to be about heavy thinning (conditioned on the plant's own vigour, not a universal limit).
