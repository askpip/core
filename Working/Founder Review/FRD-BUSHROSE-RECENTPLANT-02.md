# Founder Review Dossier — Recently Planted Bush Roses: Pruning Restraint and Age-When-Unknown (Revision 2)

## What Changed Since FRD-BUSHROSE-RECENTPLANT-01, and Why

FRD-BUSHROSE-RECENTPLANT-01 concluded, on Q2, that no evidence-based method exists for estimating a bush rose's age when the planting date is unknown. That conclusion was correct for the question actually researched — precise age determination, the same kind of question as "how old is this tree" — but on Founder review, that framing was identified as too narrow for what the Suitability Gate actually needs. The gate does not need to know how many years old a plant is; it needs to know whether the plant *looks* established enough to safely receive the MVP's pruning guidance. That is a visual, comparative, proportionate question, not a precise-dating one — closer in kind to the dead-vs-living-wood observation this project has already built than to dendrochronology.

This revision reruns Q2 under that corrected framing: **what observable characteristics, if any, distinguish an established bush rose from a recently planted one, well enough for a beginner gardener to assess from the plant in front of them (and photographs of it)?**

Everything in FRD-BUSHROSE-RECENTPLANT-01 concerning Q1 (AF-1, and the unresolved RHS-versus-Zimmerman disagreement) is unchanged and is not repeated in full here — see that document. This revision adds a new source log, evidence evaluation and Assessed Finding for the reframed Q2, and revises Founder Decision Point 3 accordingly. FRD-BUSHROSE-RECENTPLANT-01 is preserved as submitted; this document supersedes only its Q2 conclusion, per Founder Review Dossier Standard §9.2.

## Additional Evidence Collected — Source Log (Q2, reframed)

8. **Spring Hill Nursery**, "How Roses are Sized and Graded," https://springhillnursery.com/pages/about_rose_grading — commercial nursery grading standard. A standard Grade #1 bare-root rose — the typical grade sold to home gardeners — has "an average of three flower-ready canes that will grow in the first season." "Jumbo" grade roses (larger, more mature stock) have six to nine canes and are described as up to three times the size of a Grade #1 plant. This establishes a concrete, sourced baseline: a newly purchased, newly planted rose typically starts with around three canes.
9. **Marin Rose Society** (Nanette Londeree, Master Rosarian), "Before You Buy New Roses," https://www.marinrose.org/care-basics/buying-roses/ — states own-root roses "start smaller and may take a few years to mature," while budded (grafted) roses are "typically larger and bloom in their first year" — meaning the same establishment signals (size, cane count) develop on different timelines depending on how the rose was propagated, a limitation on how confidently any single visual threshold could be applied.
10. **ForeverYard** (Matthew Lewis, "Backyard Makeovers," commercial gardening publication, 2 May 2025), "How Long Do Shrubs Take to Establish?", https://foreveryard.com/how-long-do-shrubs-take-to-establish/ — general shrub guidance, not rose-specific. Lists observable establishment signs: presence of new leaves, shoots or buds; healthy, non-stressed foliage; the plant feeling "firmly anchored in the ground" when gently tested, indicating a developed root system. States most shrubs are "considered fully established after 2-3 years," with minimal top growth in year one (root focus), noticeable growth in year two, and full establishment by year three — independently converging with Fine Gardening's Zimmerman (FRD-01 Source 4) on a roughly similar multi-year timeframe, from an unrelated non-institutional source.

**Not found:** no source, rose-specific or general, was located that validates cane thickness, bark colour, or bark texture as a reliable stand-alone maturity signal. The Chicago Botanic Garden distinction noted in FRD-01 (Source 7) remains a within-plant comparison only, not corroborated elsewhere as a whole-plant indicator.

## Revised Evidence Evaluation (Q2)

**Relevance:** Sources 8–10 are directly relevant to the reframed question — each addresses observable establishment characteristics, not precise dating.

**Source Reliability:** Source 8 is a commercial nursery's stated grading standard — a business fact (what they sell as Grade #1), not horticultural research, but a concrete, verifiable, sourced number rather than an opinion. Source 9 is a named Master Rosarian writing for a rose-society publication — the same tier this project has used previously for the American Rose Society's material. Source 10 is a commercial gardening blog, the lowest-reliability tier used in this dossier, equivalent to Fine Gardening's treatment in FRD-01.

**Consistency:** Sources 9 and 10, independently, both describe a multi-year establishment period rather than a single season — this is now two non-institutional but independent sources converging with each other and directionally with Zimmerman (FRD-01), even though none of the three is a strong individual source. No source directly contradicts another on the reframed Q2 (unlike Q1's RHS/Zimmerman conflict).

**Independence:** Sources 8, 9 and 10 are independent of each other and of the FRD-01 source set.

**Limitations:** This is a thin evidence base — three sources, none an extension or university publication, one of them a commercial grading standard rather than research. Source 9 also flags a real complicating factor: own-root and budded (grafted) roses establish on different timelines, so a single visual threshold ("more than three canes") would not apply evenly across rose types, and this MVP has not yet researched which propagation type is in scope. No source combines "new growth present," "cane count above nursery baseline," and "firm when tested" into one validated composite test — that combination, if used, would itself be a design choice for KIT/Founders to make, not something directly evidenced.

**Uncertainty:** Considerable. These are plausible, individually-sourced signals, not a validated method — the honest distinction the previous version of this dossier drew for Q2 as a whole still applies to *precise age*, and now applies in a softer form here: these signals indicate likely establishment, not confirmed establishment.

**Overall Evaluation:** Low confidence. This is a real improvement on "no evidence-based method identified" — there is now a sourced basis for a rough, honest indicator — but it falls well short of the Moderate confidence given to AF-1, because no source validates these signals in combination, none is rose-specific research, and the own-root/budded distinction (Source 9) means the same signals may not mean the same thing across different roses.

## Revised Principal Findings

**New Assessed Finding:**

- **AF-2:** Several observable signals correlate with a bush rose's degree of establishment: the presence of active new growth; a cane count meaningfully above the roughly three-cane baseline typical of a newly purchased Grade #1 rose; and the base of the plant feeling firmly anchored rather than loose when gently tested. No source validates these in combination as a reliable pass/fail test, and their reliability may vary by propagation type (own-root versus budded/grafted), which this project has not yet researched.

This does not overturn FRD-01's conclusion that no method exists for *precise* age determination — that conclusion stands, for that question. AF-2 answers a different, narrower, more useful question: rough, visual establishment assessment, not age in years.

## Revised Evidence Assessment

### AF-2 — Observable Establishment Signals

**Evidence Confidence Level: Low.**

Three independent sources, none of them an extension or university publication, converging on plausible but unvalidated signals, with a known complicating factor (propagation type) not yet accounted for. Per EAS §5.4, Low confidence means the evidence is limited and should be treated as a helpful pointer rather than something to rely on heavily on its own — an appropriate description of AF-2: useful enough to inform a first-draft gate design, not strong enough to state to a gardener as settled fact.

## Revised Recommendations for Further Research

- **Rose-specific establishment research**, ideally from extension or university sources rather than commercial/rosarian publications, testing whether cane count, cane thickness, or new-growth presence actually predicts safe pruning readiness — this dossier's Source 8 (nursery grading) and Source 9/10 (general establishment signs) were the best available, but neither is a rose-pruning-safety study.
- **Propagation-type dependency** (Source 9): before any cane-count threshold is built into a gate, determine whether this MVP's scope needs to distinguish own-root from budded/grafted roses, since Source 9 suggests the same signal may appear on a different timeline for each.
- Everything recommended in FRD-01 §9 concerning Q1 (the RHS/Zimmerman reconciliation, and ROC's state-based gate-question design idea) still applies unchanged.
