## R-5 — F11's Single-Source Status

**Question.** Can a second, independent institutional source be found for pre-winter watering and pre-winter debris removal, currently resting on one Penn State Extension source?

**F11, as currently stated (FRD §7):** "Watering thoroughly after the first hard frost but before the ground freezes, so the plant enters winter well-hydrated, and clearing fallen leaf debris from around the plant before winter to reduce overwintering fungal spores, were both specifically described as part of winter preparation."

**New source found.**

| Source | Organisation | Type | URL |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Preparing Roses for Winter" | Nebraska Extension, Lancaster County, US | University cooperative extension | lancaster.unl.edu/preparing-roses-winter |

**What it says.** Fetched and quoted directly: "continue to water roses until the ground freezes, even if they are dormant. Soak plants to a depth of 8–12 inches, every 2–3 weeks." On debris: "Begin by removing fallen leaves and other debris from around each plant. Removing all of the old leaves is very important, especially if disease problems occurred during the summer. Leaves serve as a good source of overwintering fungal spores that can re-infect your plants next year."

**Evaluation (Evidence Assessment Standard factors).**
- *Relevance:* direct — both halves of F11 (pre-winter watering, debris removal) are addressed specifically, not incidentally.
- *Source reliability:* a university cooperative extension service, the same class of source as nine of Round 1's eleven sources, including Penn State itself.
- *Independence:* Nebraska Extension is a wholly separate institution from Penn State Extension, in a different state, with its own authorship — genuine independent corroboration, not syndicated material.
- *Consistency:* the substance matches closely, including the specific "fungal spores" reasoning for debris removal — the two sources aren't just agreeing on the practice, they're giving the same underlying reason for it.
- *Limitations:* the Nebraska source gives a specific watering depth and interval (8–12 inches, every 2–3 weeks) that Penn State's original wording (as captured in F11) did not specify to the same precision; this is an enhancement to the finding, not a conflict.

**Outcome.** F11 is corroborated by a second independent institutional source. Recommend raising its Evidence Confidence Level from Low to Moderate at minimum (two independent institutional sources, consistent in substance and reasoning) when this is folded into a revised dossier, and considering whether to fold in the more specific watering depth/interval detail.

## R-1 — Consistency with Already-Approved Findings

**Question.** Do F7 and the general watering/care pattern in F2–F6 sit consistently alongside the already-approved RECENTPLANT and DORMANCY findings?

**Method.** No new external sourcing — this is a direct cross-check against the two ARCs' own text, per the commission's scope.

**What RECENTPLANT (AF-1, AF-2) says.** A bush rose's *full pruning treatment* should generally wait until the rose is established, "placed by the evidence at roughly three or more years after planting"; dead-wood removal specifically may proceed sooner. Where planting date is unknown, active new growth, an above-baseline cane count, and base firmness are offered as a rough establishment indicator — specifically for pruning readiness.

**What DORMANCY says.** Nothing overlapping — DORMANCY concerns the biological signal (bud swell) for identifying the correct pruning *window* within a season, and complications to that signal in mild/warm-winter climates. It does not address winter protection actions (mounding, collars, watering, debris — BASICCARE's F9–F11) at all. There is no overlap to check here, contrary to what a first read of the Decision Brief's R-1 item might suggest.

**What BASICCARE (F2, F7) says.** F2: established roses are watered deeply and infrequently, "with closer attention needed in roughly the first two years after planting." F7: a rose that is too recently planted, not yet dormant, or otherwise not ready for pruning still needs ordinary care.

**Evaluation.** No factual conflict was found. But a real distinction is worth stating plainly, because it would be easy for a gardener-facing surface to blur it: RECENTPLANT's "three or more years" is an answer to *when is this rose ready for full pruning*; BASICCARE's "roughly two years" is an answer to *how long does this rose need closer-than-usual watering attention*. They are two different questions about two different aspects of care, not two different answers to the same question — but both are phrased as "how long is my rose considered new," which is exactly the kind of ambiguity a gardener could reasonably read past.

**Outcome.** No new evidence needed and none gathered. Recommend the eventual ARC (or whatever content ultimately reaches a gardener) state both timeframes side by side with their distinct purposes made explicit, rather than let either stand alone where a reader might average or conflate them.

## R-4 — The "Bush Rose" Terminology Gap (F1a)

**Question.** Is "bush rose" used as a formal classification term anywhere outside UK/Commonwealth horticultural literature?

**Method.** Targeted searches across US, Australian and South African rose-care content, both general ("bush rose" Australia/South Africa) and site-restricted (`site:.au`).

**What was found.** Every non-UK source surfaced — US extension services (already in Round 1's source log), Australian outlets (ABC Gardening Australia, Better Homes & Gardens Australia, Treloar Roses, Bunnings) — uses "rose bush," "Hybrid Tea," "Floribunda," or simply "roses," descriptively, never "bush rose" as a named classification. No US or Australian source using "bush rose" as a formal term was found.

**Evaluation.** This is additional, consistent-direction evidence for an absence, not a new institutional source establishing a positive claim — most of the Australian sources found are commercial (nurseries, retailers) or lifestyle media rather than the institutional-grade sources Round 1's evidentiary bar prefers, so this search cannot itself become a cited, confidence-rated finding the way R-5's did. What it can do is strengthen the existing F1a observation, which was always framed as an observation about absence, not a factual finding requiring the same sourcing bar.

**Outcome.** F1a's existing text ("this suggests 'bush rose' as a formal classification term may be more UK/Commonwealth-specific than universal") is corroborated by this additional, broader search and can be stated with slightly more confidence in a revised dossier, while remaining what it always was: a usage observation, not an Assessed Finding requiring its own Evidence Confidence Level. This is a live product question for the Founders (already flagged as Decision 5 in the Decision Brief) more than an open research question.

## R-3 — Q5 Depth (Non-Diagnostic Signs of Poor Health)

**Question.** Can further, more targeted sources strengthen F12 and F13, beyond the single University of Maryland source F12 currently rests on?

**Sources reviewed** (fetched or reviewed in search results, not previously in Round 1's source log):

| Source | Organisation | Type |
|---|---|---|
| "Roses: Diseases and Abiotic Disorders" | UC Statewide IPM Program, US | University-affiliated IPM programme |
| "Rose Diseases: Identification and Management" | University of Tennessee Extension, US | University cooperative extension |
| Rose disease/pest fact sheet | University of Arizona Extension, US | University cooperative extension |

**What was found.** All three organise their content by specific disease or disorder (powdery mildew, black spot, rust, named nutrient deficiencies), each with symptoms tied directly to that one cause. None offers a "notice these general signs first, before identifying a specific cause" framing independent of diagnosis — the UC IPM page, checked most closely, moves straight from a named problem to its symptoms, never the reverse.

**Evaluation.** This is a genuine, deliberate absence check, not an inconclusive search. The original FRD's own §5 (Limitations) already recorded that "most published material on rose health is diagnostic by nature" — this round's additional sources test that claim directly, by sampling three more substantial institutional sources specifically for the missing framing, and confirm it rather than turning up a counter-example.

**Outcome.** No basis found to raise F12 or F13's confidence, and no basis to lower it either. Recommend recording this search explicitly in a revised dossier as a "searched, not found" absence (per this commission's own Expected Deliverables, §6 item 3) — it is itself useful information: it tells the Founders that F12/F13's Moderate and Low–Moderate ratings reflect a genuine, checked evidence gap in the wider literature, not a shortfall in how thoroughly Round 1 looked.

## R-2 — Geographic Diversity (Not Resolved)

**Question.** Can recognised, institutional-quality sources be found from a climate tradition or region outside the UK and continental US?

**Method.** Searches for Australian, South African and New Zealand rose-care sources generally, and specifically for government agriculture departments, botanic garden authorities, and national horticultural societies in those regions; a further search for continental European (Netherlands, Germany, France) sources.

**What was found.** Consistently commercial or enthusiast content: Australian nurseries and retailers (Treloar Roses, David Austin Roses Australia, Bunnings), a lifestyle magazine (Better Homes & Gardens Australia, ABC Gardening Australia), and state-level rose societies (Rose Society of Victoria) — specialist enthusiast bodies, not government or university-extension-equivalent institutions. New Zealand results were exclusively commercial (Tui, Yates, Palmers, Oderings, garden centres). One government lead — Western Australia's Botanic Gardens and Parks Authority "Gardening tips" page — returned a 404 (page not found) when fetched directly. Continental European searches in English surfaced mainly commercial nurseries and a UK body (the Royal National Rose Society), not a continental-European institutional equivalent.

**Evaluation.** This is not inconclusive due to insufficient effort within this round — several distinct search angles were tried, across three regions, including government- and institution-targeted queries specifically. It reflects a real pattern: the kind of publicly funded, non-commercial, institutional horticultural body that produced nine of Round 1's eleven sources (US university extension services, RHS) does not appear to have an easily surfaced equivalent, in English, covering ordinary bush rose care, in the regions searched.

**What would actually resolving this require, if pursued further:**
1. Direct outreach to a specific candidate institution (for example, a state department of agriculture or a botanic garden authority) rather than relying on general web search, which may not surface material that exists but isn't well-indexed.
2. Accepting a different evidentiary bar for non-UK/US sources — for example, allowing a well-established specialist rose society (not government/university, but not purely commercial either) to count as an institutional-equivalent source for this specific gap, the way Round 1 made a narrow exception for one trade publication (Fine Gardening) because its claim was independently corroborated elsewhere. This is a Founder decision about acceptable evidentiary standard, not something the ROC can decide on its own (Evidence Assessment Standard, source reliability factor).
3. Searching non-English-language sources with translation, for continental Europe specifically — a materially larger undertaking than any other item in this round.

**Outcome.** Recorded as a genuine, checked limitation, not a finding. This item is not recommended for closure in a revised dossier; it is recommended either for a further, differently scoped research pass (per the options above, requiring a Founder decision on evidentiary standard first) or for the Founders to accept it as a standing, documented limitation of this commission.
