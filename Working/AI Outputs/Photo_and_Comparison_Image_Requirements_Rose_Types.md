# Photo and Comparison-Image Requirements — Rose-Type Recognition

## What this document is

This is production work, not a research finding — it does not carry an Evidence Confidence Level and is not itself part of `FRD-BUSHROSE-ROSETYPE-01` or `FRD-BUSHROSE-ROSETYPE-LABELS-01`. It answers the "comparison-image needs" question those two dossiers left open (`FRD-BUSHROSE-ROSETYPE-01` §7: "That brief is production work drawn from approved findings and is not a finding of this commission"). Every requirement below is drawn directly from an Assessed Finding in one of those two dossiers, cited by ID, so a reviewer can check this document against the evidence it's built from. Nothing here has been approved as an ARC; it should be read alongside, not instead of, the findings it draws on.

Eleven rose types are covered: the three provisionally approved as in-scope (Hybrid Tea, Floribunda, Grandiflora) and the eight excluded lookalike types researched as comparisons (Shrub, Patio, Miniature, Miniflora, Groundcover, Climbing/climbing sports, Standard, Old Garden/species).

## How to read each entry

- **Must show** — the specific, evidence-grounded feature(s) a photo needs to capture for that type to be recognisable at all.
- **Framing/context** — camera angle, distance, or included reference object needed to make the "must show" feature legible.
- **Known limits** — what the evidence says a photo alone cannot settle for this type, so nobody treats a comparison image as resolving a question the research left open.

---

### Hybrid Tea (in scope)

**Must show:** flowers borne singly or in very small numbers at the top of each stem, not in dense clusters (AF-1, `ROSETYPE-01`); an upright bush habit, medium-tall relative to Floribunda (AF-2, AF-3).
**Framing/context:** a whole-bush shot showing several stems and their flower spacing, plus one closer shot of a single stem's flower arrangement.
**Known limits:** height alone will not reliably separate Hybrid Tea from Floribunda — the two ranges overlap and no source gives a threshold (AF-3, `ROSETYPE-01`). A photo of a dormant, leafless plant will not show the flower-arrangement feature this type depends on (AF-1's "later use" is flagged Q/U/I precisely because of this).

### Floribunda (in scope)

**Must show:** flowers borne in dense clusters at the end of each stem (AF-1, `ROSETYPE-01`); a compact, bushy habit, generally similar-to-shorter than Hybrid Tea (AF-2, AF-3).
**Framing/context:** a whole-bush shot capturing several clusters at once, so the density of blooms per stem-end is visible against the surrounding foliage.
**Known limits:** same height-overlap caveat as Hybrid Tea (AF-3). One source (OSU) describes Floribunda as the larger plant, contradicting the more common "Hybrid Tea taller" pattern — height should not be used as a distinguishing photo cue at all.

### Grandiflora (in scope, provisional — see Founder Decision Point 4, both FRDs)

**Must show:** Hybrid-Tea-shaped individual flowers, but borne in small clusters rather than singly (AF-1, `ROSETYPE-01`); the tallest habit of the three included classes (AF-2, `ROSETYPE-01`).
**Framing/context:** a whole-bush shot wide enough to show the plant's height relative to a fence, person-height marker, or neighbouring plant, plus a close shot of the clustered-but-Hybrid-Tea-form flowers.
**Known limits:** Grandiflora is a first-class, commonly recognised US retail category but is essentially absent from New Zealand and Australian retail and inconsistent in the UK (AF-27, `ROSETYPE-LABELS-01`) — a comparison image drawn from a US source may show a plant type a New Zealand or UK gardener would rarely encounter under this name. The pruning-response evidence for this class is Low confidence (AF-6, `ROSETYPE-01`); a photo cannot substitute for that still-unresolved question.

### Shrub (excluded)

**Must show:** a larger, open, spreading or arching form, generally wider and looser than an upright bush (AF-7, `ROSETYPE-01`).
**Framing/context:** a wide shot capturing the whole plant's width and arching cane shape, ideally alongside an upright Hybrid Tea/Floribunda bush for direct size-and-shape contrast.
**Known limits:** the shrub class is explicitly described as heterogeneous — a "catch all" for roses that don't fit elsewhere (AF-8, `ROSETYPE-01`) — and this commission found the retail label "Rose Bush" can appear on stock a retailer's own category places in Shrub (AF-5, `ROSETYPE-LABELS-01`). No single comparison photo can represent the whole class; at minimum, a compact shrub cultivar and a large sprawling one should both be shown so a gardener isn't misled by one example.

### Patio (excluded)

**Must show:** a small, container-scale bush, roughly 30–75 cm (AF-9, `ROSETYPE-01`).
**Framing/context:** shown in or near a pot, with a full-size garden bed or an upright bush visible in the same or an adjacent frame for scale contrast — scale is the entire distinguishing feature here.
**Known limits:** sources disagree on what a patio rose is nearest to in form — closer to miniatures (RHS), a "down-sized floribunda" (one NZ nursery), or "closer to dwarf floribundas" (a UK nursery) (AF-10, `ROSETYPE-01`) — so a patio-rose photo should not be captioned as resembling one class more than another. This commission also found patio roses sometimes sit inside a "Bush Roses" retail bucket rather than their own category (AF-1, `ROSETYPE-LABELS-01`, Oderings' separate "Patio Bush"), so a caption should note the type by name, not rely on "bush" wording alone.

### Miniature (excluded)

**Must show:** very small overall size, stated upper heights around 50–75 cm across sources, with several giving 20–50 cm or less (AF-11, `ROSETYPE-01`).
**Framing/context:** a scale reference object (a trowel, a ruler, a standard plant pot of known size) in the same frame — without one, small stature alone is hard to judge from a photo.
**Known limits:** several sources combine miniatures with minifloras under the same figures (AF-11's own limitation), so a miniature photo and a miniflora photo should be presented together, not evaluated in isolation.

### Miniflora (excluded)

**Must show:** bloom and foliage size distinctly between miniature and floribunda (AF-12, `ROSETYPE-01`) — larger than a miniature's, smaller than a floribunda's.
**Framing/context:** ideally a three-way comparison frame or caption alongside a miniature and a floribunda photo, since this type is defined entirely by being "in between" rather than by any feature of its own.
**Known limits:** this distinction rests on bloom and foliage size, which is not observable on a dormant, leafless plant (AF-12's own "later use" flag is U for exactly this reason) — a winter photo of this type will not be useful for recognition regardless of framing.

### Groundcover (excluded)

**Must show:** a low, wide-spreading habit, generally under 50 cm tall but spreading sideways over a metre, forming a dense mat or mound (AF-13, `ROSETYPE-01`; AF-11, `ROSETYPE-LABELS-01`, Tesselaar's "low, dense and compact bush").
**Framing/context:** an elevated or angled shot that captures the plant's width, not just a straight-on view that could be mistaken for a low bush.
**Known limits:** the appearance evidence for this type rests substantially on one marketer's description (AF-13's own limitation) — a photo drawn only from that source's marketing material should be flagged as such, and this commission separately found the word "bush" is sometimes applied to groundcover-type stock at general retailers (AF-3, `ROSETYPE-LABELS-01`, Bunnings NZ), so a groundcover comparison photo is exactly the kind of image needed to counter that mislabelling.

### Climbing (including climbing sports) (excluded)

**Must show:** long canes (roughly 2–3 m, sources describe 6–10 ft for climbing sports specifically) trained on or reaching toward a support, with flowers on shorter lateral side-shoots off the main canes, not spread evenly over a rounded bush (AF-14, `ROSETYPE-01`).
**Framing/context:** a shot including the support structure (trellis, arch, fence) so the trained, vertical growth habit is unambiguous, plus a closer shot showing flowers on the shorter laterals rather than the main canes themselves.
**Known limits:** no source addresses whether a climbing sport of an in-scope cultivar ever reverts to bush form (AF-14's own limitation, both dossiers) — a photo cannot settle that question, and captions should not imply it can.

### Standard (excluded)

**Must show:** a single tall, bare, woody stem topped with a grafted, rounded crown of foliage and flowers — the "lollipop" silhouette (AF-15, `ROSETYPE-01`).
**Framing/context:** a full-height shot including the bare stem from ground level to crown, since the bare stem is the entire distinguishing feature.
**Known limits:** none of the sources address how an older standard with regrowth below the graft point should be photographed or recognised (AF-15's own remaining uncertainty in `ROSETYPE-01` §8) — a photo of a young, clean standard should not be presented as representative of all standards a gardener might actually own.

### Old Garden / species (excluded)

**Must show:** this type is defined by history, lineage and flowering habit (often once-blooming) rather than by any single visual plant-form feature (AF-16, `ROSETYPE-01`) — a photo should show an informal, historic-variety flower form (commonly many-petalled, cupped, or single-petalled with 4–8 petals) rather than attempt to show a "typical shape," since none is established.
**Framing/context:** a caption naming the specific historic class shown (Alba, Bourbon, Damask, Gallica, etc., per the NZ Rose Society's own type list, AF-6 `ROSETYPE-LABELS-01`) is more useful than the photo alone, since the category is defined by lineage a photo cannot show.
**Known limits:** this is the most evidence-thin category in either dossier for visual recognition — no source gives a plant-form rule, only historical/flowering-season facts (AF-16's own "later use" is Q/U for this reason). A single representative photo risks implying a visual consistency the evidence does not support.

## Cross-cutting limitations that apply to every type

1. **Dormancy.** No source in either dossier addresses what any of these eleven types look like as a leafless, dormant winter plant — every "must show" feature above depends on flowers, foliage, or a trained support structure being visible. A photo requirement built from this document should assume it applies to an actively growing or flowering plant, and a separate Founder decision is needed on what (if anything) Pip does with a dormant-season photo, consistent with Recommendation R-1 in `FRD-BUSHROSE-ROSETYPE-01`.
2. **"Bush" alone is not a photo cue.** Because the word "bush" is used inconsistently at retail in every market examined (AF-26, `ROSETYPE-LABELS-01`), no comparison photo should be labelled merely "a bush rose" — every image in this set is labelled by its specific type, and any real-world photo a gardener submits that's only labelled "bush rose" on its own tag should not be treated as self-identifying.
3. **These are illustrative, not verified reference photographs.** Sample AI-generated images accompanying this document (see below) are for illustrating the features described above, not for training or verifying an identification system, and have not been checked against a real specimen or an expert rosarian — consistent with the caution `FRD-BUSHROSE-ROSETYPE-01` AF-20 and AF-22 already raised about identification difficulty and photo-based accuracy generally.
